# Feeling Blue at the End of Life: Trajectories of Depressive Symptoms From a Distance-to-Death Perspective

Mona Diegelmann, Oliver K. Schilling, and Hans-Werner Wahl Ruprecht-Karls-Universität Heidelberg

This article examines the end-of-life development of depressive symptoms and characterizes prototypical groups following the same depressive symptoms development. We modeled time-to-death-related trajectories of depressive symptoms (Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale), applying a latent class growth analysis to deceased older adults from the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (Waves 1 to 5;  $N_{\rm Time~1~(TI)} = 2,219$ ;  $M_{\rm Age(TI)} = 73.9$  years,  $SD_{\rm Age(TI)} = 9.4$  years; 51% male, 1% non-White). Four prototypical trajectories of depressive symptoms were identified at the end of life: a stably nondepressed group (31.2%); 2 groups with an exponential terminal symptom increase, of which 1 was nondepressed and 1 low depressed (8.3% and 38.4%, respectively); and a stably depressed group (22.2%). Using a combination of growth curve models and individual level and slope values as predictor variables showed that individuals suffering from increasing sensory, mobility, or overall health problems or decreasing quality of life were more likely to have an increase in symptoms of depression in their last years of life. Men were more likely to be stably nondepressed and women more likely to be chronically depressed. We conclude that a group-based analysis of end-of-life depressive symptoms is useful in adding to the understanding of distance-to-death psychology at large as well as pointing to preventive and intervention strategies when it comes to late-life depressive mood.

Keywords: CES-D, depressive symptoms, English Longitudinal Study of Aging, latent class growth analysis, time-to-death

A sizable fraction—8% to 16%, depending on sample characteristics and assessment methods—of community-dwelling older adults suffer from clinically relevant depressive symptoms (Blazer, 2003), with depression rates rising in advanced old age, that is, close to death (Luppa et al., 2012). Thus, more research is needed to better understand developmental trajectories of depressive symptoms, the possible occurrence of important subgroups, and corresponding risk factors. In this article, we take a time-to-death (TTD) data-analytic view to contribute to this scientifically, societally, and ethically relevant issue.

# The Distance-to-Death Perspective on Late-Life Development of Depressive Symptoms

Previous research frequently addressed late-life development of depressive symptoms regarding age-graded normative changes (Blazer, 2003; Luppa et al., 2012). In recent years, however, a growing body of research suggested that intraindividual psychological changes in late life are better predicted by TTD than distance-from-birth. Inspired by substantial evidence of terminal changes in cognitive functioning (Bäckman & MacDonald, 2006), evidence of TTD-related dynamics in late life has been provided, for example, in sensory, health-related, and social domains (Gerstorf, Ram, Lindenberger, & Smith, 2013), life satisfaction (Gerstorf, Ram, Estabrook, et al., 2008; Gerstorf et al., 2010; Gerstorf, Ram, Röcke, Lindenberger, & Smith, 2008; Mroczek & Spiro, 2005; Palgi et al., 2010), and positive and negative affect (Palgi et al., 2010; Schilling, Wahl, & Wiegering, 2013; Vogel, Schilling, Wahl, Beekman, & Penninx, 2013; Windsor, Gerstorf, & Luszczs, 2015). Concerning the processes that drive psychological changes in late life, TTD-related changes may reflect the impact of biological and/or functional deteriorations, which occur specifically during a limited time period preceding-and precipitating-death (Bäckman & MacDonald, 2006; Ram, Gerstorf, Fauth, & Zarit, 2010; Schilling, 2016). These terminal deteriorations of the human system may be affected by proximal and distal impacts across the life span. Thus, following a time-left—instead of a time-lived perspective follows the rationale that detrimental impacts across the life span fuel a process that will inevitably end with the individual's death; thus, that the temporal order of detriments matches the TTD metric late in one's life.

Depressive symptoms have been viewed theoretically as composite of a general factor of negative affect and a specific factor of *anhedonia* (i.e., low-positive affect; [Clark & Watson, 1991\)](#page-12-4). People approaching the end of life might increasingly develop and report depressive symptoms because terminal aggravations of their physical functionality disrupt their behavioral means to gain positive affective experiences, and/or because they increasingly experience negative emotional states when they perceive accumulating losses, running out of vitality and future time perspective, and feeling the end come closer. Indeed, such terminal decreases of positive and increases of negative affect have been evidenced in recent studies of TTD-related change in affective outcomes [\(Palgi](#page-13-4) [et al., 2010;](#page-13-4) [Schilling, Wahl, & Wiegering, 2013;](#page-13-5) [Vogel et al.,](#page-14-0) [2013;](#page-14-0) [Windsor et al., 2015\)](#page-14-1). All these studies found a TTD-related increase of negative affect, and some [\(Vogel et al., 2013;](#page-14-0) [Windsor](#page-14-1) [et al., 2015\)](#page-14-1) found TTD-related decline of positive affect. Whereas most of these studies (except [Schilling, Wahl, & Wiegering, 2013\)](#page-13-5) analyzed subscores of positive and negative affect obtained from a depressive symptoms inventory (i.e., the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale; [Radloff, 1977\)](#page-13-8), [Gerstorf et al.](#page-13-1) [\(2010\)](#page-13-1) evidenced a terminal increase in depressive symptoms measured by that same inventory. Moreover, a terminal drop operationalized as a terminally accelerated decrease of affective well-being described end-of-life trajectories in affective domains better than linear-decline models did [\(Gerstorf et al., 2010;](#page-13-1) [Vogel](#page-14-0) [et al., 2013;](#page-14-0) [Windsor et al., 2015\)](#page-14-1). Altogether, these findings suggest that TTD-related processes may be crucial for the late-life development of depressive symptoms.

# **Considering Heterogeneity of Developmental Dynamics in Late-Life Depressive Symptoms**

Going further, it may be questioned how frequently such a terminal increase of depressive symptoms occurs and whether it occurs similarly among those close to death. For instance, death may come slow for many old persons, preceded by accumulating losses of physical function and health that will eventually precipitate death; others die fast, hit by rapid pathological processes or by "external shocks" (e.g., accidents, injuries). Such differences in speed and duration of terminal deteriorations might also shape reactive changes of affective experiences, accordingly generating heterogeneity of terminal changes of depressive symptoms. The present study aimed to add to recent research on a TTD-related increase of depressive symptoms by examining the potential heterogeneity of terminal developmental dynamics in depressive symptoms.

Recent research on intraindividual change in late-life depressive symptoms typically used latent growth curve models [\(Clapp et al.,](#page-12-5) [2013;](#page-12-5) [Huang et al., 2011;](#page-13-9) [Sutin et al., 2013;](#page-14-2) [Wauterickx & Bracke,](#page-14-3) [2005;](#page-14-3) [Yang, 2007\)](#page-14-4). This approach rests upon two important theoretical implications, namely (a) the trajectory function (i.e., the curvature), assumed as proper description of a systematic change process that unfolds within all individual trajectories over time (e.g., linear or quadratic trends), and (b) that individual differences in the unfolding of this systematic process disperse nonsystematically, displaying a continuous random (normal) distribution of the process' level and slope components around one typical developmental trajectory (fixed level and slope effects describing the course of an "average individual" over time). Recently, however, some researchers considered a different approach to interindividual differences in intraindividual changes in depressive symptoms in late life [\(Byers et al., 2012;](#page-12-6) [Hsu, 2012;](#page-13-10) [Kok, Aartsen,](#page-13-11) [Deeg, & Haisman, 2015;](#page-13-11) [Kuchibhatla, Fillenbaum, Hybels, &](#page-13-12) [Blazer, 2012;](#page-13-12) [Schilling, Wahl, & Reidick, 2013\)](#page-13-13). Instead of continuous random variability around the "normative" developmental trend of an average individual, there might be heterogeneity of different developmental dynamics that unfold only in parts of the late-life population, such that the interindividual differences in changes in depressive symptoms could—rather substantially— be attributed to a limited number of prototypical subgroup trajectories. A person is thus not seen as deviating from an overall normative development, but as a member of a particular, homogeneously developing group. A suitable data-analytical approach to identify such latent subgroups in the broader general population is latent class growth analysis (LCGA; [Muthén & Muthén, 2000\)](#page-13-14).

Why should such heterogeneity of developmental dynamics of depressive symptoms be relevant from a TTD perspective? Research on potential triggers of depressive symptoms points at the complexity of the affective processes that generate depressiveness [\(Blazer, 2003\)](#page-12-0). That is, depressive symptoms might develop when accumulations of risk factors and emotionally burdening conditions meet individual vulnerabilities and limited coping resources. Thus, rather than any single cause, the co-occurrence and interplay of extrinsic (e.g., environmental, sociocultural, or health stressors) and intrinsic (e.g., [mal-]adaptive emotional coping styles) conditions promotes patterns of affective responses emerging as depressive symptoms. However, factors that could interact in triggering depressive symptoms may not all occur mutually independent but may build patterns of co-occurrence that provide specific risk constellations in late life. For instance, poor socioeconomic conditions often come with worse health conditions and higher morbidity risks across the life span [\(Präg, Mills, & Wittek, 2016\)](#page-13-15), hence leading to an accumulation of challenges that in old age could overburden individual emotional coping capacities. Poor coping capacities may, in turn, bring about further adversities in terms of stressors and emotionally burdening experiences. Thus, a person with a life history of challenging circumstances might be particularly vulnerable to get depressed by late-life loss experiences, when she or he also has poor personal coping resources and maladaptive response styles. In contrast, a person equipped with a resilient personality might have rather been "trained" and strengthened by the same high load of lifetime adversities. However, both these persons may also differ from one that went through a life largely free of health challenges and major life stressors, facing crucial losses in late life for the first time. Therefore, the late-life development of depressive symptoms might be shaped by different typical risk constellations, including proximal loss experiences that occur frequently at the end of the human life span, but also more distal impacts met earlier in one's life span and personal characteristics needed for emotional resilience.

This reasoning leads to the assumption that late-life changes in depressive symptoms might be characterized by variations of individual developmental trends clustered around prototypical trajectories that signify different risk constellations, rather than by a single developmental trajectory representing some continuously dispersed variability in unfolding this one and only normative trend. Thus, given the mere multitude of potential impacts that could be considered theoretically as crucial facets in such risk constellations, an explorative approach using the LGCA as a tool to search for such prototypical trajectories could add valuable knowledge about late-life development of depressive symptoms. It might be particularly useful to identify latent groups more or less at risk for mental illness, according to their prototypical trajectories over TTD.

# **Predictors of Prototypical Distance-to-Death Trajectories of Depressive Symptoms**

Given that distinct prototypical TTD-related trajectories of depressive symptoms could be identified, we aimed to complete these findings by analyzing associated potential risks and resources. Different predictor constellations associated with different groups may add important facets to the explanation of differential developmental pathways of depressive symptoms at the end of life. Further, predictor variables may be of practical significance, for instance, in providing information on where and how to intervene. Thus, aiming for at least some hints toward risks and resources that might be crucial for particular terminal dynamics in depressive symptoms, we considered the processpersoncontexttime model (PPCT) proposed by [Bronfenbrenner and Morris \(2006\)](#page-12-7) as a guideline. In particular, the PPCT provides an integrative view of process, person, and context variables as well as time dynamics as key components of development.

First, *processes* denote interactions that translate person, context, and time factors into developmental outcomes. Drawing from the basic reasoning of the socioemotional selectivity theory that emotionally gratifying interactions become more important when the time horizon shrinks [\(Carstensen, Fung, & Charles, 2003\)](#page-12-8), the interaction quality with one's partner may be a crucial process indicator particularly relevant in a TTD design. Empirically, emotional strain and support from one's partner or children predicted 2-year changes in depressive symptoms [\(Stafford, McMunn, Za](#page-13-16)[ninotto, & Nazroo, 2011\)](#page-13-16). Accordingly, we expected emotional strain to be associated with "unfavorable" TTD-related trajectories, showing increasing or stably high loads of depressive symptoms, whereas emotional support should be associated with more favorable depressive symptoms trajectories.

Second, the *person* component of the PPCT consists of demand characteristics, which evoke or suppress immediate reactions from the social environment, thereby changing proximal processes; resources and limitations, which aid or limit the effectiveness of proximal processes; and forces, which are motivational dispositions that help initiating and/or sustaining an action. We did not address motivational forces, as these could be considered as less predictive of, but rather as affected by, depressive symptomatology. As basic demand characteristics, which have been found associated with late-life changes in depressive symptoms, we considered sex, age, and ethnicity (e.g., [Kuchibhatla et al., 2012;](#page-13-12) [Luppa et al., 2012;](#page-13-0) [Yang, 2007\)](#page-14-4). These empirical findings suggest that women might have higher probabilities of unfavorable trajectories of depressive symptoms, and higher age-at-death might be predictive of TTD-related rates of depressive symptoms, although findings on ethnicity are somewhat inconsistent.

Concerning resources and their limitations, functional health limitations and psychosocial resources seem to be crucial candidates to consider. Regarding functional health limitations, we focused on self-rated vision and mobility limitations because these are critical for getting along independently and may be accompanied by reduced emotional well-being [\(Heyl, Wahl, & Mollenkopf,](#page-13-17) [2005\)](#page-13-17). Trajectories of late-life depressive symptoms were associated with sensory, mobility, and perceived health limitations [\(Gunn et al., 2013;](#page-13-18) [Hsu, 2012;](#page-13-10) [Huang et al., 2011;](#page-13-9) [Kuchibhatla et](#page-13-12) [al., 2012;](#page-13-12) [Schilling et al., 2013\)](#page-13-13). Accordingly, we expected high levels of functional health limitations to be associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms; and increases of limitations to be associated with trajectories of increasing depressive symptoms. Furthermore, psychosocial resources that deserve consideration as potential impacts on late-life depressive symptoms trajectories are memory functioning, perceived loneliness, and subjective quality of life (QoL).1 Increasing memory limitations may aggravate daily interactions, interfering with one's generation of hedonically rewarding experiences and promoting negative self-evaluations. Feeling lonely may be accompanied by reduced positive reinforcement from social interactions, which may be associated with elevated and increasing levels of depressive symptoms. In contrast, positive perceptions of one's QoL and basic need-fulfillment may serve as a buffer against the development of depressive symptoms when one experiences terminal degradations and feels the end come close. Empirically, trajectories of depressive symptoms were associated with cognitive limitations, loneliness, and eudemonic well-being [\(Cacioppo, Hughes, Waite, Hawkley, & Thisted, 2006;](#page-12-9) [Kuchibhatla et al., 2012;](#page-13-12) [Zaninotto, Falaschetti, & Sacker, 2009\)](#page-14-5); thus, we expected to find similar associations with TTD-related trajectories, in that worse memory function, higher loneliness, and less perceived QoL would be associated with less favorable depressive symptoms trajectories.

Third, regarding the PPCT *context* component, a person's social position, financial means, and neighborhood characteristics may indicate developmental prerequisites (e.g., better education, less sorrows about how to make a living, and easier accession to health services), which may be associated with more or less favorable processes and depressive symptoms. Indeed, perceived socioeconomic disadvantage was empirically associated with late-life developments of depressive symptoms [\(Gunn et al., 2013\)](#page-13-18), whereas the association with the accession to neighborhood amenities was less clear [\(Kim, 2008\)](#page-13-19). Overall, we expected a higher social position, less financial difficulties, and fewer barriers to health services to be associated with favorable TTD-related trajectories of depressive symptoms.

Finally, regarding the PPCT *time* component, the TTD approach itself rescales process, person, and context factors; thus, it changes the interpretation of the time component originally included to explain development across a time-since-birth perspective. #### **Aims and Expectations**

Existing LCGA approaches to late-life changes in depressive symptoms did not analyze TTD-related trajectories (i.e., these either analyzed changes associated with age or with time-in-study; Byers et al., 2012; Clapp et al., 2013; Hsu, 2012; Huang et al., 2011; Kuchibhatla et al., 2012; Schilling, Wahl, & Reidick, 2013; Wauterickx & Bracke, 2005; Yang, 2007), whereas studies of TTD-related change in depressive symptoms (Gerstorf et al., 2010) or depression subscores of positive and negative affect (Palgi et al., 2010; Vogel et al., 2013; Windsor et al., 2015) did not examine heterogeneity in terms of different prototypical trajectories. Therefore, the present study aimed to integrate these two late-life developmental perspectives, examining latent groups of TTD-related trajectories of depressive symptoms. Concluding from the theoretical considerations and empirical evidence outlined above, hypothetical expectations about prototypical TTD-related trajectories could be made as follows.

First, we expected to reveal a group characterized by a trajectory of stably few depressive symptoms, as well as a group showing permanently clinically relevant depressive symptoms. Previous empirical work on depressive symptoms (Deeg, 2005; Schilling, Wahl, & Reidick, 2013) suggests that such stably nondepressed and stably depressive trajectories might unfold over long time periods of the adult life span, not limited to a "pre-terminal" (vs. terminal) period.

Second, we expected to also find groups of individuals that might have grown old without major depressive symptoms, but develop such an increase approaching death: These might show a pattern of accelerated terminal increase in depressive symptoms.

Third, given that such TTD-related increases in depressive symptoms are driven by vulnerabilities due to specific risk factor constellations that are elicited during the terminal phase of life, it also seems possible that more than one such prototypical trajectory of terminally increasing depressiveness exist, differing by speed and duration of the TTD-related changes.

Concerning the predictors of prototypical TTD-trajectories of depressive symptoms, a second set of analyses was conducted to examine whether selected indicators of time-varying and time-invariant indicators of process, person, and context—as explained in detail above—predict the probabilities of belonging to latent end-of-life trajectories of depressive symptoms. Though the PPCT provided a rationale for this selection of hypothetical predictors, these analyses might be understood more as an explorative search for risk factors and resources specifically associated with certain prototypical TTD dynamics in late-life depressiveness, meant to complete the findings of prototypical terminal dynamics revealed with the LCGA approach to terminal changes in depressive symptoms

#### Method

#### Sample

We used data from the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA). ELSA started recruiting 12,099 participants from the

Health Survey for England in 2002 to provide a representative sample of the community-dwelling English population aged 50 years and older, plus their cohabiting partners. The panel continues to draw subjective and objective data regarding domains like physical health, cognition, QoL, and social involvement every two years (for further information on ELSA, see Banks, Nazroo, & Steptoe, 2012). The sample was supplemented at waves three (2006/07) and four (2008/09) to ensure the representativeness of the target population. The present study used data that were gathered on five measurement occasions (Times 1-5 [T<sub>1</sub>-T<sub>5</sub>], 2-year intervals) between 2002 and 2011. The sample comprised 2,327 deceased individuals ( $N_{\text{T1}} = 2,219, M_{\text{Age(T1)}} = 73.9 \text{ years},$  $SD_{Age(T1)} = 9.4 \text{ years}, 51\% \text{ male}, 1\% \text{ non-White}; N_{T2} = 1,345;$  $N_{\rm T3} = 885; N_{\rm T4} = 508; N_{\rm T5} = 147)$  who provided information on depressive symptoms and TTD. For a more detailed sample description, see Table 1.

#### Measures

**TTD.** Mortality information was provided by the Office for National Statistics through February 2012. TTD at each measurement occasion was computed as the difference between age-at-death and age-at-occasion.

**Depressive symptoms.** Participants rated depressive symptoms experienced during the previous week on eight dichotomous items of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES–D), a shortened CES-D version developed for the Health and Retirement Study (Steffick, 2000). Items comprised feeling depressed, lonely, sad, everything being an effort, sleeping restlessly, being happy, enjoying life, and not getting going. Negatively worded items were reverse-coded. CES-D scores were calculated if participants answered at least five of eight items (Chodosh, Miller-Martinez, Aneshensel, Wight, & Karlamangla, 2010; i.e., scale scores were the number of depressive symptoms divided by the number of items answered, multiplied by eight, and rounded to integers so that the score can be compared to other studies and the cut-off score). The internal consistency was fairly good (Cronbach's  $\alpha = .76$  to .80, for measurement occasions).

**Predictors of trajectory group membership.** In general, we used perceived process, person, and context variables, which are known to predict subjective outcomes in old age better than objective variables (e.g., Smith, Fleeson, Geiselmann, Settersten, Jr., & Kunzmann, 2001).

The *emotional support* from reciprocal interactions with their spouse/partner was rated by the participants answering "How much does your partner really understand the way you feel about things?", "How much can you rely on your partner if you have a serious problem?", and "How much can you open up to your partner if you need to talk about your worries?" They also rated the emotional strain, answering "How much does your partner criticize you?", "How much does your partner let you down when you are counting on him/her?", and "How much does your partner get on your nerves?" (Walen & Lachman, 2000) on a four-point scale ranging from 0 (*not at all*) to 3 (*a lot*). If more than 20% of the scale's items were missing, the scale score was set missing (we applied this rule to all following scale scores, too). Participants' sex, age-at-death, and ethnicity were included as demand characteristics in terms of the PPCT model. As age-at-death was not available if a person exceeded age 90 at death, participants' true age-at-death was computed as the sum of  $age_{(T1)}$  (if  $\leq$ 90) and the difference between year-of-death and year-of-interview. Age-at-death was mean-centered for further analyses.

Concerning functional health limitations, participants rated their perceived vision limitation, completing the question "Is your eyesight (using glasses or corrective lens as usual) . . .?" with answer options from 1 (*excellent*) to 5 (*poor*). Likewise they rated their perceived general health limitation, completing the question "Would you say your health is . . .?" with the same answer options. Ten perceived limitations of mobility (e.g., whether participant had difficulty climbing several flights stairs without resting) were

queried (Fonda & Herzog, 2004). Applying the maximal-20%-missing rule (see preceding text), scale values were calculated by multiplying the number of mobility limitations divided by the number of items answered and multiplied by 10—the number of scale items. The internal consistency was fairly good (Cronbach's  $\alpha = .82$  to .85, for measurement occasions).

Concerning psychosocial factors, participants also rated their perceived memory limitation completing the question "How would you rate your memory at the present time. Would you say it is . . .?" with answer options from 1 (excellent) to 5 (poor). Note that perceived memory scores were not available at T<sub>5</sub>. Loneliness was measured by a three-item scale (Hughes, Waite, Hawkley, & Cacioppo, 2004) developed from the R-UCLA Loneliness Scale. Participants rated whether they lacked companionship, felt left out, or felt isolated from others, from 1 (hardly ever or never) to 3 (often). Higher loneliness scores indicated greater perceived social isolation. Internal consistency was fairly good (Cronbach's  $\alpha$  = .81 to .88, for measurement occasions). Note that loneliness scores were not available at T<sub>1</sub>. Subjective QoL was measured using the CASP-12 self-report scale, which was specifically designed to evaluate QoL in early old age (Wiggins et al., 2008). CASP-12 comprises a six-item control-autonomy factor (e.g., "I can do the things I want to do"), a self-realization (e.g., "I feel that life is full of opportunities"), and a pleasure (e.g., "I enjoy the things that I do") factor (three items each). Notably, tests of the latent factor structure strongly supported that CES-D and CASP represent distinctive latent constructs (Vanhoutte, 2014). Item codes ranged from 0 (never) to 3 (often); negatively worded items were reversed. Higher scores indicated more perceived OoL. The internal consistency was fairly good (Cronbach's  $\alpha = .80$  to .84, for measurement occasions).

As indicators of context, participants positioned themselves on a scale ranging from 5 (worst off) to 100 (best off) to indicate their perceived social status. They described how they got along financially on a scale ranging from 1 (manage very well) to 6 (have severe financial difficulties) to indicate their perceived financial difficulties. Finally, they indicated their barriers accessing health services (chiropodist, dentist, general practitioner, hospital, optician). Items were dichotomized to 0 (very or quite easy access) or 1 (very or quite difficult access); the accession barrier score was computed by multiplying the number of barriers reported divided by the number of items answered by five. The internal consistency of the score was fairly good (Cronbach's  $\alpha = .84$  to .91, for measurement occasions). Accession barriers were not available at  $T_3$ .

#### Statistical Analyses

Modeling prototypical distance-to-death trajectories and the terminal increase. A LCGA was run to analyze latent trajectories of depressive symptoms as a function of TTD and to receive associated individual probabilities of being a member of each trajectory group. LCGA may be considered a more parsimonious version of full growth mixture models as it does not estimate group-specific random effects (Muthén & Muthén, 2000). A LCGA was considered sufficient for the current study, as we did not address such random effects.

SAS PROC TRAJ (Nagin, 1999), run on SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., 2013), was used to model trajectories of depressive symptoms. Models for zero-inflated Poisson-distributed (ZIP) out-

comes were run as CES-D scores are counts of depressive symptoms and revealed very high numbers of zeros (30%, 28%, 31%, 27%, 19% for occasions  $T_1$  to  $T_5$ ). Also descriptively, the ZIP-curve pictured the data better than the normal curve (see Figure 1). PROC TRAJ estimates polynomial functions of the predictor (i.e., the time metric used), but cannot fit the change point trajectories which are frequently modeled in TTD analyses (Gerstorf et al., 2010; Vogel et al., 2013). To model terminally accelerated developments, we transformed TTD before the analysis and fitted polynomial ( $X_{it} = TTD_{it}$ ), and inverse exponential ( $X_{it} = e^{-TTD_{it}}$ ) functions. Thus, the respective ZIP model could be denoted by two equations that refer to two zero-generating processes:

$$ln(\lambda_{itc}) = \sum_{p=0}^{P} \beta_{pc} X_{it}^{p}$$
, and

$$\ln\left(\frac{\rho_{it}}{1-\rho_{it}}\right) = \sum_{q=0}^{Q} \alpha_{qc} X_{it}^{q},$$

where  $\lambda_{itc}$  is subject i's expected number of depressive symptoms at occasion t, given membership in the latent trajectory group c. Thus, the first equation refers to a process generating Poissondistributed counts, some of which may be zero. The function's order is P. The second equation refers to a process generating structural zeros (above those expected by the Poisson distribution).  $\rho_{it}$  gives the probability of the respective binary distribution. Notably,  $\rho_{it}$  was assumed to be invariant across groups (i.e., between-groups differences in zero counts were due to the Poisson process). As we were interested in mean trajectories and a potential terminal increase, a quadratic polynomial (i.e., P = 2) or a linear inverse exponential (i.e., P = 1) function appeared sufficient to model the "Poisson part" of the above model. For the polynomial functions, the meaning of the  $\beta$ 's is as in usual polynomial regression functions. For the inverse exponential function,  $\beta_{0c}$  denotes the asymptotic bottom level of depressive symptoms (i.e., the number of symptoms for large TTD values, given  $\rho_{it} = 0$ ) and  $\beta_{1c}$ gives the difference between the bottom level and the expected level of depressive symptoms at year of death (given  $\rho_{it} = 0$ ). Note that this function is a restrictive version of the exponential growth function in that it implies a fixed "steepness" of the curve. That is, "mimicking" an exponential growth function by prior transforma-

tion of  $TTD_{it}$  (to build the polynomial function needed for PROC TRAJ), we could not model parameter  $\beta_{2c}$  of the "full" function  $X_{it} = \beta_{0c} + \beta_{1c} e^{-\beta_{2c}TTD_{it}}$  (e.g., McArdle & Wang, 2008). We expected that the inverse exponential may outperform the polynomial function in modeling the very sharp bend coming from the expected terminally accelerated symptom increase in depressive symptoms.

We ran 3,276 ZIP models for polynomial (c=1-6; p=0-2; zero inflation: none, Q=0, Q=1) and 378 for inverse exponential TTD values (c=1-6; p=0-1; zero inflation: none, Q=0, Q=1). Model fit comparisons were based on the Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC) and a more complex model was preferred if the convergence criterion was met and the Bayes factor approximation  $2(\Delta BIC) \ge 2$  (Jones, Nagin, & Roeder, 2001).

Examining predictors of membership in a latent trajectory **group.** A two-step approach (Schilling, Wahl, & Reidick, 2013) was used to link the membership probability for each terminal trajectory of depressive symptoms to indicators of the selected predictor variables. First, growth curve models were run (using SAS PROC MIXED, SAS Institute Inc., 2013) to estimate TTDrelated individual intercept and linear slope parameters for each time-varying indicator.<sup>2</sup> Second, individual latent level and slope scores revealed from the growth curve models were used as predictors of an individual's probabilities of belonging to a particular trajectory group in a logit model (separate analyses were run for each group using SAS PROC NLIN, SAS Institute Inc., 2013). The procedure does not offer an ordinary fit index, wherefore the squared correlations of PROC TRAJ-estimated and PROC NLIN-predicted group membership probabilities served as an ad hoc  $R^2$  (Schilling, Wahl, & Reidick, 2013). Notably, PROC TRAJ and PROC MIXED employ missing-at-random maximum likelihood methods-thus using all available data-to accommodate longitudinal dropout. As PROC NLIN was run on latent individual scores gained from missing-at-random estimations, it considered all available data as well.

# **Predictors of Distance-to-Death Trajectories of Depressive Symptoms**

To gain a more comprehensive understanding these end-of-life trajectories of depressive symptoms, we used Bronfenbrenner and Morris' PPCT developmental model (2006) as a guideline to integrate effects of process (emotional support and strain from interactions), person (demand characteristics, functional health limitations, and psychosocial resources), and context (barriers to health services, financial deprivation, and social position) variables across TTD. Overall, the unfavorable groups involving many depressive symptoms prior to death (rising-from-few and stably depressed) were characterized by aggravated end-of-life levels of functional health and psychosocial conditions (QoL, loneliness), while the favorable groups with few depressive symptoms in the terminal phase of life (stably nondepressed and soaring-from-zero group) went with altogether beneficial levels of these conditions. This pattern is in line with previous research on risk and protective factors of late-life depression [\(Blazer, 2003;](#page-12-0) [Fiske, Wetherell, &](#page-12-14) [Gatz, 2009\)](#page-12-14). Notably, this overall conclusion applies to levels "reached" in these conditions at the end of life, not taking into account whether these might have been kept stable or had changed (worsened) over a larger period preceding death.

However, we also revealed some counterintuitive slope effects, linking the favorable trajectories with a worsening of health indicators, but stable depressiveness with less worsening (of mobility and QoL), seemingly inconsistent with their respective level effects. To resolve this inconsistency, it must be considered that these slope effects refer to regression coefficients with respective level effects (and all other effects) partialed out. For instance, the slope effects of mobility limitations indicate that individuals, whose limitations increased more than expected by the end-of-life level of limitations they reached, were more prone to a favorable group, than those with less increase than expected by their end-of life level. [Figure 4](#page-10-0) depicts schematically how the slope effect refers to differences between slopes adjusted for the average slope associated with the respective end-of-life level. For instance, Persons C and D share the same slope effect predicting group membership probabilities, but D has a higher probability to belong to a favorable group due to the level effect. However, comparing the trajectories of Persons A, B, and C, which all end at the same level, the slope effect indicates that B (showing the steepest negative slope) is less likely than A and C to belong to the stably depressed and more likely to belong to the nondepressed or the soaring-fromzero. Comparing Persons B and D, B's steeper slope conflicts with D's lower level, but if the level effect is much larger in size than the slope effect (as in most of our results), D is more likely to belong to a favorable group than B (note, however, that also the level effects are controlled for the slopes, which is not illustrated in [Figure 4\)](#page-10-0).

[Figure 4](#page-10-0) also illustrates that the negative level-slope correlations and the finding that people hardly declined in health limitations might represent a ceiling effect: Those already highly limited when "entering" the terminal period (i.e., high limitations at the right hand side of [Figure 4\)](#page-10-0) could not worsen further, whereas those who entered the terminal period with no limitations could unfold a broader range of worsening, including steep increase of limitations. Altogether, this provides an understanding of how the counterintuitive slope effects add to the level effects: In the data analyzed, less increase of health limitations was indicative of long lasting health burden, across and even prior to the terminal phase of life, rather than absence of health limitations until the end of life. Such enduring health burden was associated with being stably depressed, whereas an only terminal worsening of functional health did at most trigger a soaring-from-zero trajectory of depressive symptoms (the only large slope effect was revealed for mobility limitations predicting this group). Therefore, among those who revealed a high end-of-life level of limitations, individuals who already entered the terminal period of life with a high load of functional health limitations have been most susceptible to high depressiveness— compared to those that suffered from functional health losses prior to death, but maintained fairly good health conditions until the beginning of their terminal period of life. This suggests that it may be the "wear and tear" of dealing with health limitations across larger periods in late life that promotes high loads of depressive symptoms at the end of life, whereas maintaining good health as long as possible has a protective effect, mitigating the effects of terminal health losses and enhancing one's probability to avoid increase of depression under impending death.5

Conforming only partly to previous findings [\(Stafford et al.,](#page-13-16) [2011\)](#page-13-16) and our expectations, emotional strain from partner interactions showed some association with TTD-related trajectories of depressive symptoms, whereas emotional support did not. At least medium sized effects indicated that higher emotional strain lowers the chances of being stably nondepressed and increases the probability of getting depressed under impending death (rising-fromfew). Consistent with findings from epidemiological studies in advanced old age [\(Luppa et al., 2012\)](#page-13-0), males were more likely to be stably nondepressed, whereas women were more likely to be chronically depressed (large effects). The context variables we considered appeared as altogether weak predictors of TTD-related trajectories. An explanation might be that these impacts on depressive symptoms were only mediated through other variables, such as functional health.

Finally, considering predictors of pronounced terminal increase of depressive symptoms as evident in the prototypically rising-from-few trajectory, it seems notable that altogether the weakest effects were revealed predicting this trajectory group. End-of-life decline of QoL was specifically predictive of a rising-from-few, but considering that the QoL measure embraces a "pleasure" component, it is arguable whether this medium-sized slope effect signals a condition triggering terminal increase of depressiveness, or whether it only reflects the interference of pleasurable experiences (anhedonia) implied by increasing depressive symptomatology. Refraining from speculative interpretations of these rather low effects, we conclude that our findings do not provide clear-cut hints toward conditions that might trigger terminal increase of depressiveness. Thus, the set of predictors that we examined accounted only sparsely for the probability of unfolding such terminal increase, suggesting that a more complex set of processes and conditions interacts in provoking terminal dynamics of depressive symptoms—yet to be investigated by future research.

### **Limitations**

Several limitations of this study deserve consideration. First, because ELSA participants aged 90 and above had been grouped into a single age category for confidentiality, some observations from the very old participants had to be excluded from the analyses when no exact age information (needed to compute TTD) was given at any measurement occasion (i.e., participants aged 90 whenever measured). Thus, a small portion (1.6%) of the participants that deceased and provided CES-D data had to be excluded from the analyses.

Second, TTD was scaled in years, based on the participant's year-of-death, and the longitudinal measurements were performed on a 2-year basis. Thus, it may be concerned whether shorter between-measurement intervals (days, months) are needed to confirm our findings and gain a more in-depth understanding of terminal changes in depressive symptoms.

Third, CES-D is not an age-specific instrument. Because depressive symptoms may present themselves differently in midcompared to late life [\(Blazer, 2003;](#page-12-0) [Fiske et al., 2009\)](#page-12-14), ageadapted measures are desirable. However, the CES-D is used in large surveys of the elder population (e.g., the Health and Retirement Study, [Steffick, 2000\)](#page-14-8).

Fourth, the CES-D items share some overlap with the QoL and loneliness scales. We argue that, as these measures could be distinguished empirically (in terms of "non-shared" variance, see [Table 2\)](#page-7-0) and theoretically (in terms of conceptual definitions), it does make sense to consider perceived loneliness and QoL as factors that may impact on an individual's generation of depressive symptoms, but we also call for caution with regard to the causalities underlying the associations evidenced in our analyses. ### **Conclusion and Future Research**

The findings presented in this study may hold important implications in practical, social, and ethical terms. On the one hand, the ongoing extension of the human life span seems to come not only with an increasing length of life as such, but also with a cohortrelated increase in psychosocial functions, such as well-being and positive affect [\(Christensen et al., 2013;](#page-12-16) [Gerstorf et al., 2015\)](#page-12-17). On the other hand, our data as well as other data [\(Hülür, Ram, &](#page-13-28) [Gerstorf, 2015\)](#page-13-28) suggest that living longer comes with pronounced physical and emotional vulnerabilities in the proximity to death. Therefore, a major public health concern may be the life situation at the very end of life. Given future substantiations, the present findings may, for example, help to develop screening instruments and prevention programs for at-risk groups.

Several aspects deserve consideration for future studies. First, the PPCT model [\(Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006\)](#page-12-7) states that reciprocal interactions (proximal processes) may explain how person and context variables translate into development at the microlevel of time—which the present study could not address. Therefore, future research should use experience sampling or observational studies of microlevel interactions to understand better the role of proximal interpersonal processes in the TTD-related development of depressive symptoms. Second, in line with the lines-of-defense model proposed by [Heckhausen, Wrosch, and](#page-13-29) [Schulz \(2013\),](#page-13-29) future research may address whether terminally increasing depressive symptoms could hold some "paradox" adaptive benefits, facilitating disengagement from life and preparing the system's end.
