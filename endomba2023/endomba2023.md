#### **Key summary points**

**Aim** What are the prevalence and correlates of insomnia disorder in older persons according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)?

**Findings** The pooled prevalence of insomnia disorder in older persons according to the DSM was 19.6%, and varied according to the sample size, the age cutof used, and the study quality (risk of bias). Insomnia in older persons is associated with the female gender, depression, anxiety, as well as cardiovascular, respiratory, and painful conditions.

**Message** Further researches are needed with a focus on African populations, the widening of sample sizes, and the comparison according to the institutionalization status.

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence and correlates of insomnia disorder in older persons, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM).

# **Introduction**

#### **Rationale**

Broadly defned as a quantitative and/or qualitative sleep deterioration originating from predisposing, precipitating, and perpetuating factors, insomnia is a frequently encountered sleep disturbance, and its nosology have markedly evolved since the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM)- III-R [[1](#page-9-0)[–3](#page-9-1)]. The respective mean frequencies of insomnia symptoms and insomnia disorder in the general population are estimated to be 30% (variations of 30% to 35%) and 10% (variations of 3.9% to 22.1%), respectively [\[4](#page-9-2)[–7](#page-9-3)]. These proportions are signifcantly afected by the type of assessment tool [[8,](#page-9-4) [9](#page-9-5)]. The reported prevalence of insomnia symptoms in older persons, respectively, fuctuates from 24.5 to 48% [\[8,](#page-9-4) [10](#page-9-6)–[13\]](#page-9-7), aging being a recognized predisposing factor of insomnia symptoms and disturbed sleep, particularly sleep fragmentation [[6,](#page-9-8) [11\]](#page-9-9). This is in part due to age-related changes such as an increase in sleep latency, time awake after sleep onset, the number of arousals from sleep (nocturnal awakenings), as well as a decrease in sleep efciency, total sleep time, and slow wave sleep (deep sleep) [\[11,](#page-9-9) [14–](#page-9-10)[16\]](#page-9-11). Also, modifcations include an advance in circadian timing (advanced sleep phase), a decrease in circadian amplitude, a reduced ability to adjust to changes in the phase of circadian rhythms, and an increase in daytime napping [[11](#page-9-9), [14](#page-9-10), [16\]](#page-9-11). Insomnia symptoms and disorder among seniors seem linked to somatic conditions (painful diseases, cardiovascular conditions, neurocognitive disorders, respiratory diseases, other sleep problems, etc.), mental health disorders (depression, anxiety, etc.), but also medications targeting subacute or chronic diseases (antihypertensive drugs, anti-infammatory drugs, diuretics, stimulants psychotropic drugs, etc.) [\[5](#page-9-12), [11,](#page-9-9) [15](#page-9-13)]. Insomnia disorder negatively impacts the quality of life as well as the morbimortality of older populations through mental health, functional, or somatic consequences such as cardiometabolic disturbances, frailty, and risk of falls [[5,](#page-9-12) [7,](#page-9-3) [11\]](#page-9-9).

The diagnosis of insomnia (disorder) is a clinical one and according to the ffth edition Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders (DSM-5), as well as the third

Taking into account the growing proportion of people aged 60 and over, the increased morbidity associated with aging and especially sleep disorders such as insomnia, and the lack of summarizing data on the epidemiology of insomnia disorder using internationally recognized diagnostic criteria, we planned this meta-analytical literature review.

# **Methods**

#### **Objectives**

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to address the following question: what is the pooled prevalence of insomnia disorder in older persons according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)? This question was built using the Condition–Context–Population (CoCoPop) approach [[23](#page-9-20)], with insomnia disorder (according to the criteria of any DSM version) and older persons representing, respectively, the condition and the population, and irrespective of any geographic setting.

# **Protocol and registration**

Before conducting this study, we submitted our protocol for registration in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The protocol was later registered with the following number: CRD42022344675. Our review's sections are detailed based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement, which checklist is available as supplementary data (Supplementary Table 1) [\[24](#page-9-21)].

# **Eligibility criteria**

For our systematic review, we targeted peer-reviewed articles published without timespan limitations, in English, French or Spanish. We focused on observational studies (crosssectional/ case–control/ cohort studies) reporting insomnia prevalence in older persons or enough crude data (numerator and denominator) to calculate it, with the diagnosis of insomnia made using any version of the DSM. We focused our selection on older persons (with a threshold of at least 60 years), living in institutions or in the community, and who have not been selected on the basis of a specifc condition.

We excluded from our syntheses and analyses unrelated or duplicated articles, unavailable full texts or abstract-only papers, and articles pertaining to studies whose key data were not accessible even after request from the authors.

# **Information sources**

We performed advanced and extensive searches in PubMed/ MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Web of Science (WoS). All these databases were last searched for relevant articles by February 21, 2022 at 07:30 (Greenwich Mean Time). We updated our searches on June 28, 2023, and in addition, references of selected articles were scrutinized to retrieve other pertinent ones.

#### **Search strategy**

For each database, and using the Boolean operator "AND", we joined two main queries, respectively, pertaining to "insomnia", "old persons", and "diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders". Keyterms and their synonyms were searched in titles and abstracts. A summary of our search strategies is available in Supplementary Table 2. All the records (from the three databases) were joined as a single review project in Rayyan, a web and mobile app for screening purposes in systematic reviews [[25](#page-9-22)]. After removing duplicates, we proceeded to article selection.

# **Selection process and data collection**

At this step, two authors (FTE and PYT) independently selected articles by examining titles and abstracts of each article, in accordance with our eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by a consensus or if necessary, by a third assessor (EC or DLAW).

After selection, we collected data of each retained article using a predefned and pretested extraction tool. This task was independently ensured by two authors (FTE and PYT) with discrepancies resolution by consensus or with the intervention of a third assessor.

#### **Data items**

The extraction process allowed us to collect data regarding bibliometric details (name of the frst author and year of publication), study characteristics (country, study design, sample size, data collection period), participants' characteristics (age cutof used in each individual study for inclusion, mean age and range, total sample size, total number of males and females, response rate), and outcomes (DSM version used to diagnose insomnia, number of participants with a positive diagnosis of insomnia disorder).

# **Study risk of bias assessment**

The risk of bias of selected articles was determined using the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI's) critical appraisal checklist for studies reporting prevalence data [[26\]](#page-10-0). This tool assesses nine items: (1) sample frame adequacy, (2) recruitment method, (3) sample size, (4) participants and the setting, (5) coverage, (6) diagnostic methods, (7) the reliability and standardization of measurements, (8) statistical analysis, and (9) the response rate. For each item, the available options were: 'yes', 'no', and 'unclear'. The number of 'yes' answers to these nine domains was counted, with a higher number of yes representing less risk of bias. Studies were characterized as follows: low risk of bias (≥70% of questions answered "yes"), moderate risk of bias (≥50% and<70% of questions answered "yes"), and high risk of bias (<50% of questions answered "yes"). Two authors independently ensured the assessment of the methodological quality (FTE and PYT).

#### **Efect measures**

We used the R statistical software (version 4.0.3, The R Foundation for statistical computing, Vienna, Austria) for our analyses. Insomnia prevalence was obtained by the ratio between the number of insomnia cases and the total number of participants tested for insomnia diagnosis. We reported prevalence data as proportions, and used a random-efects model with a 95% confdence interval (CI) to pool data. We

did a Freeman–Tukey transformation (arcsine square root transformation) to reduce the efect of studies with extremely small or large prevalence estimates [\[27](#page-10-1), [28\]](#page-10-2). The results of this step were graphically illustrated in a forest plot, with heterogeneity appraisal using the Cochrane's Q statistic and *I* 2 values [[29](#page-10-3)]. We planned to classify heterogeneity as it follows: (1) 0% to 40%: might not be important; (2) 30% to 60%: may represent moderate heterogeneity; (3) 50% to 90%: may represent substantial heterogeneity; (4) 75% to 100%: considerable heterogeneity. In addition to the global estimation of insomnia prevalence, we performed subgroup analyses according to studies' sample sizes (with the cutof of 323 according to the generally admitted prevalence of insomnia of 30%), age thresholds used to select participants (60 or 65 years), world regions, and the DSM version used. Sensitivity analyses were done by assessing the efect of sequential exclusion of each article, and we realized a metaregression by considering the mean age, the gender ratio, the sample size, and the risk of bias score. We also narratively synthetized signifcant insomnia correlates.

#### **Reporting bias assessment**

We used both visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger's tests to evaluate publication bias [[30\]](#page-10-4). A statistically signifcant publication bias is considered for *p* values<0.10.

## **Certainty of evidence**

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to examine certainty of evidence [\[31](#page-10-5), [32](#page-10-6)]. This approach targets fve axes: study limitations (risk of bias of the studies included), imprecision (sample sizes and confdence interval), indirectness (generalizability), inconsistency (heterogeneity), and publication bias, as stated in the GRADE handbook [\[31\]](#page-10-5). We further characterize the evidence's certainty as high, moderate, low, or very low.

# **Results**

#### **Study selection**

After applying our search strategies in predefned databases, we retrieved 441 results from which 235 duplicates have been removed (via a web dedicated tool, with a manual verifcation). From the 206 remaining results, 161 were excluded and 45 were assessed for eligibility. We excluded 30 of these 45 articles due to non-compatible outcomes and/or populations (with respect to our selection criteria), full-text missing, and duplicated data. While updating our searches, we had 1 additional article, and therefore 16 papers were fnally retained for qualitative and quantitative syntheses [[33–](#page-10-7)[48\]](#page-10-8). Our study selection process is summarized in Fig. [1](#page-3-0).

## **Characteristics of included studies**

Our systematic review included a total number of 18,270 participants (sample sizes ranging from 104 to 4,064), from cross-sectional studies conducted between 1994 and 2019 in 9 countries (and 3 continents, notably Asia, America, and Europe). More precisely, we had four studies from Spain [\[37,](#page-10-9) [39](#page-10-10), [40,](#page-10-11) [42\]](#page-10-12), two from the United States of America [[33](#page-10-7), [46](#page-10-13)] as well as from Taiwan [[36](#page-10-14), [44](#page-10-15)] and Canada [\[38,](#page-10-16) [47](#page-10-17)], and one, respectively, from Turkey [[35](#page-10-18)], Germany [[43\]](#page-10-19), Italy [\[45\]](#page-10-20), South Korea [[41](#page-10-21)], India [[48](#page-10-8)], and China [\[38](#page-10-16)]. Most of the selected studies were community based (*n*=14), one was institution based, and one was conducted among outpatients.

Gender distributions were detailed in 12 studies (15,047 participants), and considering these studies, the male/ female ratio was 0.89 (nearly 53% of women). The age threshold of inclusion was at least 65 years in 11 studies, and at least 60 years in 5 studies. The mean age was available in eight studies and varied from 65.9 to 83.1 years. As diagnostic tools, the DSM-III-R, DSM-IV, DSM-IV-TR, and DSM-5, were, respectively, used in 1, 11, 3, and 1 studies. In Table [1,](#page-4-0) we recapitulated the main characteristics of studies included in our syntheses.

The total score pertaining to the risk of bias varied from 4 to 9, with a mean of 6.4/9. The risk of bias was low, moderate, and high, for, respectively, four, eleven, and one studies. A question score of zero was most frequently encountered for questions n°5, 7, and 8. In Table [2](#page-5-0), we summarized information pertaining to risk of bias assessment.

Based on a random-effects meta-analytic model, the pooled frequency of insomnia was 19.6% (95% CI [12.3%; 28.3%]) with substantial heterogeneity amid studies included in the analysis (*I* 2= 99%, *τ*2= 0.0421, *p*

value<0.01) (Fig. [2](#page-5-1).). Our results also signifcantly difered according to the study quality (Supplementary Fig. 5), with a pooled prevalence of 21% (95% CI [11.2%; 32.9%]) for studies with a low risk of bias. ## **Reporting biases**

The funnel plot we used to qualitatively appraise publication bias appeared asymmetrical. In addition, our quantitative assessment using the Egger's test displayed signifcant results (*t*=2.43, df=14, *p* value=0.0289). These results suggest a publication bias (Fig. [3](#page-6-1)).

# **Certainty of evidence**

We settled the certainty of our meta-analytic review evidence as very low. More precisely, our rating started from low certainty since most of the studies were not population based. We downgraded the level of evidence certainty considering that there was a publication bias, a very serious inconsistency (heterogeneity), and a serious imprecision (Supplementary Table 5).

# **Discussion**

This systematic review aimed to address the epidemiological features of insomnia disorder in older persons according to the DSM, which involves the estimation of prevalence data, and the question of correlates. Based on 16 reports from 10 countries (and 3 continents, notably Asia, America, and Europe), including 18,270 individuals, the pooled prevalence rate of insomnia was 19.6%. Our subgroup and meta-regression analyses found that this prevalence was signifcantly and negatively correlated with the study sample size. The pooled frequency also signifcantly varied according to the cutof used, but not the DSM version. Of note, 2/3 of included studies assessed insomnia disorder with the DSM-IV. As correlates of insomnia disorder, we noticed the female gender, depression, anxiety, the presence of more than one somatic illness especially cardiovascular, respiratory, and painful ones.

The prevalence resulting from our analyses is similar or greater than the one of the insomnia disorder in the general population, as reported by most of the large sample and/or multinational studies, or meta-analytic reviews [\[6,](#page-9-8) [8](#page-9-4), [22](#page-9-19)]. Indeed, its prevalence is generally estimated at 10% [[7](#page-9-3), [8,](#page-9-4) [22](#page-9-19)]. More specifcally, in an article published in 2002, while considering insomnia symptoms and daytime consequences, the frequency oscillated between 9 and 15%, and while using the DSM-IV classifcation for a decision-making diagnosis, 6% of the general population were concerned by insomnia [[10](#page-9-6)]. Roth et al*.*, through the America Insomnia Survey which includes 10,094 participants, found that insomnia prevalence estimates varied widely from 22.1% for DSM-IV-TR to 3.9% for ICD-10 (International Statistical Classifcation of Diseases, Tenth Revision) criteria [[8\]](#page-9-4). Otherwise, the frequency we are reporting is lower than the one reported for insomnia symptoms in older persons, which ranged from 24.5 to 48% according to epidemiological studies, and depending on assessment tools [[8,](#page-9-4) [10](#page-9-6)[–13\]](#page-9-7). This difference might be related to the lower expression/perception of daytime dysfunction among older adults [\[5](#page-9-12), [20](#page-9-17)]. The use of scales/questionnaires such as the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) might overestimate insomnia frequency notably considering the diference in terms of evolution duration. With these tools, symptoms are assessed considering the precedent 2 to 4 weeks, meanwhile with DSM criteria, a duration of at least 3 months is required [[17\]](#page-9-14). Also, the DSM criteria take into consideration a minimal number of three symptomatic nights per week, as well as diferential diagnoses and/or comorbid conditions, which is not the case for previously mentioned scales/questionnaires [\[17](#page-9-14)]. A signifcant proportion of the population with sleep complaints do not ft into DSM and ICSD criteria [[21,](#page-9-18) [22](#page-9-19)], and previously published meta-analyses on insomnia prevalence in older adults mostly included articles that used screening tools [[12,](#page-9-23) [13\]](#page-9-7).

The relatively higher rates of insomnia symptoms/disorder among older persons could be at least partially explained by intrinsic changes in sleep/wake rhythms with age [\[11,](#page-9-9) [16](#page-9-11)]. As previously mentioned, these changes include, for instance, an advanced in circadian timing (they fall asleep and wake-up earlier), a reduced nocturnal sleep duration, an increased count of nocturnal awakenings, a prolonged time of wake after sleep onset, a decrease in slow wave sleep quality/quantity, an increased time spent in stages 1 or 2 of non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, and an increased frequency of daytime naps [[11](#page-9-9), [14](#page-9-10)[–16,](#page-9-11) [49\]](#page-10-23). On a mechanistic view, these modifcations in sleep architecture could be linked to biological changes [[14,](#page-9-10) [49](#page-10-23)]. These encompass a decrease in the mean amplitude of the endogenous circadian temperature, of melatonin oscillation (decline of the maximal nocturnal peak), as well as growth hormone, this last being implicated in slow wave sleep [[14,](#page-9-10) [49](#page-10-23)[–51](#page-10-24)]. Aside, there are changes in the circadian rhythm of cortisol, with an advanced phase regarding secretion, and raised nocturnal levels [[14,](#page-9-10) [51,](#page-10-24) [52](#page-10-25)]. The natural lessening in sleep quality and quantity among older people might at least predispose or perpetuate insomnia. This, through unrealistic and anxiogenic expectations about sleep, but also frequent naps which can reduce the homeostatic process, therefore, lowers the nocturnal propensity to fall asleep [\[11](#page-9-9), [49](#page-10-23), [53\]](#page-10-26). Indeed, daytime napping are more prevalent in older persons, with a frequency ranging from 20 to 60% according to epidemiological studies, and can be explained by a reduced daytime activity, the lack of social interactions, and for planned/ perceived naps, the possible aim to compensate a nocturnal sleep perceived as of poor quality [[16,](#page-9-11) [53,](#page-10-26) [54](#page-10-27)]. Moreover, these naps might also be the result of medications targeting insomnia, anxiety, depression, or chronic cardiometabolic disturbances, which most frequent side efects include fatigue and drowsiness [[16,](#page-9-11) [53](#page-10-26)]. Aside from these intrinsic factors afecting sleep after 60 years, some extrinsic factors seem to contribute to the greater prevalence of insomnia in this population compared to younger individuals [\[11,](#page-9-9) [15](#page-9-13)]. These factors include pain-associated impairments, cardiometabolic diseases, chronic respiratory illnesses, sleep

disorders (including other sleep disorders such as sleep disordered breathing, restless leg syndrome, REM sleep behavioral disorder), dementia, mental health conditions (notably depression), multiple medications, but also environment, especially for elders in institutions [\[11](#page-9-9), [15\]](#page-9-13).

While reviewing insomnia disorder correlates within the elder population, we found that females were more likely to have insomnia disorder than their male counterparts [\[55–](#page-10-28)[57\]](#page-10-29). This is corroborating reports in the general population. Indeed, Zeng and colleagues, through a meta-analysis that aimed to examine the gender diference regarding the prevalence of insomnia among the general population, found signifcantly higher prevalence among females [\[56](#page-10-30)]. More precisely, their analysis concerned 139,349 females and 187,559 males, and they reported an odds ratio of 1.58 [[56\]](#page-10-30). Similar conclusions were established by Zhang and Wing, whose meta-analytic study reported a female predisposition of insomnia (1,265,015 participants, female/ male: 718,828/546,187), with a risk ratio of 1.41 [\[55\]](#page-10-28). In their review article, the female predisposition was consistent and gradual across age, with more signifcance in older persons [[55](#page-10-28)]. A range of factors could explain the female excess of insomnia, the most reported one being the higher prevalence of anxiety and depression in women, the greater ease to express emotional distress and somatic symptoms, but also the postmenopausal period (especially for women around 60 years old) [[55](#page-10-28), [56](#page-10-30), [58,](#page-10-31) [59\]](#page-10-32). It has been shown that postmenopausal women, especially the ones who had never used hormone therapy, had a poorer sleep efciency (higher odds of SE<70%), and a longer time of wake after sleep onset (higher odds of WASO≥90 min) [[59,](#page-10-32) [60\]](#page-11-0). This might be partially due to the postmenopausal profle of sex steroid hormones, since estrogen infuences various sleep/ wake cycle determinants such as mood, body temperature, stress reactivity, and circadian rhythm (by interacting with sex hormones receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus) [[59,](#page-10-32) [61](#page-11-1), [62](#page-11-2)].

The role of depression and anxiety in the occurrence of insomnia disorder among the older persons might have a plurality of explanations, as in younger adults. We have, for instance, stressful life events and stress reactivity, since insomnia, anxiety, and depression can be considered as stress-related disorders [\[63](#page-11-3), [64](#page-11-4)]. Other mechanistic hypotheses of the insomnia–depression comorbidity (and to a lesser extent the insomnia–anxiety comorbidity) involve disruptions in circadian and homeostatic processes among patients with depression and/or anxiety [[63,](#page-11-3) [65](#page-11-5), [66\]](#page-11-6). These implicate at least partially a dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, a dysregulation in serotoninergic, orexinergic, dopaminergic, glutaminergic and GABAergic neurotransmissions, as well as an altered neuroplasticity [[63,](#page-11-3) [65](#page-11-5), [66](#page-11-6)]. Regarding antidepressants, some of them have a negative impact on sleep, especially a reduced sleep latency and REM sleep time with serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and an increased wakefulness with some selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) also in general anxiety disorders [[54](#page-10-27), [67](#page-11-7), [68\]](#page-11-8). As the link between depression and anxiety is known to be bidirectional, and considering the fact that most of our included studies were cross-sectional, the association we found might be due to the impact of insomnia on mood [[64,](#page-11-4) [69,](#page-11-9) [70\]](#page-11-10). Indeed, insomnia symptoms/disorder are reported to independently increase the risk of subsequent depression in older persons, and the supposed mechanisms encompass neuroinfammation, activation of stress system, oxidative stress, reduction in brainderived neurotrophic factor, and alterations in melatonin metabolism [\[11](#page-9-9), [64](#page-11-4), [70](#page-11-10), [71](#page-11-11)].

The impact of chronic cardiovascular, pain-associated, and respiratory diseases on insomnia disorder prevalence among older persons might be linked to multiple medications including some beta-blockers (known to reduce melatonin levels), corticosteroids, and bronchodilators medications, but also frequent arousals due to nocturnal symptoms of concerned illnesses [\[11](#page-9-9), [54](#page-10-27), [72\]](#page-11-12).

To our knowledge, this study is the frst one to summarize data on the prevalence and the correlates of insomnia disorders in older persons, with a diagnosis made using DSM criteria. Nevertheless, the interpretation of our fndings should take into account some limitations. We have, for instance, the substantial heterogeneity in the pooled frequency of insomnia among included studies, which can be in part related to the disparateness of studies' sample sizes. In addition, our results displayed a publication bias, and the level or certainty was very low. Other limitations are the unrepresentativeness of world regions such as Africa, especially considering the difculties in providing health care in older African people [\[73,](#page-11-13) [74](#page-11-14)], and the lack of studies comparing institutionalized patients and their non-institutionalized counterparts, since the environment plays a key role in sleep/wake rhythms [\[72](#page-11-12), [75](#page-11-15)].

The results of this meta-analytic review seem to call health care workers, especially medical doctors, to have a particular regard on older patients' sleep in general, and specifcally on insomnia symptoms. For further, DSM insomnia diagnostic criteria should be appraised in patients with suggestive symptoms, especially for women, as well as in case of, mental health illnesses, multiple medications, somatic diseases involving the cardiovascular and respiratory systems. Our study might also support the need of further studies addressing the limitations mentioned in a previous section, with notably the focus on African populations, the widening of sample sizes, the comparison according to the institutionalization status.
