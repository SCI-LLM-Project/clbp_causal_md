# **in the Elderly**

Alastair **J. Flint, M.B., Ch.B., F.R.C.P.(C), F.R.A.N.Z.C.P.**

*Objective: The author reviewed the epidemiology and comorbidity ofanxiety disorders in the elderly. Method: Data from I 970 onward were obtained through a computerized literature search, a review oflndex Medicus, and the bibliographies ofretrieved articles. Eight randomsample community surveys of anxiety disorders in persons 60 years of age or older were identified. Studies relating to the comorbidity of late-life anxiety and depression, dementia, alcoholism, and medical illness were also reviewed. Results: The majority of studies showed that anxiety disorders are less common in the elderly than in younger adults. Generalized anxiety disorder and phobias account for most anxiety in late life; panic disorder is rare. Agoraphobia, and possibly obsessive-compulsive disorder in females, may occur as a primary disorder for the first time in old age, whereas simple phobia, obsessive-compulsive disorder in males, and panic disorder either persist from younger years or arise in the context of another psychiatric or medical disorder. There is considerable comorbidity ofgeriatric depression and generalized anxiety disorder and phobias, although the depression usually goes untreated or is inappropriately treated with benzodiazepines. The rate ofcomorbidity ofanxiety and medical illness and alcoholism is lower in the elderly than in younger persons. Conclusions: Epidemiologic data on the prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and the first oc currence of generalized anxiety disorder and PTSD in late life are still needed. Further comorbidity studies are needed to determine the extent to which anxiety arises secondary to depression, as well as the optimal treatment and prognosis for this mixed state.*

**T he elderly population is growing at <sup>a</sup> rapid rate. To make the most effective use of limited health care resources, there is a need for accurate information on psychiatric disorders in this group. To date, anxiety disorders in the elderly have received much less study than depression and dementia. Factors as sociated with aging, such as social isolation, decreased autonomy, financial insecurity, poor health, and im-**

**pending death, might be expected to increase the prevalence of anxiety in late life. However, anxiety disorders account for only a fraction of psychiatric hospital admissions of patients over the age of 65 years, and there is a decline in anxiety disorders among elderly hospital outpatients (1). Nevertheless, it is possible that elderly persons with anxiety do not present themselves to health services or are not recognized by them, leading to hidden morbidity (1). Communitybased epidemiologic surveys are likely to provide more accurate data about the extent of anxiety in the elderly, thereby facilitating treatment planning, service delivery, and, possibly, prevention. The purpose of this article, therefore, is to bring together and review the existing epidemiologic data on late-life anxiety disorders.**

**Until the late 1970s anxiety in late life was studied, along with some forms of depression and personality disorder, under the rubric of "neurosis." It is only re cently that research has focused on specific types of** anxiety disorders, **and as a result there is a paucity of data on these subtypes among the elderly. Nevertheless, this review discusses anxiety disorders in old age in accordance with the DSM-III-R classification. There is also discussion of the comorbidity of anxiety and other psychiatric and medical disorders given the known frequency and importance of these associations in younger adults.**

**Information for this review was collected through 1) a MEDLINE search for English-language citations from 1970 to 1992 on anxiety disorders in the elderlywhich included the subheadings for phobias, panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and mixed anxiety-depressive disorder-and anxiety combined with dementia, medical illness, and alcoholism, 2) a manual search of Index Medicus from 1970 to 1992 according to the categories just listed, and 3) a review of the bibliographies of retrieved articles to iden**tify **other potentially relevant papers.**

# **ELDERLY**

#### *The Studies*

**The literature search identified eight random-sample community surveys of anxiety disorders or anxiety states in persons aged 60 years or older. Three of the surveys (2-4) were of individuals aged 18 years or older, and different age strata, including the elderly, were studied. The remaining five studies** *(5-9)* **exam** med **the elderly only.**

**The Epidemiologic Catchment Area (ECA) study (2) reported on the prevalence of three anxiety disorders (panic disorder, phobias, and obsessive-compulsive disorder) in 18,571 noninstitutionalized adults from five sites across the United States. Of these, 5,702 subjects were 65 years of age or older, representing an oversam pling of the elderly. One-month prevalence rates were determined with the use of the National Institute of Mental Health Diagnostic Interview Schedule (DIS), and diagnoses were made in accordance with DSM-III criteria, with the exception that they were hierarchy free (i.e., could be made in the presence of another psychiatric disorder).**

**The Edmonton study (3), using the same methodology as the ECA study,surveyed 6-month prevalence rates of the same three anxiety disorders in 3,258 mdividuals (of whom 358 were 65 years of age or older) living at home in Edmonton, Canada. One hundred ninety-nine elderly persons living in nursing homes were also studied.**

**In the 1** *979* **National Survey of Psychotherapeutic Drug Use (4), the self-rated Hopkins Symptom Check-** **list was administered to 3,161 American adults aged 1 8-79 years, of whom 442 were 65 years of age or older. DSM-III diagnoses of agoraphobia-panic, other phobias, and generalized anxiety disorder were made in a hierarchical manner on the basis of symptoms present over the preceding 12 months.**

**Copeland et al. examined the 1-month prevalence rates of phobias, generalized anxiety disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder in a total sample of 1,911 elderly persons living in Liverpool** *(5),* **London (6), and New York (6) with the use of the Geriatric Mental State interview and computer-generated AGECAT diagno ses. The AGECAT system uses a strict hierarchy of diagnosis in which phobic and nonphobic anxiety are the two bottom levels of eight diagnostic clusters. Each cluster has a confidence level of 0-5, where level 3 or above is a "case," and levels 1 and 2 are "subcases."**

**The Guys/Age Concern Survey (7) of 890 persons 65 years of age or older, living in a low socioeconomic area of inner-city London, used a structured interview based on a specifically designed scale that drew questions from the Present State Examination, the Geriatric Mental State interview, and the Comprehensive Assessment and Referral Evaluation Schedule. Diagnoses of generalized anxiety disorder and phobias were supported by a CATEGO-derived index of definition of 4 or more (an index of definition of** *S* **or more implied that sufficient symptoms were present for the CATEGO computer program to assign an ICD-8 diagnosis); panic disorder was defined according to DSM-III criteria. Prevalence rates were for 1 month.**

**Finally, the studies of Bergmann (8) (300 subjects aged 65-80 years) and Maule et al. (9) (487 subjects aged 62-90 years), conducted during the 1960s, used semistructured interviews to elicit symptoms, but they did not use diagnostic criteria to arrive at the diagnosis of "anxiety state" and did not specify the prevalence period. Subtypes of anxiety were not studied. Berg mann excluded cases of anxiety associated with organic brain syndrome and psychosis but included depression, and Maule et al. diagnosed anxiety state only after depression and organic brain syndrome had been ruled out. The group studied by Maule et al. included persons who had been living in nursing homes for less than 2 years, although the authors did not report on this group separately. It should be noted that the Edmonton study (3) was the only other survey to include institutionalized elderly persons.**

**Most of the studies (2, 3,** 4-7) **used trained nonmedical personnel to interview subjects, while the remainder (6, 8,** *9)* **used research psychiatrists. Interrater reliabil**ity **was assessed in five of the studies (2, 3,** *5-7)* **and found to be satisfactory.**

Both the ECA (2) and Edmonton (3) studies found the overall prevalence of anxiety disorders to be lower among the elderly than among all age groups combined. In the ECA study the peak prevalence of combined anxiety disorder diagnoses was in the 25- to 44-year age group. Throughout the life span women had approximately twice the rate of anxiety that men had. The Edmonton survey also studied the rate of anxiety in nursing home populations. In comparison with the household samples (table 1), total anxiety disorder was lower among institutionalized men (1.4%) but higher among institutionalized women (7.1%). The lower finding for male subjects may have been the result of a high nonsampling rate in that group, mainly due to cognitive impairment. In contrast with these two studies, the National Survey of Psychotherapeutic Drug Use (4) found total anxiety to be slightly higher among individuals 65 years of age or older than among all age groups combined and only marginally lower than the peak prevalence. However, the higher overall prevalence among the elderly was accounted for by a high rate of generalized anxiety disorder, which was not examined in the ECA and Edmonton studies.

Total anxiety rates in studies that examined the elderly alone (table 2) varied considerably, from 0.7% in the New York study by Copeland et al. (6) to 18.6% reported by Bergmann (8). Possible reasons for these discrepant findings are discussed later in this article. Bergmann was the only investigator to separate late-onset from chronic, early-onset anxiety. Chronic anxiety, however, also encompassed "anxiety-prone" personalities. Of his subjects, 8.6% had late-onset anxiety and the remaining 10.0% had a chronic state. In both

groups, approximately one-third were rated as having anxiety of moderate severity and two-thirds as having mild severity.

# Specific Anxiety Disorders

Phobic disorders. The ECA study (2) found that of the three anxiety disorders surveyed, phobias were the most common for subjects of all ages, including the elderly, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1. Phobias were the second most frequent psychiatric diagnoses, next to cognitive impairment, for both men and women 65 years of age or older. With respect to types of phobia in the elderly, Weissman et al. (10) noted a 6-month prevalence of 3.1% for simple phobia and 1.4% for agoraphobia at the New Haven ECA site. Social phobia prevalences were 0.1% and 1.5% for men and 0.3% and 1.9% for women at the St. Louis and Baltimore ECA sites, respectively (11). The Edmonton survey (3) also found that phobias were more common than panic disorder and obsessive-compulsive disorder among noninstitutionalized elderly persons, but phobias were no more common than panic disorder and less common than obsessive-compulsive disorder among subjects in nursing homes. However, Copeland et al. (5, 6) and the National Survey of Psychotherapeutic Drug Use (4) surveyed generalized anxiety disorder in addition to phobias and found that even when a hierarchical approach to the diagnosis of anxiety disorders was used, phobias were less common than generalized anxiety disorder. In fact, Copeland and coworkers reported that only 0.7% of their subjects in Liverpool qualified as having a case of phobia (5), and the case rate in London and New York was 0.0% (6).

In contrast to the ECA data (2), but consistent with findings in the National Survey of Psychotherapeutic Drug Use (4), agoraphobia was most common (7.8%), followed by simple phobia (2.1%) and social phobia (1.3%). There were no significant differences in the rates of phobia for individuals 75 years of age or older compared with those 65–74 years of age. Also, there was no correlation between phobias and social class, in contrast to the ECA findings, where in all age groups phobias increased with lower socioeconomic status (12). However, this negative finding in the Guy's survey may have been due to the socioeconomic homogeneity of the sample.

Using ECA data, Eaton et al. (13) calculated the annual first incidence of phobic disorders in the 65-yearsor-older age group, where there were 4.29 disorders per 100 person-years of risk, compared to a peak incidence of 4.58 for the 30- to 44-year age group. This difference was not statistically significant. These incidence data implied that the majority of cases of phobia in old age were of new onset, not the result of younger phobic subjects growing old. This interpretation is supported by the findings of Lindesay (1), who compared 60 elderly phobic subjects with nonphobic age- and sexmatched subjects. He found that the majority of subjects with agoraphobia had a late onset, and many had moderate to severe social impairment. However, simple phobia usually began in childhood or early adulthood and was associated with only mild social impairment.

Only 11% of Lindesay's late-onset agoraphobic subjects had histories of panic attacks (1). This finding is consistent with data from the ECA study (12), which found that in the general population, only 6% of individuals with agoraphobia also had had panic disorder over the preceding 6-month period, and 10% had a lifetime history of panic disorder. Even when the DSM-III panic disorder requirement of three panic attacks in 3 weeks was dispensed with, Thompson et al. (14) found that only 18% of agoraphobic subjects had had four or more panic symptoms over a 6-month period. Thus, these studies found that the majority of individuals with agoraphobia, both young and old, did not have coexisting panic disorder and that the rate of comorbidity of these disorders remained constant throughout life. The implication of these data is that for most elderly individuals, agoraphobia is not a conditioned response to panic attacks, and other etiological factors play a role. In fact, Lindesay (1) found that in most cases, late-onset agoraphobic subjects attributed the start of their fears to episodes of physical illness or to traumatic events such as falls or muggings. He also found a significant association between phobic disorders and early parental loss due to separation or death, suggesting that early experiences may confer a vulnerability to the subsequent development of phobic disorders. However, a similar relationship has also been reported for depression (15), and since one-third of the phobic patients in Lindesay's study also had significant depression, it is possible that the depressive disorder accounted for at least some of this association.

Panic disorder. According to ECA data (2), panic disorder is the least common of the anxiety disorders. The 1-month prevalence for combined age groups was 0.5%, with females more frequently affected than males. As with phobic disorders, the peak prevalence was in the 25- to 44-year age group. Thereafter the frequency steadily declined, so that in the elderly there was a 0.2% prevalence among females and a 0.0% prevalence among males. The ECA annual incidence (13) for persons 65 years of age or older was 0.04%, implying that the few cases of panic disorder in old age did not arise de novo but instead had persisted from younger years. This is further supported by the work of Sheehan et al. (16), who examined the age at onset of "endogenous anxiety" (panic disorder, panic plus generalized anxiety, and generalized anxiety) in clinic patients and found that 96% of cases began before the age of 40 years, with a mean age at onset of 24 years. The Guy's survey (7) failed to find any cases of DSM-III-defined panic disorder within the month preceding the survey. In Edmonton (3), the rate of panic disorder in household samples was low (0.3%); it increased to 1.0% for those in nursing homes. Again, females accounted for all cases. Parmelee et al. (17) surveyed 994 residents of a long-term care facility in Philadelphia (mean age=84 years) and found that 0.3% met modified DSM-III-R criteria for panic disorder. Thus, according to surveys from the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom, panic disorder in the elderly is rare, and when it does occur, females account for all cases.

**A few casereports (1 8-20) have described panic attacks (not necessarily meeting the criteria for panic disorder) arising for the first time in old age.However, most of these subjects had another psychiatric disorder-most** commonly, depression-or **coexistent medical illness. Beit man et al. (21) reported on nine elderly, female cardiology patients who presented with chest pain, did not have angiographic evidence ofcoronary** artery **disease, and met the DSM-III-R criteria for panic disorder. However, no attempts were made to rule out other medical or psychiatric conditions, although three of the nine subjects re** ported **symptoms of depression. Also, patients with panic disorder were taking significantly more cardiac medications than those without panic.**

*Generalized anxiety disorder.* **The ECA study did not report prevalence rates for generalized anxiety disorder. However, Blazer et al. (22) reviewed data collected on 2,902 individuals at the Durham site and found that the 1-year prevalence of generalized anxiety disorder for adults 18 years of age or older was 2.2% (2.6% for women; 1.8% for men). Dubovsky (23) reported 1 month to 12-month prevalence rates of2.S%-6.4% for DSM-III generalized anxiety disorder and contended that in the general population, this disorder accounts for up to one-half of all anxiety disorder diagnoses.**

**In the elderly, the prevalence of generalized anxiety disorder has been found to vary from 0.7% to 7.1% (tables 1 and 2). Blazer et al. (24) found that when cases of major depression were excluded, the 6-month prevalence of generalized anxiety disorder in persons 65 years of age or older was 1** *.9%,* **with the highest rate in black females. These investigators found that only 3.0% of cases of generalized anxiety disorder began after the age of 65. Generalized anxiety disorder ac counted for 60%-100% of cases of anxiety disorder in the studies of Copeland et al.** *(5,* **6), 70% of cases in the National Survey of Psychotherapeutic Drug Use (4), but only one-third of cases in the Guy's/Age Concern Survey (7). In the Guy's survey there was no significant difference in rate of generalized anxiety disorder be**tween **individuals 65-74 years of age and those over** *75* **years, and there was no relation between generalized** anxiety disorder and socioeconomic status.

*PTSD.* **There are no published data on the epidemi**ology **of PTSD in old age. However, investigators have studied PTSD in Holocaust survivors (25, 26), as well as veterans who were prisoners of war (POWs) during World War II (27-29). Kuch and Cox (25) found that 46% of a group of elderly Holocaust survivors met the criteria for PTSD and that those exposed to atrocities suffered more than those who had not been exposed. Studies of former POWs found that S0%-67% met the criteria for PTSD immediately following repatriation, and 20%-29% continued to meet the criteria 40 years later (27-29). The strongest predictors of PTSD in vet erans were having been tortured and the degree of weight loss during captivity (28). These studies, therefore, show that PTSD persists into late life in a significant proportion of individuals who suffered severe trauma during World War II. However, there was no**

**mention of PTSD developing for the first time in old age in these at-risk individuals. Also, the literature review did not find any reports of PTSD developing in relation to trauma sustained in old age. However, as noted previously, Lindesay (1** *)***did find that most cases of late-onset agoraphobia arose secondary to traumas such as muggings, falls, or severe illness.**

*Obsessive-compulsive disorder.* **The ECA study (2) found that obsessive-compulsive disorder had a peak prevalence of 1.8% in the 18- to 24-year age group, followed by a gradual reduction with age to 0.8% in subjects 65 years of age or older (table 1** *).* **The differ ence between female and male subjects that characterized phobias, panic disorder, and generalized anxiety disorder was far less pronounced for obsessive-compulsive disorder, with a marginally higher rate among females. Eaton et al. (13) calculated that while the annual incidence of obsessive-compulsive disorder in men decreased steadily from 0.66% in the 18- to 29-year group to 0.12% in persons 65 years of age or older, it rose slightly in elderly women following a low of 0.69% in the 45- to 64-year group. In the Edmonton survey (3) the 6-month prevalence of obsessive-compulsive disorder was considerably higher among institutionalized elderly women than among those in the corn munity and slightly higher among institutionalized elderly men than among their counterparts in the corn munity. The rate among the elderly living at home was comparable to the rate for all age groups combined (table 1** *).* **Consistent with their findings of lower prevalence rates of other types of anxiety disorders, Copeland et a!.** *(5,* **6) reported that cases of obsessive-compulsive disorder ranged from 0.0% in New York to 0.6% in London. Finally, Jenike (30) reported that of 1 83 evaluations at a specialized obsessive-compulsive disorder clinic, only 4% of this selected population were aged 60 years or older, although the proportion of new-onset cases as compared with chronic cases in aging patients was not stated.**

# OTHER **DISORDERS IN LATE LIFE**

## *Depression*

**Anxiety and depression often coexist. In the ECA study (12) it was estimated that during a 6-month penod, anxiety occurred in 33% of adults with a depressive disorder, and depression was present in 21 % of those with an anxiety disorder. The relation between anxiety and depression is complex and remains controversial; the reader is referred to reviews of this topic by Stavrakaki and Vargo (31) and Liebowitz et al. (32).**

**In the elderly, the comorbidity of anxiety and depression has received limited study. The Guy's/Age Concern Survey (7) found that 39% of phobic subjects also had depression, compared with 1 1 % of nonphobic subjects, a statistically significant difference. Also, severity scores for generalized anxiety disorder and depression**

**were significantly associated, as were cases of generalized anxiety disorder and cases of depression. Ninety one percent of persons with generalized anxiety disorder had depressive symptoms, supporting the findings** in younger patients (3 1 *,***32) of considerable overlap between generalized anxiety disorder and depressive disorders. In a study of 60 male psychiatric inpatients aged** *55* **years or older, Hyer et al. (33) found a high degree of association between scores on the State Anxiety Scale and scores on the Beck Depression Inventory. Parmelee et al. (1 7) studied the longitudinal relationship between generalized anxiety disorder and depression. In a longterm care facility, 3% of the residents met modified DSM-III-R criteria for generalized anxiety disorder, and an additional 13% had subsyndromal anxiety. Of the generalized anxiety disorder group, 60% had major depression, compared with only 4% of the nonanxious residents, and another 1 7% had minor depression. At 1-year follow-up, 60% ofthe residents with generalized anxiety disorder had recovered, and this was signifi**cantly associated with concomitant improvement in de**pression. The 1-year incidence of generalized anxiety disorder was 2.3%, and over two-thirds of these new cases arose in the context of a new or preexisting depressive illness.**

**Other investigators have examined the frequency of anxiety disorders in elderly patients with diagnosed depression. Ben-Arie et a!. (34) used the Present State Examination to study a group of elderly subjects in South Africa. Of those with a CATEGO diagnosis of depression, 26.3% had generalized anxiety and 5.3% had panic attacks, compared to 2.3% and 0.0%, respectively, of subjects without depression. Although depression increased the probability of panic attacks in this elderly group, the rate of comorbidity of these two disorders was considerably less than that quoted for younger patients (35). A significantly greater percentage of depressed subjects than nondepressed subjects were taking minor tranquilizers, but none had been prescribed antidepressant medication, which suggests that the anxiety symptoms, rather than the depressive illness, were the focus of treatment. Similarly, despite Lindesay's finding** *(* **1** *)* **that one-third of patients with phobic disorder were also depressed, the majority of patients in his study did not receive any form of psychiatric treatment, and among those who were treated, benzodiazepines were the mainstay of therapy. Blazer et al. (36) followed up a group of elderly and middleaged patients who had been hospitalized for treatment of depression. At 1- to 2-year follow-up, 32.1% of the patients reported anxiety, mostly in the morning. The patients with the most severe depression at assessment were more likely to have a poor outcome at follow-up and experience persistence of anxiety symptoms. Blazer et al. hypothesized that the presence of anxiety at follow-up was an indication of incomplete recovery from depression. Finally, Alexopoulos (37) reported that of 45 consecutive geriatric outpatients with major depression, 38% also met the DSM-III-R criteria for an anxiety disorder.**

**These studies show thatthere is considerable comor bidity of anxiety disorders and depression in late life. The rate of comorbidity of generalized anxiety disorder and depression in old age is similar to that quoted for younger patients. However, the depressed elderly appear to be at less risk ofdeveloping panic attacks. The studies that were cross-sectional in design do not tell us to what extent the anxiety and depression began con currently, as opposed to one disorder having arisen sec ondary to the other. However, the findings of Parmelee et al. (17) and Blazer et al. (36) suggest that in most cases, the anxiety occurs secondary to the depression.**

# *Dementia*

**Bergmann (38) observed that persons with early, mild dementia often had "neurotic" symptoms, including anxiety. However, there has been only one published study (39) that specifically examined the association between dementia and anxiety. Fifty subjects with dementia, most of them with Alzheimer's disease, who scored in the mild range of intellectual impairment on the Extended Scale for Dementia, were compared with 134 control subjects on measures of anxiety and depression. Anxiety and depression were assessed by self-report with the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale. This scale inquires only about anxious mood and motor restlessness and is not a diagnostic instrument. Significantly more anxiety and depression were found among the patients with dementia: 16% had definite anxiety and 22% possibleanxiety; 14% had definite depression and 14% possible depression. The investigators did not ex amine the correlation between depression and anxiety, so itis not known to what extent anxiety was associated with depression rather than dementia. There was no correlation between severity of dementia and anxiety scores, although the relative homogeneity of mild cognitive impairment may have accounted for this finding.**

#### *Alcohol Abuse and Dependence*

**General population data show a significant interrelationship between alcohol problems and anxiety (40, 41** *).* **It is interesting, however, that alcohol abuse or dependence in the elderly does not appear to increase the risk of anxiety disorders. Finlayson et al. (42) studied a group of 21 6 alcoholic patients 65 years of age or older and found a 0.5% rate of DSM-III anxiety disorders, which is in marked contrast to the high rates reported for younger adults and is also lower than the rates re ported for the nonalcoholic elderly population. Krystal et al. (43) examined the interactive effects of aging and alcoholism on 6-month prevalence rates of panic disorder in the ECA study and confirmed the increased prevalence of panic disorder among male drinkers aged 25-54 years and female drinkers aged 18-64 years. However, they found that the rate of panic disorder dedined earlier, more rapidly, and to a lower level in individuals with histories of alcoholism than in those without. Among individuals 65 years of age or older,**

**there were no cases of panic disorder in men or women with histories of alcohol abuse or dependence, com pared with a 0.2% rate in nonalcoholic elderly women. These data, albeit limited, suggest that in the elderly, in contrast to younger individuals, alcoholism does not in crease the rate of anxiety disorders and may in fact be** associated with a decreased prevalence.

## *Medical Illness*

**Given the frequency of medical illness in the elderly, there has been surprisingly little study of anxiety in older, physically unwell patients. In a cross-sectional study, Magni and De Leo (44) compared SCL-90 anxiety and depression factor scores of 1 78 general medical inpatients aged 60 years or more and 201 patients less than 60 years of age. The anxiety factor measures non phobic anxiety. The older group of subjects had a significantly higher mean score than the younger adults on the depression factor, while the reverse held true for the anxiety factor score. Thus,** *S* **1 % of the older group and 30% of the younger patients reported moderate to high levels of depression, but moderate to high levels of anxi**ety **were present in only 1 3 % of the elderly, compared with 40% of the younger adults. There were no differ ences in rates of anxiety between elderly men and women, but elderly female patients did have a higher mean depression score. Also, there were no differences among disease states for severity of anxiety, although central nervous system disease was associated with higher depression scores. Starkstein et al. (45) studied 225 stroke patients, many of whom were over the age of 60 years, and found that while only 2.6% of the patients met the DSM-III criteria for generalized anxiety disorder alone, one-half of the 20.9% of patients who had major depression also met the criteria for generalized anxiety disorder.**

**The prevalence rates of generalized anxiety disorder found in the elderly in these two studies are considerably lower than the 20%-62% prevalence ofgeneralized anxi**ety **disorder reported in studies of young or middle-aged patients with a variety of medical conditions (46).**

# **DISCUSSION**

#### *Methodological Issues*

**Prevalence rates for combined anxiety disorders, phobic disorders, and generalized anxiety disorder in the elderly vary considerably among studies. Rates for panic disorder and obsessive-compulsive disorder are more consistent. Several methodological factors, including case definition, hierarchical versus nonhierarchical approaches to diagnosis, and survey methods, may account for this variability.**

*Case definition.* **For anxiety disorders, case/noncase distinctions tend to be somewhat arbitrary, since anxi**ety **symptoms lie on a continuum with normal behavior. Differences between cases and noncases are there-** **fore quantitative. Changing threshold levels for "caseness" can have a profound effect on reports of prevalence rates. For example, in the studies of Copeland et a!.** *(5,* **6), if subcases at level 2 were added to cases at levels 3-5, prevalence rates of generalized anxiety disorder increased dramatically from approximately 1 % to 16% in each of the three sites surveyed. Similarly, in Liverpool phobias rose from 0.7% to 5.6%, and in London obsessive-compulsive disorder increased from 0.6% to 4.6%. The Guy's/Age Concern Survey (7), which also used structured interviews and validity criteria, found considerably higher rates of phobia and generalized anxiety disorder in London than did the study of Copeland et al. However, the Guy's survey used a CATEGO-derived index of definition of 4 or more to define a case. Traditionally, an index of definition of** *S* **or more implies that sufficient symptoms are present to make an lCD diagnosis, whereas an index of definition of 4 is the threshold between a case and a noncase. This may partly explain the difference in rates between these two stringently designed studies of the same geographic population. Finally, although Bergmann (8) did not use diagnostic or case criteria, only one-third of his anxiety-state group had moderate severity. If this were taken as a definition of "caseness," the prevalence of anxiety state in his study would drop to 6%, a figure more consistent with results of later surveys.**

**If the definition of a case includes the criterion that symptoms interfere with daily living, many elderly peopie with anxiety may not reach case level, since with aging their lifestyles may have become more restricted and housebound than when they were younger. Therefore, there may be less opportunity for anxiety symptoms to cause social dysfunction. This is especially so if the elderly person is institutionalized or is being well supported in the home by social services, thereby minimizing the need to leave a secure environment. A related issue has to do with the "appropriateness" of the anxiety. The Geriatric Mental State interview is only used to rate fears that the interviewer believes are "unreasonable" in the elderly population. This introduces a degree of subjectivity, which may lead to the underdiagnosis of phobias because of incorrect assumptions about what is or is not appropriate for normal aging (47).**

**"Caseness" will also be affected if the elderly person minimizes, explains away, or forgets to recall symptoms of anxiety. The elderly tend to emphasize somatic over psychological symptoms (48). Also, complaints of memory impairment secondary to anxiety may lead to an erroneous diagnosis of dementia, thereby missing the primary anxiety disorder diagnosis. The ECA lifetime prevalence rate ofcombined anxiety disorder diag noses was 7.4% for individuals 65 years ofage or older, compared to 1 1.2% for all age groups (2), suggesting that some past or present cases of anxiety in the elderly may have been missed. Difficulty in recall, decreased willingness to report symptoms, or misclassification may have accounted for this finding, although other possible explanations include disorder-associated mor-** tality and a historical increase in risk among the present **younger generation (49).**

*Hierarchical approach to diagnosis.* **Surveys using a hierarchical approach to diagnosis will likely yield lower prevalence rates for anxiety disorders than sur veys with nonhierarchical approaches. The AGECAT system** *(5)* **uses a hierarchy of diagnoses in which phobic and nonphobic anxiety are the two bottom levels of eight diagnostic clusters. Copeland et al.** *(5,* **6) noted that in their studies, very few persons with moderate to severe anxiety were free of other psychiatric disorders. The Guy's survey (7) found that over one-third of phobic patients also qualified for a diagnosis of depression and that when cases of depression were excluded, the prevalence of phobic disorders fell from 10% to 6%. Similarly, when Maule et a!. (9) excluded cases of depression and organic brain syndrome, the prevalence of "anxiety state" fell from 12.3% to 9.4%. Recently, Lindesay and Banerjee (47) compared three diagnostic systems in assessing phobias among a single group of eld**erly subjects. There was considerable variation in **1-month prevalence rates, ranging from 3.6% according to the hierarchical Geriatric Mental State interview to 25.6% according to the nonhierarchical Phobic Disorders Screen. In this study the prevalence rate decreased from 25.6% when nonhierachical DSM-III criteria were used to I 3.7% when hierarchical diagnostic rules were applied.**

*Survey methods.* **Differences in case definition and hierarchical diagnostic rules seem to account for much of the variance in prevalence rates between studies. However, factors related to survey methods should also be considered. First, the method of eliciting symptoms could be expected to have an impact on case detection. Lindesay and Banerjee (47) found that differences in the wording of questions, instructions to interviewers, and the list of fears presented to the respondents had an important bearing on the number of cases of phobia identified by the Geriatric Mental State interview/AGECAT package and the Phobic Disorders Screen. Conversely, the ECA (2) and Edmonton (3) studies both used the DIS and, despite significantly different sample sizes and different periods for determining prevalence, reported similar rates for anxiety disorders.**

**Second, most of the surveys did not examine institutionalized elderly persons. However, in the United States and the United Kingdom, only about** *5%* **of the elderly population reside in institutions. It is unlikely, therefore, that the exclusion of the institutionalized elderly would have a significant effect on overall prevalence rates. A possible exception to this assumption are the very. old, who are overrepresented in institutions. The only survey to compare the 65- to 74-year age group with persons aged 75 years or more (7) sampled households only and did not find differences in the rates of generalized anxiety disorder or phobias. Given that there are important physical, psychological, and social differences between the "young old" and the "old old," anxiety disorders in the institutionalized and frail elderly require more study.**

# *Late-Onset Versus Early-Onset Anxiety*

**The ECA incidence data (13) and the findings of Lindesay (1) suggest that the majority of cases of agoraphobia in old age are of recent onset. Given the significant asso ciation between phobic disorder and depression in late** life, it is possible that many cases oflate-onset agorapho**bia arise secondary to a depressive illness. However, re alistic fears may also result in some individuals being misdiagnosed as having late-onset agoraphobia. For ex ample, a frail elderly person who falls on the street may subsequently refuse to leave the house alone because of a valid fear of further falls and injury.**

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder is a chronic disorder that can persist into late life (30). It is interesting that very few cases of new-onset obsessive-compulsive disorder appear in elderly men, but the incidence increases slightly in elderly women. As already noted, the Edmonton study (3) found that the prevalence of obsessive-compulsive disorder among women in nursing homes was considerably higher than among those living at home. Given the increased incidence of dementia with age and the high rate of dementia in nursing home residents, some of the increased obsessive-compulsive disorder in elderly women may be due to obsessional symptoms arising secondary to the organic brain syndrome or a coarsening of obsessional personality traits associated with the dementia.**

**Since no late-life incidence data are available for generalized anxiety disorder or PTSD,** it is not known to **what extent these disorders arise for the first time in old age. However, in all reports of case series of PTSD in the elderly, it was mentioned that the disorder had started earlier in life. Also, comorbidity data (7, 17, 33, 34, 37) support the clinical observation that generalized anxiety arising for the first time in late life is usually associated with a depressive illness.**

#### *Comorbidity*

**Similar to other age groups, elderly subjects show considerable comorbidity of geriatric depression and generalized anxiety disorder and phobias. However, in contrast to younger adults, the depressed elderly have only a mildly increased rate of panic disorder. Also, medical illness and alcoholism in late life are associated with either no increase or only a modest increase in generalized anxiety.**

**These comorbidity data are consistent with the hypothesis that aging reduces the propensity for developing anxiety disorders. Given the life events that might be expected to increase the rate of anxiety in the elderly, it is possible that age-related biological factors reduce the older person's sensitivity to the effects of threatening events. It has been postulated that the pathophysiology of panic disorder and some cases of generalized anxiety disorder involves dysregulation of noradrenergic, serotonergic, and GABA-ergic neurotransmitter systems, leading to hyperactivity of the limbic system and brainstem (SO, 51). The locus ceruleus, which con-**

**tains nearly one-half of the brain's noradrenergic neurons and has numerous efferent and afferent con nections with other brain regions, is thought to play a significant role in modulating the emotional and autonomic manifestations of anxiety (SO, 51). With aging there is substantial cell loss in the locus ceruleus, and there are decreased brain levels of noradrenaline and increased levels of monoamine oxidase (43, 52). In addition, there are reductions of brain serotonin. These changes may be one factor, therefore, that accounts for the older person's decreased propensity for developing anxiety. Krystal et al. (43) have hypothesized that neurodegeneration beyond that of normal aging (for example, prolonged neurotoxicity from alcohol con sumption) may lead to further noradrenergic reduction and thereby explain the lower rate of panic disorder in elderly alcoholics. If this were so, less anxiety would also be expected in Alzheimer's disease, especially in advanced cases, where there is depletion of several neurotransmitters (52). The one study (39) that examined this question found, in fact, increased anxious mood in patients with early Alzheimer's disease, but the instrument that was used is not used for the diagnosis of panic disorder or generalized anxiety disorder. Therefore, further studies to test this hypothesis are needed.**

# **CONCLUSIONS**

**Despite a number of methodological factors influencing prevalence rates, the majority ofcommuniry surveys and comorbidity studies show that anxiety disorders are less common in the elderly than in persons of other ages. However, the different prevalence rates between** types **of anxiety disorders support the usefulness of subtyping anxiety in the elderly. Although any of the anxiety disorders may arise for the first time in old age in the context of another psychiatric or medical disorder, agoraphobia appears to be the only primary anxiety disorder that may begin to any significant degree in late life. As with younger patients, there is considerable comorbidity of geriatric depression and generalized anxiety disorder and phobias. Although in clinical ex perience the anxiety often arises secondary to the depression and resolves with successful treatment of the affective disorder, there is a need for longitudinal studies to clarify this issue. Of concern is the finding of corn munity studies that in patients with these mixed anxious-depressed states, the depressive illness often goes untreated or is inappropriately treated with benzodiazepines. There is obviously a need to educate physicians further about the benefit of definitive antidepressant therapy for these patients.**
