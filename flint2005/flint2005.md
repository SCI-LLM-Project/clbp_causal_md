**Abstract** Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is characterised by at least 6 months of excessive uncontrollable worry accompanied by symptoms of motor tension and vigilance and scanning. As with other anxiety disorders, GAD is less prevalent in older adults than younger adults. GAD has a high level of comorbidity with other psychiatric disorders and this has a bearing on estimates of its prevalence. GAD that is comorbid with another psychiatric disorder has a period prevalence of approximately 4% in community-dwelling older people. On the other hand, 'pure' GAD is less common, with a period prevalence of approximately 1%. Pure GAD in late life is a fairly even mix of chronic cases that began earlier in life and cases starting for the first time in later life.

The most frequent and consistent finding regarding late-life generalised anxiety is its high level of comorbidity with major depression. There are few longitudinal data pertaining to the temporal association of generalised anxiety and major depression in late life, but the data that do exist suggest that the anxiety is frequently symptomatic of the depression. If generalised anxiety occurs exclusively during episodes of major depression, a separate diagnosis of GAD is not warranted.

Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) is the most frequently studied psychological treatment for GAD. Although CBT is more effective than a wait-list control condition, it is not more effective than nondirective therapies in late-life GAD. Furthermore, a standard course of CBT appears to be less efficacious for GAD in older adults than younger adults. Further research is needed to develop more efficacious and specific forms of psychotherapy for late-life GAD.

The three classes of medications that are most commonly used for GAD are: (i) antidepressants; (ii) benzodiazepines; and (iii) buspirone. Antidepressant medication is the pharmacological treatment of choice for most older adults with generalised anxiety. When generalised anxiety is secondary to an episode of major depression, the selection of an antidepressant is guided by the same principles that apply to treatment of nonanxious depression. Antidepressant medication is also effective for GAD in the absence of an episode of major depression. In this situation, citalopram and venlafaxine have been found to be efficacious in older people. Data from studies of mixed-aged patients suggest that escitalopram, paroxetine and trazodone may also be beneficial in late-life GAD. Despite their widespread use in older persons with anxiety, benzodiazepines have a limited role in the treatment of GAD in the elderly. If a benzodiazepine is initiated, pharmacokinetic considerations favour the use of either lorazepam or oxazepam. Buspirone also has a more limited role than antidepressants in the treatment of late-life GAD.

# 1. Evolution of the Concept and Definition

For most of the 20<sup>th</sup> century, generalised anxiety was subsumed in the category of anxiety neurosis. Generalised anxiety was first defined as a distinct disorder in 1980, when the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 3<sup>rd</sup> Edition (DSM-III)<sup>[1]</sup> separated it from panic disorder. However, in DSM-III, generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) was primarily a residual disorder diagnosed by exclusion. The diagnosis could only be made if the symptoms were judged to be independent of another mental disorder. In DSM-III-R,<sup>[2]</sup> the diagnostic criteria for GAD were substantially revised, in part, because of the poor reliability of the DSM-III construct. For the first time, GAD had its own defining

characteristic: excessive or unrealistic worry unrelated to another axis I disorder. In addition, the associated symptom criteria were revised to require at least six of 18 symptoms from three clusters: motor tension, autonomic hyperactivity and vigilance and scanning. The minimum duration of symptoms was changed from 1 month to 6 months. Finally, the exclusion criteria were made more explicit, in that GAD could be diagnosed in the presence of another anxiety disorder, as long as the focus of worry was unrelated to it. However, given that generalised anxiety can be symptomatic of other mental and physical disorders, a diagnosis of GAD was not made if the disturbance occurred exclusively during the course of a mood or psychotic disorder, or was the result of an organic factor.

Interestingly, two studies that examined the prevalence of GAD across age strata in persons ≥65 years of age found an ongoing decline in prevalence between 65 and 85 years.[12,15] Findings of a lower prevalence of GAD in late life are consistent with a reduced prevalence of other anxiety disorders in older adults compared with younger adults.<sup>[8]</sup> Throughout the lifespan, GAD is more common in women than men.

There are no community-based data on the incidence of GAD in older adults. The Epidemiologic Catchment Area study is the only epidemiological study that has reported data on age of onset of GAD across the adult lifespan.[19] In that study, age of onset of 'pure' GAD (i.e. without comorbid major depressive disorder or panic disorder within the 12-month period of prevalence) was fairly evenly distributed until the age of 60 years. After that, there was a sharp decline in the number of new cases of GAD. Nevertheless, this study found that approximately half of the group ≥65 years of age reported a duration of pure GAD of 5 years or fewer. These data suggest, therefore, that pure GAD in late life is a fairly even mix of chronic illness that persists from earlier in life and cases starting for the first time in old age.

# 3. Comorbidity

# 3.1 Depression

In the general population, GAD has a high rate of comorbidity with other psychiatric disorders. For example, the National Comorbidity Survey, a large general population survey of persons aged 15 to 54

years in the non-institutionalised civilian population of the US, found that >90% of persons with a lifetime diagnosis of GAD also suffered from at least one other psychiatric disorder during their lifetime. The most frequent comorbid disorder is major depression, but a high level of comorbidity is also found with dysthymic disorder, other anxiety disorders, and in some studies, substance abuse. [6,7]

With one exception,<sup>[20]</sup> community-based epidemiological studies of older adults also reported a high level of comorbidity between GAD and other psychiatric disorders, most commonly depression.<sup>[11,12,14,15,17]</sup> In three of these surveys, between 60% and 90% of persons with GAD had concurrent comorbid case-level depression.<sup>[14,15,17]</sup> The British studies found that only 10–20% of cases of GAD were not concurrently comorbid with a depressive disorder or other anxiety disorder.<sup>[14,15]</sup> Similar findings have been reported in clinical populations and older people in long-term care facilities.<sup>[21-23]</sup>

There are few longitudinal data pertaining to the temporal relationship between GAD and major depression in late life, and the data that do exist are derived from selected populations. In these studies, the majority (55–80%) of new-onset cases of GAD occurred in the context of a new or established episode of major depression. [21-23] Furthermore, in a study of nursing home residents, recovery from GAD was closely correlated with recovery from major depression. [23] Interestingly, in a small study of older persons living in the community, 50% of 12 subjects with GAD at baseline had recovered following an average of 2 years of follow-up. [24] Eighty-three percent of these individuals also had major depression at baseline and the recovery rate of major depression was very similar to that of GAD.<sup>[24]</sup> These data, therefore, suggest a closer temporal association between GAD and major depression in older adults than has been found in younger adults.[7,25,26] In particular, many cases of late-onset generalised anxiety appear to be concurrent with episodes of major depression. GAD in late life is not simply a function of ageassociated changes that may be stressful, such as declining physical health or cognitive function, financial insecurity associated with retirement, widowhood or impending death. Older persons with GAD can be differentiated from nonanxious elderly by their worry and anxiety symptoms despite comparability in demographics, physical health and cognitive function. [48] Thus, differentiation of the pathological worry of GAD from 'appropriate' or realistic worry in late life, depends on the duration, frequency and intensity of the worry, the number of worries, the uncontrollability of the worry, and the distress and impairment associated with the worry and associated anxiety symptoms. There is no content of worry that is specific to GAD, although older adults with GAD may focus more on personal health, social and interpersonal matters, and finances than persons without GAD.<sup>[48]</sup> Not surprisingly, older people in general tend to worry less about work or school than younger individuals.[48]

As noted, GAD is frequently associated with other psychiatric conditions. Thus, evaluation of a person with generalised anxiety should include inquiry about symptoms of depression, panic disorder and phobic disorders. In addition, use of prescribed and over-the-counter medications, caffeine, alcohol and illicit drugs should be assessed.

## 6. Treatment

# 6.1 Cognitive Behaviour Therapy

Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) is the most frequently studied psychological treatment for GAD. [52] CBT for GAD usually includes the following components: education regarding GAD, breathing retraining and progressive muscle relaxation to target physical symptoms, cognitive restructuring to address the maladaptive worry of GAD, and *in vivo* or *in vitro* graded exposure so that the patient can practise coping skills in worrying situations. Among young and middle-aged patients, CBT that is specifically formulated for GAD has been found to be more effective than wait-list control conditions and nondirective supportive psychotherapy (reviewed

by Antony and Swinson<sup>[52]</sup>). Nevertheless, the rate of recovery from GAD following a course of CBT is modest, with approximately 50% of patients recovered at 6-month follow-up, and 30–40% recovered at 3–8 year follow-up.<sup>[53,54]</sup>

It is only recently that CBT has been investigated as a treatment for GAD in the elderly. Two studies found that 12-15 sessions of group-format CBT were more efficacious than a wait-list control condition in the treatment of this disorder in older adults.[55,56] Nevertheless, the rates of response in these studies were generally lower than those found in studies of younger subjects with GAD. Only 28-45% of subjects met criteria for response (defined as at least a 20% improvement on at least 75% of main outcome measures) and only 3-22% of subjects met criteria for high end-state functioning following the course of treatment.<sup>[55,56]</sup> In a small pilot study, Mohlman et al.[57] found that an enhanced CBT condition, in which subjects received individual CBT for GAD enhanced with learning and memory aids, resulted in a higher rate of response than that found in another study conducted by the same investigators using a standard course of CBT (75% vs 40%).

In contrast to some studies in younger individuals, CBT has not been found to be more effective than nondirective therapies in older persons with GAD. Wetherell et al.<sup>[56]</sup> found no difference in outcome between CBT and a discussion group, and Stanley et al.<sup>[58]</sup> found that about as twice as many GAD subjects responded to supportive therapy (54%) than to CBT (28%).

Overall, these findings suggest that CBT does not have the same efficacy or specificity in older persons with GAD compared with their younger counterparts. Some investigators have speculated that the group format, used in the majority of studies of CBT in late-life GAD, may have limited the treatment's efficacy.<sup>[55,56]</sup> However, response to standard individual-format CBT<sup>[57]</sup> was similar to that associated with group-format CBT<sup>[55,56]</sup> in the elderly. Moreover, a recent study of group CBT in younger persons with GAD found that rates of response and high end-state functioning were comparable with those

reported with individual CBT in younger individuals.<sup>[59]</sup> Nevertheless, further research is needed to determine whether modifications to CBT (such as trying a more individualised and interactive approach, along with enhancement of learning and memory aids) can improve the treatment's efficacy in older patients.

# 6.2 Pharmacotherapy

A variety of medications have been found to be more efficacious than placebo in the treatment of GAD (table II). [60-74] The three classes of drugs that are most commonly used in clinical practice are benzodiazepines, azapirones (namely buspirone) and antidepressants.

In contrast to the numerous treatment studies of GAD in younger adults, there has been only one prospective placebo-controlled study of the pharmacological treatment of GAD in older patients. [65] This 8-week trial found that citalogram was more effective than placebo in patients with DSM-IV GAD. Two other prospective studies found that buspirone and oxazepam were more effective than placebo in the treatment of older subjects diagnosed with 'anxiety state' or 'anxiety neurosis', diagnoses which approximate GAD.[75,76] Finally, a retrospective analysis of data pooled from five randomised, placebo-controlled trials found that venlafaxine extended-release (ER) was as effective and well tolerated in the treatment of older patients with GAD as in younger patients.[70] In the citalogram and venlafaxine studies, two-thirds of subjects were classified as responders following 8 weeks of treatment.<sup>[65,70]</sup> Of the subjects who continued to take venlafaxine ER and were available for assessment at 24-week follow-up, 88% were classified as responders,<sup>[70]</sup> suggesting the potential for a high rate of recovery amongst older individuals who persist with antidepressant medication.

In view of the limited data pertaining to the pharmacotherapy of GAD in older adults, many recommendations regarding the treatment of this condition in the elderly must be extrapolated from data obtained in younger individuals.

# 6.2.1 Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines are the most frequently prescribed medication for GAD in older adults.[15] Although benzodiazepines are efficacious for GAD, they are not the optimal first-line treatment in older persons. First, the frequent comorbidity of GAD and major depression in later life suggests the need for a medication with both antidepressant and anxiolytic effects. With the exception of alprazolam for the treatment of depression of mild-to-moderate severity, benzodiazepines are not an effective treatment for the core symptoms of depression.<sup>[77]</sup> On the other hand, antidepressant medications are an effective treatment for GAD, even when major depression is absent. The presence of even mild subsyndromal depressive symptoms may predict a better response of GAD to an antidepressant than a benzodiazepine. [67] Second, there are a number of risks of using benzodiazepines in the elderly, including cognitive impairment, psychomotor impairment, excessive daytime sedation, instability of gait, falls and hip fractures.<sup>[78]</sup> Finally, many patients with GAD require long-term treatment, but long-term use of benzodiazepines carries the risk of physical dependence,[78] which is not a risk with antidepressant drugs or buspirone.

Although benzodiazepines are overused as a primary treatment for GAD in older adults, they have a role in certain situations. ## 6.2.3 Antidepressants

The high level of comorbidity of GAD and depression, and the observation that late-onset generalised anxiety is frequently secondary to depression, suggest that antidepressant medication should be the pharmacological treatment of choice for many older adults with generalised anxiety. There is no consistent evidence that one class of antidepressant medication is better than another in treating depression that is complicated by a high level of generalised anxiety. In this situation, selection of an antidepressant is guided by the same principles that apply to the treatment of nonanxious depression. [84] However, it is important to be aware that a high level of anxiety may delay the response of major depression to antidepressant medication.

GAD in the absence of major depression is also responsive to antidepressant medication (table II). Data from studies of older patients support the use of citalopram and venlafaxine for the treatment of GAD in later life. [65,70] In addition, data from younger individuals indicate that escitalopram, paroxetine and trazodone are effective in treating GAD. [66-69] Although imipramine has efficacy in GAD in younger patients, [67] this tertiary amine tricyclic antidepressant is not recommended in the elderly because of its capacity to cause orthostatic hypotension, adverse cardiac events and significant anticholinergic effects. [84]

Of the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), citalopram and escitalopram are well suited to older persons. They have linear pharmacokinetics across the dose range (i.e. an increase in dose results in a predictable increase in plasma concentration), no clinically active metabolites, elimination half-lives of 33 hours or less and little propensity for drug-drug interactions. [86] Paroxetine has greater potential for drug-drug interactions and anticholinergic adverse effects than citalopram or escitalopram. [84] Paroxetine potently inhibits cytochrome P450 2D6,

a hepatic isoenzyme that metabolises a wide range of drugs, including class IC antiarrhythmics, opioids and several antidepressants, antipsychotics and βadrenoceptor antagonists.<sup>[84]</sup> In addition, paroxetine has nonlinear pharmacokinetics and, therefore, an increase in the dose of this drug can result in a disproportionate increase in its plasma concentration and, potentially, adverse effects. [84] If started at too high a dose, SSRIs may initially exacerbate symptoms of anxiety. Therefore, it is prudent to start at a low dosage of medication (citalogram or paroxetine 5-10 mg/day, or escitalopram 5 mg/day). Once it has been established that the patient is tolerating the starting dose (usually by the end of the first week of treatment), the dose should be gradually increased to the minimum target dose (table III). Because many patients will respond to the minimum target dose of an SSRI, it is recommended that the patient remain on that dose for 4-6 weeks before a decision is made about efficacy. If there has not been significant improvement in the patient's symptoms by that time, a gradual upward titration of dose is then indicated.

Venlafaxine is a serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, although it has been postulated that its noradrenergic effect occurs only at higher dosages (possibly 150 mg/day or higher). [84] The dosages used to treat GAD in older persons in randomised controlled trials were relatively low (75–150 mg/day in the majority of older subjects), [70] raising the possibility that the serotonergic action of the drug may be sufficient to treat GAD in many older patients. The aforementioned SSRIs or venlafaxine are suitable 'first-line' antidepressants for older persons with GAD. If GAD does not respond to a therapeutic trial of the first-line drug, switching to a different class of antidepressant (i.e. from an SSRI to venlafaxine or vice versa) is recommended.

Anxious patients frequently misattribute somatic symptoms of anxiety to the adverse effects of medication. Thus, in addition to starting with a low dose of antidepressant, adherence with medication can be enhanced by discussing potential adverse effects in advance, frequent monitoring of the patient during the first few weeks of treatment, and availability of the clinician by telephone between appointments to address any questions or concerns that the patient may have about treatment.

# 6.2.4 Other Medications

The antipsychotic drug trifluoperazine has been found to be effective in the treatment of GAD.<sup>[71]</sup> However, antipsychotic medications are not recommended for the treatment of GAD because their risks outweigh their benefits. Antipsychotic medication can be beneficial in the treatment of motor agitation which, at times, can be associated with generalised anxiety in patients with depression, dementia or a psychotic disorder.

Pregabalin (an antiepileptic), hydroxyzine (an antihistamine) and opipramol (a tricyclic iminostilbene derivative) have been found to be more effective than placebo in the treatment of GAD in younger patients.<sup>[72-74]</sup> There are no data on the efficacy or safety of these drugs in older adults with GAD and they are not currently recommended for the treatment of GAD in later life.

## 6.2.5 Duration of Pharmacotherapy

In young and middle-aged persons, especially those seeking treatment, GAD tends to be a chronic disorder with a low rate of spontaneous remission.[87] Thus, individuals who develop GAD earlier in life and grow old with the disorder will usually require lifelong treatment. On the other hand, generalised anxiety that starts for the first time in late life appears to have a higher rate of remission,[23,24] in large part because of its close temporal association with episodes of major depression. When generalised anxiety is symptomatic of major depression, the duration of treatment is primarily guided by the patient's depression history, [88,89] rather than the anxiety per se. The natural history of late-onset GAD that is not associated with major depression is unknown. In this situation, it would be prudent to continue treatment for at least 1 year following remission of symptoms.

### 7. Conclusion

The most frequent and consistent finding pertaining to generalised anxiety in late life is its cooccurrence with major depression. Although 'pure' GAD exists in late life, it is approximately one-third as prevalent as comorbid generalised anxiety. In approximately one-half of older persons with 'pure' GAD, the disorder developed earlier in the lifespan. Thus, if generalised anxiety starts for the first time after the age of 60 years, the diagnosis should be depression until proven otherwise. Given the considerable overlap of symptoms between DSM-IVdefined GAD and major depression, it is very important to inquire about the presence of anhedonia (low positive affect) and depressed mood which, by definition, differentiate major depression from GAD and are more reliable than worry (negative affect) in discriminating between the two disorders.<sup>[4]</sup>

Generalised anxiety that is confined to episodes of major depression is best regarded as a symptom of the depressive disorder and treated accordingly. In this situation, antidepressant medication, not a benzodiazepine, is the primary pharmacological treatment. Unfortunately, the current clinical reality is that benzodiazepines are prescribed more fre-

quently than antidepressants for anxious depression, [90] suggesting that the underlying depressive illness is missed in many of these cases. More education is needed to alert physicians, and other clinicians, to the fact that generalised anxiety is frequently a marker of depression in the elderly.

There is accumulating evidence that antidepressant medications are also effective for the treatment of GAD that is not associated with current major depression, although there are few data that are specific to the elderly. Chronicity of symptoms, which is common among older patients with pure GAD, should not deter attempts at treatment. Patients who have had symptoms for years, or even decades, can benefit from treatment. For example, the mean duration of GAD in older patients treated with venlafaxine ER was 10 years, yet two-thirds of these subjects met criteria for response by the end of 8 weeks of treatment. [70]

The chronicity of early-life-onset GAD that persists into late life suggests the need for lifelong treatment. Little is known about the natural history of pure GAD that starts for the first time in late life and there are no data regarding the optimal duration of treatment for these late-onset cases. However, it is prudent to continue treatment for at least 1 year in these individuals.

Further research is required to clarify the role of CBT in the treatment of GAD in late life. Because CBT is limited by the availability of specialists who are trained in the procedure, it will be important to determine whether CBT offers advantages over psychological treatments that require less training and expertise. The current, albeit scant, data do not support the specificity of CBT for late-life GAD. It remains to be determined whether modifications to the CBT procedure can improve its efficacy in older persons with GAD.
