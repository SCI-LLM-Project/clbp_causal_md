# Does pain-catastrophising mediate the effect of tai chi on treatment outcomes for people with low back pain?

Amanda M. Hall <sup>a</sup>,∗, Steven J. Kamper <sup>b</sup>,c, Richard Emsley d, Christopher G. Maher <sup>b</sup>

## Abstract

Objectives: There is evidence to support the effectiveness of tai chi for reducing pain-related symptoms across multiple musculoskeletal conditions, however, the mechanism(s) are unclear. This study explores the role of pain-catastrophising as a possible mediator in the relationship between practising tai chi and back pain symptoms.

Design: Exploratory mediation analyses using a Baron and Kenny approach and bootstrapping methods were employed as a secondary analysis of data from a previously published randomised controlled trial. Setting: Adults with persistent low back pain were recruited via community advertisement in Sydney, NSW, Australia.

Interventions: A 10-week tai chi intervention and a wait-list control.

Main outcome measures: Patients completed self-assessments of pain intensity and bothersomeness using 0–10 numerical rating scales, disability using the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire and pain catastrophising using the Pain Coping Strategies Questionnaire pre and post intervention.

Results: The results suggest partial mediation, by which a reduction in catastrophising explained approximately 1/3 of the effect on pain intensity and bothersomeness and 2/3 of the effect on disability. Conclusion: As the first known mediation analysis of tai chi for reducing low back pain-symptoms, it provides initial evidence that it may do so by having an effect on cognitive appraisal outcomes such as pain-catastrophising and information for choosing appropriate process measures for future studies. As tai chi is growing in popularity as an alternative treatmentfor patients with chronic musculoskeletal conditions, it is important to gain a better understanding of how it might work using confirmatory mediation analysis alongside future RCTs.

# **1. Introduction**

Tai chi is a gentle form of exercise that involves performing a sequence of physical movements combined with deep breathing; it originated in China and is practised to promote general health and well-being[.1](#page-4-0) In Western countries, including the UK, Australia and the United States,tai chi is used in some care facilities as a treatment for arthritis, in these countries its therapeutic use is endorsed by the respective Arthritis Foundations[.2–4](#page-4-0) The recent use of tai chi for health purposes has led to an increase in research investigating its health benefits. For example, in the last five years, there have been systematic reviews on the effectiveness of tai chi for reducing pain and disability in osteoporosis,[5](#page-4-0) and managing chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions[.6,7](#page-4-0) While the methodological quality of individual trials included in the reviews is varied, the consensus from the reviews is that tai chi provides small positive effects on several health outcomes including, pain, disability and depression, balance and self-efficacy.

Despite the increasing body of research assessing effectiveness, the potential mechanism(s) responsible for the effect of tai chi on musculoskeletal pain have not yet been investigated. Cognitive appraisal refers to a person's interpretation of a situation and can include catastrophising, fear avoidance, self-efficacy. Catastrophising is a term that was formally introduced to describe a mal-adaptive cognitive style employed by patients with anxiety and depressive disorders which included the concept of an irrationally negative forecast of future events[.10,11](#page-5-0) In particular, pain-related catastrophising has been broadly defined as a negative cognitive-affective response to an anticipated or actual pain.[12](#page-5-0) In a recent review, the association between higher levels of paincatastrophising and poor pain-related outcomes (i.e. pain intensity, disability and healthcare utilisation) was consistently reported.[13](#page-5-0) However, the magnitude of the relationship varied among studies. Practising tai chi includes concentration on completing a series of movements that are generally taught in a gradual progression in terms of difficulty and complexity (e.g. progressing from double leg stance moves to simple forward transitions to more complex multi-side transitions requiring single stance). Similar, to the fear avoidance model, it is thought that the practice of movements in this way allows the participant to perform a movement and not experience pain which may decrease the negative cognitiveaffective response to those movements.[14](#page-5-0)

Although, this pathway has not yet been investigated in tai chi, pain-catastrophising has been studied as a potential mediator of treatment outcome in two studies in patients with chronic low back pain (CLBP)[.15,16](#page-5-0) These studies were randomised controlled trials that investigated the effects of cognitive-behavioural treatment[,15,16](#page-5-0) and active physical therap[y15](#page-5-0) on pain and disability outcomes. In these studies, analyses indicated that the both cognitive and non-cognitively targeted interventions had an effect on pain catasrophising. It is of interest to determine if this pattern is the same in other interventions being used in this population.

Recently, a randomisedcontrolledtrial ofthe effects of a 10 week tai chi program for CLBP was conducted by two of our authors (AH, CM).[17](#page-5-0) In this study the tai chi intervention had a positive effect on self-reported pain-related symptoms. In order to explore a potential mediating factor that may act as a guide for future identification of potential mechanisms of action, a measure of catastrophising was collected. The measure of catastrophising was a part of a series of secondary outcomes, however, unfortunately part way through the RCT, it was found that the number of self-reported patient outcomes was becoming burdensome for patients and resulting in missing data, thus, the secondary non-clincial outcomes were removed from the assessmentfor the remainder ofthe trial[.17](#page-5-0) Thus, catastrophising was completed in a sub-sample of participants (the first 102 recruited participants) from the trial that allowed us to conduct an exploratory mediation analysis to study the longitudinal relationships between catastrophising and outcome.

This will be the first study to explore how practising tai chi improves pain symptoms using mediation analysis. The aim of this study is to investigate thehypothesis thatthe effect oftai chi onpain and disability was partially mediated by patient catastrophising.

## **2. Methods**

## 2.1. Data source

A randomised controlled trial oftai chi for people with CLBP was conducted in Sydney, NSW, Australia. The study received ethics approval form the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC Approval No. 10452). Recruitment of 160 community volunteers took place between August, 2008–June 2010. All participants provided data on outcomes of pain intensity, pain bothersomeness and pain-related disability. A sample of the first 102 participants in the study provided data on catastrophising. All variables were collected at two time points; at baseline (0–7 days before intervention) and at follow-up (within 7 days of completion of the 10 week intervention).

## 2.2. Participants

Participants were community volunteers with non-specific low back pain of greater than 3 months duration. Participants were recruited between August 2008 and May 2010 via local community advertisements in Sydney, New South Wales, Australia.

# 2.3. Intervention

The intervention was a 10-week tai chi program; whichincluded two 40 min classes per week for the first 8 weeks, and one 40 min class for the last 2 weeks. The tai chi instructor (AH) had completed instructor courses and has 4 years of previous teaching experience; to ensure a standardised level of training, the instructor completed a 1-week refresher course prior to the study. The intervention was delivered at various community venues in the Sydney Area, NSW Australia. The wait-list, control group were asked not to change their usual care during the 10 week study period and were offered participation in the tai chi program after their 10 week follow up.

## 2.4. Measures

## 2.4.1. Patient-reported outcomes

- (i) Pain bothersomeness; assessed as the average pain bothersomness during the past week with an 11-point numerical rating scale, where 0 is "not at all bothersome" and 10 is "extremely bothersome".[18](#page-5-0)
- (ii) Pain intensity; assessed as the average pain intensity during the past week with an 11-point numerical rating scale, where 0 is "no pain" and 10 is "worst pain ever".[18](#page-5-0)

(iii) Pain-related disability; assessed as the disability experienced today with the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) which is a 24-item Yes/No questionnaire with a possible total score of 0–24 with higher score representing greater disability.[19](#page-5-0)

# 2.4.2. Mediating variable

(i) Catastrophising was assessed with the Coping Strategies Questionnaire (CSQ) catastrophising subscale.[20](#page-5-0) This is a 6-item subscale with scores ranging from 0 (never do) to 6 (always do that when in pain). The items related to the past week. The total score is a sum of all 6 items with possible score ranging from 0 to 36.

# 2.5. Methodological quality of the datasource

The study was prospectively registered (ACTRN12608000270314) and followed a published protocol. Trial participants were assigned to two groups using a computerised random-number generator,the treatment codes were placed sequentially into sealed opaque envelopes for concealed allocation. The participants and treatment provider were un-blinded. The outcome assessments were all pre-validated instruments with good clinometric properties for the study population and were all self-reported by the participants. The original RCT had a follow-up rate of >90% on the clinical outcomes of pain and disability and analysis was conducted by intention to treat with point estimates and variability reported for all clinical outcomes. As stated previously, a mid-way data quality check indicated that there was missing data on several secondary outcomes and to reduce patient assessment burden, non-clinical outcomes were removed from the study assessment booklet. As such the current secondary analysis is comprised of the first 102 participants in the study.

# 2.6. Analysis

Mediation analysis was conducted via a series of linear regression models designed to assess the strength of the relationships between random allocation to the treatment/control arm, the mediator and outcome.[21](#page-5-0) First, we regress the outcome on random allocation; this is labelled the total effect. Second, we regress the mediator on random allocation; this is labelled the indirect effecta. Finally we regress the outcome on random allocation and the mediator; here, the effect of the mediator on outcome controlling for allocation was termed indirect effect<sup>b</sup> and the effect of random allocation on outcome (controlling for the mediator) was termed the direct effect. The product of the indirect effect<sup>a</sup> and indirect effect<sup>b</sup> was termed the indirect effect. As potential confounders ofthe mediator-outcome relationship,the baseline values of the mediator and outcomes were included as covariates in all three regression models. Bootstrapping was used to generate 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effect. The three models are presented graphically in [Fig.](#page-3-0) 2. The models only include complete cases, so that patients with either missing outcomes or mediator values are not included in the analysis; we indicate the numbers included in our results. This approach assumes that, conditional on the baseline covariates and randomisation, the missing outcomes and mediators are missing at random.[21](#page-5-0)

All analyses were conducted using Statistical Software for Social Sciences package (SPSS version 20) software. A bootstrapping approach involving 1000 resamples was performed using the INDI-RECT macro in SPSS 20, available at [http://www.afhayes.com/](http://www.afhayes.com/spsssas-and-mplus-macros-and-code.html) [spsssas-and-mplus-macros-and-code.html](http://www.afhayes.com/spsssas-and-mplus-macros-and-code.html)

## **3. Results**

## 3.1. Participants

Data for this study came from 102 community volunteers who had LBP for greater than 3 months. Description of the participants in terms of age, gender and baseline levels of pain intensity and pain-related disability is provided in Table 1. The data reveal a predominantly female sample most of whom had a history of previous low back pain episodes and entered the study with moderate levels or pain and disability.

## 3.2. Total effects of tai chi on treatment outcome

All outcomes were measured at follow-up immediately after the 10-week treatment. Effect sizes, standard error and p-values for the total effect are provided in [Table](#page-3-0) 2. Results are reported as unstandardised regression coefficients. The total effects showed better outcome on measures for the tai chi group and were all significant at the 5% significance level. The total effect of tai chi on the pain intensity was 0.96, indicating a mean difference of 0.96 points on the 0–10 numerical rating scale between the tai chi group and the wait-list group. The total effect of tai chi on pain bothersomeness was 1.13 points on the 0–10 scale and the total effect of tai chi on the 24-item RMDQ was 1.38 points.

## 3.3. Catastrophising as a mediator of treatment outcome

The indirect effect via catastrophising for pain intensity was 0.35 (95% CI 0.03 to 1.08), and the direct effect was 0.62. The indirect effect for pain bothersomeness was 0.43 (95% CI 0.05 to 1.11), and the direct effect was 0.70. The indirect effect for pain-related disability was 0.99 (95% CI 0.25 to 2.06), and the direct effect was 0.43 [\(Table](#page-3-0) 2). For all outcomes,the direct effect was lower than the total effect and not significant. These results indicate that a proportion of the effect oftai chi on pain intensity, bothersomeness and disability was due to a reduction in catastrophising.

# **4. Discussion**

We tested the extent to which our data were consistent with a theoretical model that aims to explain the effect of tai chi on pain-related symptoms by reducing catastrophising. The findings supported this model, indicating that a reduction in catastrophising explained approximately 1/3 ofthe effect oftai chi on pain intensity and pain bothersomness and 2/3 of the effect of tai chi on painrelated disability.

In these studies, analyses indicated that the both cognitive and non-cognitively targeted interventions had an effect on pain catastrophising. As far as we are aware this is the first study to provide empirical evidence showing that tai chi has an effect on catastrophising. The exact mechanisms for this effect are unclear, however, given that the tai chi intervention did not include specific cognitive strategies to target maladaptive beliefs, it can be theorised that the effect is likely to be behaviourally based. This finding is consistent with the study of Smeets et al[.15](#page-5-0) which demonstrated a treatment effect via catastrophising due to a purely physical treatment. This effect may be due to the tai chi instruction protocol which involved gradual progression in terms of difficulty and complexity of movements through the course of the intervention period. (e.g. progressing from double leg stance moves to simple forward transitions to more complex multi-side transitions requiring single stance). The tai chi components may be similar to strategies used in operant or exposure-based behavioural treatments based on the fear-avoidance model[.14](#page-5-0) Thus, the mediating effects of pain catastrophising observed here may not be specific to the particular movements or components used in the tai chi intervention but rather to interventions based on principles of graded exercise progression.

The fact that we found only partial mediation via reducing pain catastrophising suggests it is likely that other factors not investigated in this study are involved in explaining the effect of tai chi on pain. Other potential mediators could include variables that the intervention is likely to target. In this study, the tai chi intervention included components of physical movements and deep breathing techniques delivered using the principles of graded activity and exercise progression. For example, practising tai chi is thought to improve lower limb strength and flexibility via the consistent double-leg and single-leg squatting movements required throughout the activity. Improved lower limb function is thus postulated to improve physical activities associated back pain, such as getting in and out of a chair, climbing stairs and walking. Similarly, other cognitive appraisal related variables such as fear avoidance and self-efficacy may be involved. It is plausible that using principles of exercise progression would improve self-efficacy and decrease fear avoidance of physical activity. For example, since the development of the protocol for the original RCT in 2007, self-efficacy has been identified as a mediating factor of exercise and education interventions for pain.[23,24](#page-5-0) Moreover, tai chi was found to have positive effects on self-efficacy in an RCT of tai chi for osteoarthritis; however, the validity of this result is questionable due to small sample size and lack of a validated measurement tool for self-efficacy[.25](#page-5-0) It is possible that these other candidate variables have an equal or even greater mediating effect and investigation into variables likely targeted by tai chi is recommended.

## 4.1. Implications

Participants in the study had low levels of catastophising at study entry, an unsurprising finding given that they came from a non-care-seekingpopulation. Giventhat catastrophising was found to mediate outcome despite this, it may be that this mechanism of action is of even greater importance in populations with higher levels of catastrophising. Following this line of inquiry, it would be of interest to investigate whether level of catastrophising is an effect modifier for tai chi interventions. This information would facilitate better targeting of treatment. Of interest, the trial participants were quite typical of people seeking care for chronic LBP with the exception of gender. While low back pain is more common in women, we would not expect it to be this unbalanced. As the original RCT recruited volunteers via community advertisements, we suspect that it may be possible that a group tai chi class may be more acceptable as an intervention for women with low back pain.

# 4.2. Strengths and limitations

The data in this study come from a subsample of patients in a RCT that was conducted with attention to important methodological considerations such as following an apriori published protocol, using concealed allocation, using validated measurement instruments. In this way, we have tried to minimise bias and improve the internal validity of the findings. However, we note several limitations with respect to the methodological quality of the original RCT including thatthe treatment provider and the participants were not blinded, since the outcomes were patient-reported the outcome assessor would also be considered unblinded. Additionally, including only a subsample ofthe the larger trial data, albeit a consecutive sample, may impact on the representativeness of the study sample.

The use of an experimental design also facilitates interpretation of the mediation analysis as it rules out the possibility of reverse causation between the mediator and random allocation. Even with experimental designs, investigators cannot rule out the possibility of unmeasured confounding between the mediator and outcome, which can bias any resulting mediation analysis[.21](#page-5-0) We attempt to control for measured confounding by including baseline measures of the mediators and outcomes in our models, but we cannot rule out the presence of other unmeasured variables accounting for an association between catastrophising and the outcomes.

The sample used in the analysis was a subsample of the total number included in the RCT; it has been recommended that a sample of at least 200 is necessary to calculate a reliable estimate of mediated pathways. Indeed, observation of the confidence intervals around the indirect effects in this study show considerable imprecision in the estimates. Larger samples are also needed to generate stable estimates of the proportion mediated [\(Table](#page-3-0) 2), as such these findings should be interpreted with circumspection.

Finally, this is an exploratory study and our interpretation of the results is severely limited by the timing of the measurements for both the mediator and the outcome. Both were measured at the same time point and thus the direction of the relationship between catastrophising and outcome is unclear. Although a difficult issue, it may be possible through regular, repeated assessment of the mediator and outcome over time that this relationship could be modelled and better understood.[26](#page-5-0) While this study cannot draw conclusion regarding exact mechanism, it does provide evidence to support the hypothesis that should be confirmed in a more robust design with a larger sample.

# **5. Conclusion**

Tai chi is a gentle form of exercise that has empirical support attesting to its benefit and has been recommended for therapeutic use in people with chronic pain conditions. It appears that part of the benefit of tai chi is due to its effect reducing catastrophising. Thus, for clinicians treating patients with CLBP, tai chi may be a viable option for their patients who present with symptoms of catastrophising. Further research in populations with higher mean levels of catastrophising and into other mechanisms would improve our ability to better identify the best targets for tai chi programs.
