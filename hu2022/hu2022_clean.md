# Association of mental health with the risk of coronary artery disease in patients with diabetes: A mendelian randomization study

## KEYWORDS

Coronary artery disease; Diabetes; Depression; Anxiety; Neuroticism; Mendelian randomization

Abstract Background and aims: Observational studies have shown an association between mental health and coronary artery disease (CAD) in patients with diabetes. Nevertheless, whether these associations are causal is still unknown. In this two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study, we aimed to assess the causality between mental health and CAD in patients with diabetes. Methods and results: Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with: depression (807,553 individuals), anxiety (83,556 individuals) and neuroticism (329,821 individuals) were identified from the largest genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Summary-level data for CAD were extracted from the recently published GWAS of 15,666 diabetic patients (3968 CAD cases and 11,696 controls). The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used for the main analysis. Sensitivity analyses included weighted median, maximum likelihood, and the MR-Egger method. Genetic liability to depression was significantly associated with a higher risk of CAD in patients with diabetes (odds ratio [OR], 1.286; 95%CI,1.018e1.621;p Z 0.035). For anxiety and neuroticism, no causal association with CAD in patients with diabetes was observed. Consistent results were obtained in most sensitivity analyses.

Conclusions: This MR study provides genetic evidence that depression is a potential risk factor for CAD in patients with diabetes. However, anxiety and neuroticism were not causally associated with CAD in patients with diabetes. Mental health treatments should be enhanced to prevent CAD in patients with diabetes.

# Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. The American Heart Association reported that nearly 18.2 million Americans suffered from CAD from 2013 to 2016 [\[1\]](#page-5-0). Patients with diabetes mellitus have shown a higher predisposition for CAD occurrence [[2](#page-5-1)]. Furthermore, the 2019 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines highlighted the importance of preventing CAD in patients with diabetes [[3\]](#page-5-2). Observational studies had previously suggested that several mental disorders were associated with CAD in patients with diabetes [[4](#page-5-3)e[6\]](#page-5-3).

For instance, an observational study based on 1,380,433 participants suggested that depression was associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction in patients with diabetes (hazard ratio [HR] 1.82; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.69e1.97) [[7](#page-5-4)]. However, there was no association when only participants with more than 15 years followup were involved in the analyses [[8\]](#page-5-5). A meta-analysis of 20 prospective studies suggested that anxiety seemed to be an independent risk factor for CHD (relative risks [RR], 1.26; 95% CI, 1.15e1.38) [\[9](#page-5-6)], but in some cases, anxiety might be a protective factor [\[10](#page-5-7)[,11](#page-5-8)]. Besides, anxiety was shown to be related to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) among people with diabetes [[12\]](#page-5-9). Neuroticism commonly refers to the tendency to experience negative emotions [\[13](#page-5-10)]. A Swedish populationbased cohort study reported that neuroticism was associated with an increased risk of CAD (OR 1.2; 95%CI 1.1e1.3) [\[14](#page-6-0)]. To sum up, observational studies suggested that mental health was closely related to CAD in diabetes; however, the causal relationship remained unclear due to confounding factors (e.g., body mass index [BMI], blood pressure and lifestyle traits).

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to assess the causality between exposure and outcome [[15\]](#page-6-1). Genetic variants are randomly distributed at conception, thereby not affected by environmental factors [[16\]](#page-6-2). Therefore, it effectively overcomes the interference of confounding factors and reverses causality in observational research [[17](#page-6-3)]. Recently, a two-sample MR study suggested depression was associated with increased CAD risk. [[18\]](#page-6-4), and another MR study showed that neuroticism was casually associated with a higher risk of atrial fibrillation [[19\]](#page-6-5). However, no MR studies have explored the causality between mental health and CAD in patients with diabetes.

In this study, we carried out a two-sample MR analysis to assess the causality of genetic liability between several types of mental disorders (depression, anxiety, and neuroticism) and CAD in patients with diabetes using the GWAS database.

## Methods

## Study design

The design of our two-sample MR is displayed in [Fig. 1.](#page-2-0) Briefly, instrumental variables for mental disorders (depression, anxiety, and neuroticism) were used to examine the association with CAD in patients with diabetes. In general, there were three core assumptions: (1) The selected genetic variants should be significantly associated with exposure factor (p < 5 108); (2) The selected genetic variants should not be related to any possible confounding factors; (3) The genetic variants should exert effects on the outcome only through the exposure [[20](#page-6-6)]. All original studies have obtained informed consent from the participants and were approved by a relevant ethical review board.

## Data sources and Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) selection

Instrumental variables (IVs) for depression were available from the hitherto largest GWAS meta-analysis of 807,553 European-descent individuals (246,363 cases and 561,190 controls) [\[21\]](#page-6-7). 102 SNPs were identified from this GWAS, of which 96 reached genome-wide significance. For anxiety, we used the largest GWAS, including 126,443 individuals of Western European ancestry, of which 5 new genome-wide significant lead SNPs were identified [[22\]](#page-6-8). Data for neuroticism were obtained from the GWAS of 329,821 UK Biobank participants, and 116 significant independent loci associated with neuroticism were extracted from GWAS [[23\]](#page-6-9).

Summary-level data for CAD in patients with diabetes were extracted from the GWAS performed by the UK biobank, including 15,666 individuals (3968 CAD cases and 11,698 controls) of white British ancestry with diabetes [[24\]](#page-6-10).

We performed the linkage disequilibrium (LD) test using the two-sample MR package based on the 1000 genomes LD reference panel (EUR population) to choose valid instrumental SNPs [[25](#page-6-11)]. As such, rs7624336, rs7659414, rs67436663 for depression and rs10097870, rs70055884, rs2532386 for neuroticism were removed from the MR study. Finally, 96 SNPs for depression, 5 SNPs for anxiety, 82 SNPs for neuroticism remained as IVs.

Matched SNPs were queried in the GWAS outcome. If SNPs were unavailable in the outcome, datasets were replaced by proxy SNPs (r2> 0.9) in the European population by searching on an online website ([https://ldlink.nci.](https://ldlink.nci.nih.gov/?tab=ldproxy) [nih.gov/?tab](https://ldlink.nci.nih.gov/?tab=ldproxy)Zldproxy). The F statistic value evaluated the strength of each SNP [\[26](#page-6-12)]. An F statistic >10 indicated that it was strong enough as a valid instrument. Detailed information of the data sources about mental disorders and CAD in patients with diabetes are shown in [Table 1.](#page-2-1)

#### Statistical analysis

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) was used as the main statistical analysis to evaluate the influence of genetically predicted mental disorders on the risk of CAD in diabetic patients [[27\]](#page-6-13). In brief, we used the Wald estimator and standard error with the Delta method to obtain the effect estimates. Then, these estimates were combined in fixed-effects IVW meta-analysis to obtain the overall effect value [\[28\]](#page-6-14). The detailed description of IVW was shown in the Supplementary. Furthermore, simple median, weighted median, maximum likelihood, and MR-Egger regression approaches were conducted as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the results. The weighted median method could provide consistent estimates on causal effects when at least 50% of the weight was from valid variants [[29\]](#page-6-15). The MR-Egger method was used to detect and correct directional pleiotropy [[30\]](#page-6-16). In addition, several pleiotropy assessments were conducted to investigate the robustness of the results. Firstly, MR-Egger intercept test was applied to access the potential

## Results

## Depression

Among the 96 depression-related SNPs, six were not available in the dataset of CAD with diabetes. Suitable proxy SNPs replaced five unavailable SNPs. Therefore, 95 SNPs were used as instrumental variables to analyze CAD in depressive patients with diabetes (table S1). In the IVW analyses, genetic liability to depression was significantly associated with a higher risk of CAD in diabetic patients (OR, 1.286; 95%CI,1.018e1.621,p Z 0.035; [Fig. 2](#page-3-0)). This association was consistent in the maximum likelihood and weighted median analysis [\(Fig. 2\)](#page-3-0). In addition, the leaveone-out method found that deletion of rs1568452, rs7932640, rs2043539 and rs1343605 had a moderate influence on the causality between depression and CAD in patients with diabetes (figure S1). MR-Egger regression intercept showed no potential horizontal pleiotropy (p > 0.05; [Table 2](#page-3-1)). Similarly, scatter plots also confirmed no directional pleiotropy for CAD in patients with diabetes ([Fig. 3\)](#page-4-0).

## Anxiety

Five SNPs related to anxiety were identified as instrumental variables (table S2). Genetically predicted anxiety was not causally associated with CAD patients with diabetes (OR, 1.005; 95% CI, 0.788e1.282, p Z 0.962; [Fig. 2\)](#page-3-0) in the standard IVW method. The MR estimates of anxiety were robust and consistent in sensitivity analyses, including weighted median and maximum likelihood ([Fig. 2](#page-3-0)). The intercept test from MR-Egger showed no indication of horizontal pleiotropy (p > 0.05; [Table 2\)](#page-3-1). In addition, the leave-one-out analysis and scatter plots showed that the causal relationship was not driven by any single SNP (figure S2, 3).

## Discussion

In the present study, we applied a two-sample MR method to explore the causal relationship of depression, anxiety, and neuroticism in CAD patients with concomitant diabetes. Our analyses demonstrated that genetic liability to depression in diabetic patients had a significant positive relationship with CAD. However, there was no evidence indicating that genetically predicted anxiety and neuroticism were causally related to CAD risk in patients with diabetes.

Recently, the Nationwide Population-Based cohort study involving 2,668,615 participants with 8 years followup suggested that depression in patients with diabetes was associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (HR, 1.38; 95%CI, 1.29e1.49) [[32](#page-6-18)], which is in line with our findings. Similarly, another prospective cohort study claimed that patients with depression and diabetes had a significantly increased risk of myocardial infarction (HR,1.82; 95%CI, 1.69e1.97) compared with the control group [[7](#page-5-4)]. However, evidence from a meta-analysis of 4 prospective cohort studies suggested no association between depression and CAD when included individuals were followed up for more than 15 years [[8\]](#page-5-5). This interesting phenomenon might be due to the cure or relief of depressive symptoms during follow-up. With the recovery of depression, the association between depression and CHD risk might weaken. Our MR study further reveals the long-term and stable effects of depression on the risk of CAD in patients with diabetes, thereby confirming the consequences of observational studies.

Several possible mechanisms could explain the causal effect of depression on CAD in patients with diabetes. In depression, the secretion of the corticotropin-releasing hormone is increased, glucocorticoids are overproduced, the sensitivity of glucocorticoid receptor is decreased, and the hypothalamicepituitaryeadrenal axis negative feedback mechanism is impaired [\[33](#page-6-19)]. Previous studies have shown that behavioral changes (for example, irregular diet and treatment nonadherence) accompanied by depression could worsen blood sugar control and lead to insulin resistance [\[34](#page-6-20)[,35\]](#page-6-21). In addition, a series of biological abnormalities linked with depression, including disorder of neuroendocrine systems, autonomic neuropathy and alterations in glucose transport function, could result in visceral adiposity, elevated plasma triglyceride and

The genetic responsibility of depression is associated with CAD risk factors such as endothelial dysfunction [\[37](#page-6-23)], hypertension [[38\]](#page-6-24), and obesity [[39\]](#page-6-25). A recent MR analysis found a causal association between depression and CAD in the general population [\[18\]](#page-6-4). Our MR analysis using the summarized GWAS dataset yielded genetic evidence of the causality between depression and CAD in patients with diabetes.

Previous studies reported controversial results on the relationship between anxiety and CAD. A meta-analysis including 44 prospective studies with 30,527 individuals with CAD indicated that anxiety was associated with the risk of death in CAD patients (OR, 1.21, 95% CI, 1.06e1.39) [[40\]](#page-6-26). Additionally, another meta-analysis study involving 249,846 participants with an average follow-up of 11.2 years found that anxious people had a significantly increased risk of CAD (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.15e1.38) [\[9](#page-5-6)]. However, a prospective study of survival after coronary stent implantation indicated that patients with anxiety had fewer major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) after stenting (HR Z 0.34, 95%CI: 0.14e0.80, p Z 0.014) [[10](#page-5-7)]. This may be because anxious patients seek more medical help and adhere to secondary prevention. Importantly, there is almost no literature regarding the relationship between anxiety and CAD in diabetic patients. This may be due to the fact that there was no causal relationship between anxiety and CAD in patients with diabetes, as per our MR results.

Neuroticism is a relatively stable personality trait characterized by negative emotionality, including selfconsciousness, anger, emotional instability and irritability [[41](#page-6-27)]. In the past few decades, observational studies suggested that neuroticism might be a risk factor for CAD. A cohort study of a Swedish population found that high neuroticism was associated with an increased risk of CAD (OR1.2, 95%CI 1.1e1.3) [[14\]](#page-6-0). Similarly, in a 21-year prospective cohort study, researchers found that after controlling for multiple risk factors, neuroticism was still associated with increased CAD mortality (HR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.03e1.21) [\[6](#page-5-11)]. This study also pointed out that neuroticism might mediate death from cardiovascular disease through health behavior, socio-demographics, and physiological factors. However, in a prospective study that included 308,721 participants that were followed up for an average of 6.2 years, there was no evidence to prove the association between neuroticism and CAD mortality [[42\]](#page-6-28). In addition, neuroticism might be a protective factor for CAD in some cases. The UK Health and Lifestyle Survey found that women of high socioeconomic status with high neuroticism were significantly associated with a decreased risk of CVD mortality (HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.38e0.97) [[43](#page-6-29)]. Our MR analyses found that neuroticism was unlikely to be a risk factor for CAD in patients with diabetes. Thus, the positive results obtained in previous observational studies might be due to the interference of confounding factors. (i.e., BMI, smoking and diet).

GWAS involves the testing of genetic variants among the genomes of multiple individuals to identify genotypeephenotype associations [[44](#page-6-30)]. In the past decade, GWAS has revolutionized the field of complex disease genetics, yielding abundant convincing associations for human complex traits and diseases [\[45](#page-6-31)]. For diabetes, GWAS has generated new insights into biological mechanisms that have accelerated clinical translation and started to show good prospects in population risk stratification [[46\]](#page-6-32). Furthermore, GWAS could advance personalized medicine in diabetes, such as risk prediction and optimization of therapies based on the genotype [\[47](#page-6-33)].

In this study, we used the largest GWAS meta-analysis to explore the association between mental disorders (depression, anxiety, and neuroticism) and CAD risk in patients with diabetes through MR analyses. Our research included several significant advantages. First of all, twosample MR analyses were applied to evaluate the causal relationship between three mental characteristics and CAD in patients with diabetes. We used randomly assigned genetic variation as instrumental variables to minimize the confounding factors and reverse causality in observational studies. Secondly, summary-level data were extracted from the largest GWAS dataset, depression (807,553), anxiety (83,556) and neuroticism (329,821). The large sample size effectively improved the accuracy of SNPs selection and the statistical effectiveness of our analyses. Finally, our conclusion was drawn through comprehensive 708 T. Hu et al.

analyses, which included several reliable MR methods and sensitivity analysis to avoid the potential of pleiotropy. Nevertheless, there were still several limitations in our research. First, the population included from the GWAS dataset was primarily of European ancestry, which might not be suitable to represent all populations. Secondly, GWAS could not identify all genetic determinants of complex traits and did not necessarily pinpoint causal variants and genes [\[48,](#page-6-34)[49](#page-6-35)]. In addition, the small effect sizes of SNPs identified by GWAS study only explained the modest proportion of heritability limits their clinical predictive value [\[50,](#page-6-36)[51](#page-6-37)]. Thirdly, in our study, there was pleiotropy among a few SNPs in our analyses; nevertheless, we conducted several sensitivity analyses to reduce the influence of pleiotropic bias. Finally, due to the lack of patient-level data, such as gender and social status, we could not investigate the social status or gender-specific causality between the three mental disorders and CAD in patients with diabetes. For instance, it was previously found that women with low socioeconomic status were more sensitive to depression than men [[52](#page-6-38)].

# Conclusion

In summary, our two-sample MR study showed that genetic liability to depression had a risk effect on CAD in patients with diabetes. However, there was no evidence to support the causal relationship across anxiety, neuroticism, and the risk of CAD in patients with diabetes. Importantly, early interventions on depression could effectively reduce the risk of CAD in patients with diabetes clinically.
