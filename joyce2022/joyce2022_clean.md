## Abstract

**OBJECTIVE:** To investigate whether indirect effects via psychological mechanisms explain the effects of physical therapy (PT) or yoga versus education on back-related outcomes.

**DESIGN:** Mediation analyses using data from a randomized controlled trial of PT, yoga, and education interventions for chronic low back pain.

**METHODS:** Primary outcomes were changes in back-related pain on the 11-point numeric rating scale and disability on the modified 23-point Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire, measured at 52 weeks after randomization. Hypothesized mediators were 12-week changes in pain self-efficacy, fear-avoidance beliefs, depression, anxiety, perceived stress, and sleep quality.

We used causal mediation analysis to estimate the total effect, direct effect, indirect effect, and proportion mediated.

**RESULTS:** We analyzed data from 230 adults (mean age = 46.2 years, 69.6% female, 79.6% non-White). In the PT-versus-education model, when the mediator was perceived stress, the total effect on disability was 2.6 points (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.3, 4.9) and decomposed into a direct effect of 1.7 points (95% CI: −0.4, 3.8) and an indirect effect 0.9 points (95% CI: 0.1, 2.0; proportion mediated, 34%). No other psychological construct was a significant mediator.

**CONCLUSION:** Improvements in perceived stress mediated improvements in disability after PT treatment compared to education. Other psychological outcomes did not mediate the effect of yoga or PT on pain or disability outcomes compared to education.

#### **Keywords**

biopsychosocial; chronic pain; mechanism; mediation; physical therapy; yoga

The biopsychosocial model guides research and treatment of chronic low back pain (LBP). The severity of chronic LBP is related to psychological distress, such as anxiety or depression, and maladaptive cognitive appraisal of pain, such as pain self-efficacy, fearavoidance beliefs, and catastrophizing.10,14,18,35 Increasingly, psychological interventions like mindfulness or cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) are incorporated into treatments aimed at reducing disability and pain.1,9 It is unclear whether standard evidence-based treatments for chronic LBP, such as physical therapy (PT) or yoga, improve disability directly or through psychological factors.

Causal mediation analyses can help explain how a treatment works by decomposing the total effect of a treatment (ie, total effect) into the indirect effect and the direct effect.19,36 The indirect effect is the effect of the treatment on the outcome that goes through a specific intermediate variable called a mediator. Mediators represent a potential underlying mechanism of treatment effect. The direct effect is the remaining effect of treatment occurring through all other mechanisms. In this paper, we will assess whether psychological mediators, such as increased pain self-efficacy or reduced anxiety, explain the effect of PT or yoga versus education on disability and pain.

Rehabilitation treatments that incorporate psychological interventions (eg, CBT,<sup>12</sup> psychologically informed PT,<sup>9</sup> and cognitive functional therapy23) reduce disability. Effects of these interventions are partially explained by a reduction in fear-avoidance beliefs,12 reduced pain distress,20 and improved self-efficacy.12,23 However, whether standard evidence-based approaches to PT (eg, graded exercise, patient education, aerobic conditioning) are also mediated by improvements in psychological factors remains unclear.

Common mind-body interventions, such as yoga or tai chi, involve practices to alleviate psychological distress (eg, rhythmic breathing, mindfulness, and meditation).31 Mind-body treatments improve catastrophizing,15 pain self-efficacy,32 and back-related disability. While it is feasible that PT and yoga may have shared psychological mechanisms, we are unaware of previous studies that compare the mechanisms of these interventions.

We performed a secondary analysis of the Back to Health Study, a 52-week randomized controlled trial of PT, yoga, and education for adults with chronic LBP from underserved communities of Boston, Massachusetts. At 52 weeks, PT improved pain, and both PT and yoga improved disability compared to education.29 PT and yoga participants also experienced small-to-modest improvements in sleep quality,28 perceived stress,<sup>3</sup> depression and anxiety,17 pain self-efficacy, and catastrophizing21 over the 52-week period. Here, we investigate whether the benefits of PT or yoga compared to education on 52-week disability and pain are mediated by improvements in psychological factors at 12 weeks.

## **METHODS**

#### **Design and Participants**

A detailed description of the trial and results are published elsewhere.29,30 Briefly, this single-blinded (assessor) trial was conducted in a large safety-net hospital and 7 affiliated community health centers in low-income, racially diverse neighborhoods of Boston, Massachusetts. From June 2012 to November 2013, 320 adults (ages 18–64 years) with chronic (>12 weeks) LBP that was rated 4 or greater on an 11-point numeric rating scale were recruited. Of the 320 trial participants, we excluded those missing either outcome (n = 64) or any of the mediator (n = 26) scores for our final sample size of 230 participants. The Boston University Medical Campus Institutional Review Board approved the study before data collection, and all participants provided informed consent. We followed the Guideline for Reporting Mediation Analysis.<sup>5</sup>

#### **Interventions**

**Physical Therapy—**The PT intervention used a treatment-based classification system<sup>13</sup> to guide the selection of a program of graded exercise during 15 visits. Each PT session included a 30-minute one-on-one treatment with the physical therapist who gradually progressed the participant through an individualized program. This was followed by a 30 minute aerobic exercise routine. Additionally, PT participants who scored high on intake on the Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire (FABQ) (≥29 on the work subscale) were given a copy of The Back Book, <sup>26</sup> a brief resource that addresses the psychological influences on chronic LBP. Physical therapists reinforced the main points of the book.

**Yoga—**A yoga intervention for back pain was delivered in small groups by hatha yoga instructors.29 These sessions consisted of a relaxation exercise (savasana), breathing sequences (pranayama), and yoga poses (asanas). The 12-week treatment period was divided into 43-week segments, each with 15 to 19 poses tailored to the specific abilities of each participant.

**Education—**The education intervention was a copy of The Back Pain Helpbook, <sup>22</sup> a comprehensive resource that teaches stretching, strengthening, and emotional management techniques for people with chronic LBP. Every 3 weeks, participants also received a newsletter summarizing main points from the assigned chapters and a 5- to 10-minute check-in phone call from the research staff.

After 12 weeks, participants who attended at least 1 PT or yoga session were included in a 40-week maintenance phase. PT participants received either 5 "booster sessions" or home practice only. Yoga participants received either a weekly drop-in yoga class or home practice only. All education participants were contacted every 6 weeks to encourage continued review of the book. The original study found no difference in primary outcomes between the re-randomized subgroups; therefore, we collapsed subgroups within each treatment arm and compared the treatments.

#### **Measurement**

**Outcome Measures—**Our primary outcomes were change in pain from baseline to 52 weeks, rated on an 11-point numeric rating scale,24 and change in back-related disability from baseline to 52 weeks, measured on the modified 23-point Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ).25 We calculated change scores by subtracting 52-week scores from baseline scores. Positive scores indicate improvement.

**Mediators—**We used measures of the mediator variables collected at week 12. Our hypothesized mediators were 12-week changes from baseline scores of pain self-efficacy (Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire; 0–60), fear-avoidance beliefs (FABQ work subscale; 0–42), depressive symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire-8; 0–24), anxiety symptoms (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7; 0–21), perceived stress (Perceived Stress Scale-10; 0–40), and sleep quality (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index; 0–21). We calculated change scores by subtracting 12-week scores from baseline scores. Positive scores indicate an improvement for all mediators except for pain self-efficacy: a negative score indicates improvement. Each measure is common in research and clinical practice and has acceptable measurement properties.4–8,10,11,33

#### **Analysis**

The hypothesized causal structure is displayed in FIGURE 1, where an arrow represents the direction of effect between 2 variables. We conducted a causal mediation analysis with a counterfactual framework to decompose the total effect into the natural direct and indirect effects.16 In our study, the natural direct effect would compare the mean of the outcome for participants in the treatment group (eg, yoga) with the mean for those in the control group (ie, education) if their mediator had taken the value that would have been observed if they had been in the control group. The natural indirect effect would compare the mean of the outcome under treatment (eg, yoga) to the mean of the outcome under treatment had the mediator taken the value it would take under the control group. The proportion mediated was computed as the ratio of the indirect effect over the total effect and is interpreted as the proportion of the total effect explained by the path through the mediator.

Estimation of the indirect and direct effects from data relies on the assumptions of no unmeasured confounding between intervention and mediator and between mediator and outcome, as well as no confounders between the mediator and outcome that are affected by the treatment. Potential confounders were baseline measurements of age, sex, and race/ ethnicity as well as baseline pain, disability, and the respective mediator for that analysis. Because the treatment is randomized, no treatment-mediator confounding is expected.

We separately examined 6 mediator pathways for each intervention comparison, PT vs education or yoga vs education, for both outcomes, pain or disability, using the simulation approach by Imai et al.16 The approach simulates counterfactual mediator and outcome values based on mediator and outcome models. The reliance of the simulation approach might result in minor variability of total effect estimates among mediators for an intervention comparison. The mediator model regresses the mediator against the intervention. The outcome model regresses the outcome against the mediator and the intervention. Both models were adjusted for the potential confounders. All confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated with 2000 parametric bootstrap samples.

Additionally, we examined the effect of the interventions, PT vs education or yoga vs education, on both outcomes through all considered mediators simultaneously. We applied the multiple mediator regression approach,34 an extension of the product method.<sup>2</sup> For each intervention comparison and outcome combination, we fit 7 mediator linear models with each mediator regressed on the intervention and the confounders of the mediatoroutcome relationship. The outcome is then regressed on the 7 mediators, intervention, and confounders of the mediator-outcome relationship. The direct effect is the treatment coefficient in the outcome model, and the indirect effect is the sum over all considered mediators of the product of each mediator model's treatment coefficient and the outcome models matching mediator coefficient.

We conducted 3 sensitivity analyses:

- **1.** We assessed the robustness of the natural indirect effects to violations of the no unobserved confounders of the mediator-outcome relationship for any significant indirect effect. Imai et al16 introduced the application of the correlation between the residuals of the mediator and outcome models (rho) as the sensitivity analysis. The existence of unobserved confounders of the mediator-outcome relationship would imply a nonzero rho. By varying the value of the sensitivity between −1 and 1, we evaluate the sensitivity of the natural indirect effects to violations of the sequential ignorability assumption.
- **2.** Because the primary analysis included only participants with complete mediator and outcome data, we reanalyzed the data imputing the missing values. We created 20 imputed data sets with predictive mean matching with an imputation model including variables described above (potential confounders) as well as income, education, baseline satisfaction, treatment expectations/preference, and days in the last 3 months with lower back pain. For each imputed data set, we implemented the simulation approach to estimate the natural indirect and direct effects on each imputed data set and then combined all simulations to obtain estimates and CIs.
- **3.** To reduce lag time between the 12-week mediators and the primary 52-week outcomes, we repeated the mediation analyses using the pain and disability outcomes measured at 26 weeks and then at 40 weeks.

## **RESULTS**

Our sample was middle aged (mean age = 46 years), and predominantly female (70%) and non-White (80%) (TABLE 1). On average, participants started with moderate-to-severe back-related pain and disability. Baseline sleep quality and pain self-efficacy were poor, and perceived stress was slightly elevated. Participants had low fear-avoidance beliefs and mildto-moderate anxiety and depressive symptoms. Only 13 participants in the PT intervention had elevated FABQ scores and received The Back Book.

Sample characteristics of participants with missing 12- or 52-week data, compared to those with complete data, are presented in APPENDIX A. Participants excluded from our sample were more likely to be male, be non-White, and have less educational attainment.

#### **PT vs Education**

When the mediator was perceived stress, the average total effect on disability of 2.6 points (95% CI: 0.3, 4.9) was decomposed into a direct effect of 1.7 points (95% CI: −0.4, 3.8) and an indirect effect 0.9 points (95% CI: 0.1, 2.0; proportion mediated, 34%). Smaller and nonsignificant indirect effects were found for pain self-efficacy (0.7; 95% CI: −0.3, 1.9) and anxiety (0.7; 95% CI: −0.1, 1.6). None of the potential mediators appeared to mediate the effect of PT versus education on improvements in pain (TABLE 2).

All intervention-mediator (ie, path a) estimates were nonsignificant, whereas all mediatoroutcome (ie, path b) estimates were significant except for anxiety when the outcome was pain (TABLE 2).

The mediation model including multiple mediators did not yield significant indirect effects on disability or pain for both PT and yoga versus education models (APPENDIX B).

#### **Yoga vs Education**

None of the psychological factors appeared to be strong mediators of the effect of yoga on disability or pain (TABLE 2). Modest, indirect effects on disability were observed for stress (0.7; 95% CI: −0.2, 1.7; proportion mediated, 27%), although these were not statistically significant.

All intervention-mediator (ie, path a) estimates were nonsignificant, whereas all mediatoroutcome (ie, path b) estimates were significant except for depression when the outcome was pain (TABLE 2).

#### **Sensitivity Analyses**

The sensitivity analysis for the estimate of the natural indirect effect of PT versus education on disability score through stress indicates a fairly robust estimate to a violation of unobserved confounding in the mediator-outcome relationship. Specifically, the value of rho for which the natural indirect effect would become zero is 0.3 (FIGURE 2).

Results from mediation models that used imputed data generally reflected our primary complete-case analyses (APPENDIXES C and D). For PT versus education, stress remained

the sole significant indirect effect of disability outcome (0.5; 95% CI: 0.1, 1.2; proportion mediated, 25%). For yoga versus education, the indirect effect of pain self-efficacy became significant for disability outcome (0.6; 95% CI: 0.02, 1.3; proportion mediated, 24%).

Results were similar when comparing our 52-week outcome analysis and our 26- and 40-week outcome analyses (APPENDIXES E and F). However, while perceived stress was not a statistically significant mediator of pain or disability improvements in the yoga group at 52 weeks, it was statistically significant for both outcomes at each of the earlier time points.

## **DISCUSSION**

We found that improvements in perceived stress mediated a third of the effect of PT compared to education on disability at 1 year of follow-up (ie, 0.9 of 2.4 points in mean improvement on the RMDQ). Other psychological factors either were potentially weak mediators (self-efficacy and anxiety for PT, self-efficacy and stress for yoga) or did not appear to mediate long-term improvements in pain and disability (catastrophizing, fear-avoidance beliefs, and sleep) relative to the education control.

Compared to an education group, the yoga and PT interventions did not improve pain or disability via improvements in multiple psychological factors. The effects of PT and yoga compared to education on almost all of the psychological mediators (path a) were small. However, significant effects of change in mediators on the change in outcomes (path b) suggest that psychological outcomes may mediate pain and disability outcomes in interventions that achieve larger improvements in psychological outcomes.

Multiple characteristics of our study may explain why effects of PT and yoga on psychological outcomes at 12 weeks were small. It is possible that our education book was an active intervention as opposed to an inert treatment group. Some of the components of this group (eg, breathing and meditation exercises, techniques for managing flare-ups) may operate through similar psychological mechanisms as our PT and yoga interventions. Unpublished qualitative data from our study suggest that participants who learned selfcare strategies felt greater self-efficacy for managing their pain. We did not require that participants have elevated psychological distress for inclusion. Thus, our sample included a mix of participants with and without elevated psychological scores. The interventions were not specifically designed to address psychological factors. While our interventions included components thought to impact psychological outcomes (eg, graded exercise for PT and meditation/mindfulness for yoga), our intervention did not include a discrete intervention targeted at psychological outcomes (eg, CBT). In contrast, 2 studies of cognitive behavioral– based interventions integrated with PT for participants showed a decrease in disability through improvements in fear avoidance, self-efficacy, and kinesiophobia.12,23 For example, 1 of these studies found that a 6.3-point improvement on the Oswestry Disability Index was mediated, in part, by improvements in self-efficacy (indirect effect = 3.0) but not other factors (eg, stress, fear, sleep).<sup>23</sup>

Future research should explore other potential mechanisms for improving back-related pain and disability. The residual direct effect observed in our analyses supports alternative mediators, which may include physiologic mechanisms (eg, nociceptive processing and modulation, somatosensory representation) or more general cognitive behavioral changes such as increasing physical activity. Our participants were recruited from underserved neighborhoods of Boston, and most had an income of less than \$30 000 per year. Pairing yoga or PT interventions with interventions that address social determinants of health may be helpful to explore whether reducing social needs can improve back-related outcomes.<sup>27</sup>

#### **Limitations**

Our mediator variables were only collected at 12 weeks. Thus, we were unable to assess whether earlier changes in psychological variables (eg, by 6 weeks) explained 12-week pain and disability improvements. We assumed that psychological improvements preceded improvements in pain or disability. More frequent (eg, weekly) measurement of mediator and outcome variables would be needed to verify this assumption. Conceivably, pain and disability improvements drive psychological outcomes or that these changes happen simultaneously.

Thirty percent of the original sample had dropped out by 52 weeks, with more loss to follow-up in the yoga and PT groups than in the education group. Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation for missing data confirmed our main findings. Less than half of participants met adherence criteria (defined a priori as attending 75% and 73% of yoga and PT sessions, respectively, and reading 75% of the book), which may bias the total effect and the treatment-mediator effect toward the null.

## **CONCLUSION**

Improvements in perceived stress mediated 1-year improvements in back-related disability when comparing PT to an educational control. Effects of yoga and PT on other psychological outcomes compared to education were small and did not significantly mediate improvements in pain or back-related disability at 1 year.

#### **KEY POINTS**

#### **FINDINGS:**

A modest effect of physical therapy (PT) compared to education on back-related disability outcome after 1 year of follow-up was partially explained by larger improvements in perceived stress among PT participants. Improvements in stress were a potential mediator among yoga participants, although the indirect effect was not statistically significant. Effects of yoga and PT on other psychological outcomes compared to education were small and did not significantly mediate improvements in pain or back-related disability at 1 year.

#### **IMPLICATIONS:**

Affecting psychological characteristics associated with painand disability may improve outcomes, although the effect does not differ between PT, yoga, and education. Therefore, combining salient aspects of these interventions to decrease psychological distress may optimize functional outcomes.

#### **CAUTION:**

To further advance our understanding of mechanisms of chronic low back pain treatments, future randomized clinical trials should be designedto allow for optimal causal mediation analyses. This may involve a larger sample, more frequent collection of hypothesized mediator and outcome variables, and an inert control group.
