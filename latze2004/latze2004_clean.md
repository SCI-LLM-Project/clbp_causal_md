# Can Health Care Utilization Explain the Association Between Socioeconomic Status and Back Pain?

Ute Latza, PhD,\* Thomas Kohlmann, PhD,† Ruth Deck, PhD,‡ and Heiner Raspe, MD‡

Study Design. Cross-sectional population-based study.

Objectives. To assess whether the association between socioeconomic status and severe back pain can be explained by the preceding health care utilization for back pain.

Summary of Background Data. The ways in which socioeconomic status affects the occurrence of back pain are unclear

**Methods.** Age- and gender-adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for the association between indicators of socioeconomic status and severe current back pain (high intensity and/or high disability: no/yes) were investigated in an interview among 770 study participants out of 1113 study participants with a recent history of back pain in a survey among 2731 adults.

Results. The point prevalence of severe current back pain (39.8%) was related to educational level and health insurance status. Prior health care utilization for back pain was about 2-fold more prevalent in adults with severe current back pain. Members of private health insurance (odds ratio 0.60, 95% confidence interval 0.37-0.99) were less likely to report prior consultation of a general practitioner for back pain. Members of sick funds for whitecollar workers (odds ratio 2.81, 95% confidence interval 1.43-5.51) and private insurance (odds ratio 2.81, 95% confidence interval 1.02-6.24) and individuals with intermediate educational level (odds ratio 1.76, 95% confidence interval 1.05-2.95) utilized more physical therapy for the treatment of back pain. After additionally adjusting for health care utilization, the associations between educational level or health insurance status and severe current back pain remained unchanged.

**Conclusions.** The data suggest that education, health insurance status, and health care utilization are independently associated with severe current back pain in a society with universal access to health care.

Like other disorders, back pain and its consequences are inversely related to indicators of high socioeconomic status (SES) such as educational level (reviewed by Dionne et  $al^2$ ). The relationships between indicators of SES and other conditions, e.g., breast cancer<sup>3</sup> and cardiovascular mortality, <sup>4</sup> appear to be largely mediated by known risk factors. In contrast, the ways in which SES affects the occurrence of back pain remain to be understood. So far, the association between SES and back pain was not explained by potential risk factors for the occurrence of back pain such as smoking, 5,6 obesity, 5 height, 5,6 body mass index, 6 physical activity, 6 depressive symptoms, 5 mental distress,<sup>6</sup> and occupational factors.<sup>7-9</sup> In a study among enrollees of a health plan who consulted for back pain, some of the differences in back related-disability among participants with different educational levels were potentially mediated by somatization.<sup>8</sup> Another mechanism that may explain the associations between indicators of SES and back pain is differential access to and utilization of health care.<sup>2</sup> Under this hypothesis, lower SES would lead to a decreased utilization of health care in case of back pain and thus to a more unfavorable course of this disease. If this hypothesis holds, adjustment for health care utilization would reduce the observed inverse association between SES and severe back

The Lübeck Survey on Back Pain<sup>10,11</sup> is comprised of a population-based cross-sectional survey among German adults followed by an interview of individuals with a recent episode of back pain. In the interview, medium and, in particular, high levels of indicators of SES were associated with a reduced frequency of severe current back pain.<sup>9</sup> Lower educational level as a measure of early socioeconomic inequalities substantially contributed to an increased prevalence of severe current back pain in later life.

Our goal in the present analysis of the Lübeck Survey on Back Pain is to test if the previously observed inverse association between educational level or health insurance status and severe current back pain can be explained by the type of health care sought in the last 12 months. Further, we want to determine if the sort of health care sought was a function of the type of health insurance the study participant held; in particular, if having a copayment reduced the use of health care.

### **■** Materials and Methods

**Study Population.** A cross-sectional, population-based postal survey for back pain among 2731 German adults aged 25 to 74 years was conducted from 1991 to 1992 in the town of Lübeck. <sup>10</sup> The local ethics committee approved the study. It con-

forms to the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki. The response rate was 80.9%. Individuals who reported current back pain or a first episode within the past 12 months were selected for a standardized interview. 9,11 Of the 1113 eligible participants, 770 individuals (69.2%; 416 women and 354 men) were interviewed about 1 month after the postal survey. Participation 9,10 and characteristics of the study population, in the postal survey¹0 and in the interview, respectively, were described earlier.

Assessment of Current Back Pain and Severe Current Back Pain. Current back pain was defined as point prevalence of back pain on the day of the interview ("Do you have back pain today? No/yes"). This prevalence refers to the frequency of back pain in study participants with a recent history of back pain and not in the whole population. Severity of current back pain was measured by 2 instruments for which reliability and validity has been previously established: a 10-point rating scale of pain intensity<sup>12</sup> and a 12-item scoring of functional disability (Hannover Functional Status Questionnaire (HFSQ), activities of daily living [ADL] questionnaire)13 that does not include questions on work disability. "Low pain intensity" was defined as values smaller than 5 on the numerical rating scale, and "low disability" as scores greater than 70% in the functional disability questionnaire. Measures were combined 14 and dichotomized as described earlier<sup>13</sup> (Grade 0/I: no back pain or back pain with low intensity and low disability vs. Grade II/III: back pain with high intensity and/or high disability).

**Indicators of Socioeconomic Status.** Out of 5 obtained indicators of SES (educational level, vocational training, occupational class, household income, health insurance status), 2 were investigated in this analysis:

- Educational level was estimated by the highest qualification achieved: low (Volksschule/Sonstiges: leaving school after 8 or 9 years at the age of 14 or 15), intermediate (Mittlere Reife/Fachhochschulreife: certificate after 10 or 12 years of school at the age of 16 or 18), and high (Abitur: certificate after 13 years of school at the age of 19). Low level of education was chosen as the reference because the proportion of participants who reported low education in the postal survey (57%) was high.
- Health insurance status included local statutory sickness fund (mainly blue-collar or unemployed workers), other statutory sickness fund (mainly white-collar employees), private insurance, or other (civil servants, self-employed individuals, employees with high income).

History of Health Care Utilization. During the interview, information was obtained on visits to medical doctors within the preceding 12 months and medical treatment for back pain within the preceding 12 months. Visits to any physician and visits to frequently consulted medical specialties due to back pain (general practitioner, orthopaedist) as well as prevalent treatment methods for back pain (analgesics, injections, physical therapy) were selected for further analysis.

Analysis. The odds ratio (OR) is derived from the ratio of the odds of being exposed to a factor if disease is present compared to those being exposed if disease is absent. If the OR is greater than unity (*i.e.*, 1.0 or more), the exposure is associated with the disease, and if less than 1, the exposure is protective. As a

measure of the association between variables, the OR does not indicate the actual magnitude of the association in terms of absolute differences between probabilities. In this analysis, the prevalence of severe current back pain among patients with a recent history of back pain with and without indicators of SES or previous health care utilization was compared. Further, the presence or absence of an effect of indicators of SES on the prevalence of preceding health care utilization was investigated. The OR with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was estimated from logistic regression.

All estimates were adjusted for age (<34/35-44/45-54/55-64/>65 years) and gender. Multiplicative interaction terms of gender on the one hand and the 2 indicators of SES investigated (educational level, health insurance status) or history of health care utilization on the other hand were tested for statistical significance. An  $\alpha$  level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Participants with missing data were excluded. To explore the extent to which the association of SES with severe back pain might be explained by different medical treatment of back pain, the age- and gender-adjusted models were additionally adjusted for prior health care utilization.

#### ■ Results

#### Prevalence of Back Pain

On the day of the interview, 71.2% and 39.8% of individuals with a recent history of back pain in the postal survey reported current back pain and severe current back pain, respectively. The current pain episode had lasted for more than a year in 40.4% of patients with back pain and 44.8% of patients with severe current back pain. Information on the frequency of back pain during the previous 12 months was available for 502 patients. Among individuals with current back pain and severe current back pain, 89.8% and 95.0%, respectively, reported several additional episodes of back pain or continuous back pain within the preceding 12 months.

After stratification by age and gender (Table 1), the prevalence of severe back pain among patients with a recent history of back pain increased monotonically with age group and was lowest in 25-year-old to  $\leq$ 34-year-old males (20.3%) and highest in females aged 65 to 74 years (57.3%).

#### History of Health Care Utilization and Severe Current Back Pain

Individuals who, within the preceding 12 months, consulted any medical physician, a general practitioner due to back pain, and an orthopaedist due to back pain had a higher prevalence of severe current back pain than individuals who did not visit that particular medical specialist (OR 1.89 [95% CI 1.10–3.23], OR 2.71 [95% CI 1.92–3.83]; and OR 2.26 [95% CI 1.61–3.17], respectively) (Table 2). Severe current back pain was more common in individuals who reported prior treatment of back pain with analgesics, injections, and physical therapy than in individuals who did not seek that particular type of treatment modality for back pain (OR 1.89 [95% CI 1.36–2.61], OR 1.84 [95% CI 1.30–2.58], and OR 1.99 [95% CI 1.20–3.30], respectively).

## *Indicators of Socioeconomic Status and History of Health Care Utilization*

Members of other sick funds (OR 2.81, 95% CI 1.43– 5.51) and members of private insurance (OR 2.54, 95% CI 1.02–6.24) received more physical therapy than members of the local sick fund (Table 3). Individuals with intermediate educational level also used more physical therapy (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.05–2.95) as compared to individuals with low educational level. Gender differences were observed in the association between educational level and visit to an orthopaedist for back pain. Longer educated women tended to consult this specialist more often, whereas longer educated males tended to consult an orthopaedist less frequently.

# *Indicators of Socioeconomic Status, History of Health Care Utilization, and Severe Current Back Pain*

Compared to a low educational level, intermediate and high educational levels were associated with a decreased prevalence of severe current back pain (intermediate: OR 0.45; 95% CI 0.35–0.56; high: OR 0.22; 95% CI 0.13– 0.36) (Table 4). Members of other sick funds and private insurance were less likely to report severe current back pain as compared to members of the local sick fund (other sick funds: OR 0.59; 95% CI 0.42–0.83; private insurance or other: OR 0.40; 95% CI 0.23–0.69). Adjustment for utilization of health care during the preceding 12 months did not change the associations between indicators of SES and severe current back pain (Table 4). The protective effect of higher educational levels, as compared to a low level, and private health insurance or sick funds other than local sick funds as compared to local sick funds, respectively, on the prevalence of severe current back pain was not considerably reduced and remained statistically significant.

### **Discussion**

The demonstrated gradient between indicators of SES and severe current back pain among adults in the Lu¨beck Study on Back Pain9 confirms results on the association between SES and back pain in other industrialized countries.2 With the study of pain prevalence among study participants with current back pain or a first episode within the past 12 months in the preceding postal survey, the authors selected for those participants who suffered from frequent or long-lasting episodes of back pain. In this respect, the participants from the presented study resemble patients encountered in primary care who typically have a long history of recurrent back pain episodes.15 The prevalence of current back pain (71%) in the interview about 1 month after the postal survey corresponds to the prevalence of back pain in primary care patients 1 month after consulting for back pain.15

# Association Between Severe current Back Pain and History of Health Care Utilization

Nearly all participants with severe current back pain (93%) had visited a medical physician during the 12 months before the interview. This figure is in agreement with the percentage of adults with chronic low back pain who saw a medical physician (91%) in a populationbased study in North Carolina. 16 In the presented survey, most individuals with back pain went to a general practitioner (62%). The results confirm previous findings of about 60% in a Danish population-based survey. 17 As the German health care system allows free choice of the specialty of the medical provider but asks for a copayment for each visit to a physical therapist, comparatively many patients visited a specialist, in particular an orthopaedist (37%), whereas relatively few (14%) attended a physical therapist. In studies in North Carolina<sup>16</sup> and Denmark, 17 the prevalence of individuals who saw a physical therapist was higher (29% 17 and 25%, 18 respectively). A specialist was consulted less frequently in the Danish survey (24–29% <sup>17</sup>), whereas the percentage of individuals who saw an orthopaedist (37%) was also high in an analysis from the United States. 18 Treatment with analgesics in the presented study was comparable to the Danish figures (41% vs. 38–47%),<sup>17</sup> but injections were given more frequently (35% vs. 16–23%).<sup>17</sup>

Prior access to medical doctors and different treatment methods for back pain were about 2-fold more prevalent in participants with severe current back pain than in participants without or with less severe back pain. Comparable results were obtained when participants were restricted to those (N = 267) who reported a duration of the back pain period in the postal survey of more than 12 months (data not shown). The current period of severe back pain had started more than a year ago in nearly half of the affected in individuals. It is reasonable that a severe back pain period can last for more than a year and that individuals with intense and/or disabling back pain report more health care consumption during the preceding 12 months than to those without back pain or back pain of low intensity and low disability.

### Indicators of Socioeconomic Status, History of Health Care Utilization, and Severe Current Back Pain

Adjusting for health care utilization within the preceding 12 months did not alter the associations between education or health insurance status and severe current back pain. Moreover, the gradients between indicators of SES and severe current back pain were not explained by health care utilization among patients who reported a duration of the back pain period in the postal survey of more than 12 months (N = 267) (data not shown). Accordingly, controlling for health care utilization did not substantially reduce the importance of education in an analysis of the NHANES II data. 18 The simultaneous contribution of predictor variables (age, gender, habitat, language, general health, low back pain frequency, and chronicity) in a study on health care utilization for low back pain in Belgium revealed a U-shaped association of social class with visits to health care professionals and no significant association with medication use.<sup>21</sup> This may indicate that the association with medication can be explained by low back pain frequency or chronicity. The authors did not evaluate whether health care utilization influences the processes leading to chronic back pain. In the presented study, the current pain episode lasted for more than 3 months in only 17% of patients with severe current back pain.9

Four aspects might account for the finding that the association between indicators of SES and severe current back pain was not explained by prior consultation of medical doctors and treatment of back pain. First, health histories were determined by interview information on medical treatment for the preceding year. The validity of the self-reported information on health care utilization was not evaluated. The potential limitation of this source of data is the influence of recall bias, particularly when stratifying across various education levels. Second, health care utilization in patients may not affect the prevalence of severe back pain because many patients with back pain do not benefit from the medical treatment. 15 If so, encouraging lower SES patients with back pain to seek medical help may have little or no effect on the higher prevalence of severe back pain in lower SES. Third, access to health care may not be determined by social background in a society with universal health care, although utilization preferences might differ between social classes. When the study was conducted, most Germans (99%) had health insurance: 90% were enrolled in statutory sick funds, which offer a comprehensive benefit package and 9% in private insurances.<sup>23</sup> Fourth, the differences within the type of the health care provider might be as large as those between different providers. The health care utilization pattern differs because physicians do not follow published guidelines for the treatment of back pain utilization (reviewed by Borenstein<sup>24</sup>).

In conclusion, the presented data do not support the hypothesis that the association between SES and severe current back pain can be explained by socioeconomically dependent therapeutic influences.

### **■ Key Points**

- The prevalence of severe current back pain in German adults is inversely related to higher socioeconomic status.
- The differences in severe back pain by indicators of social class were not due to socioeconomic differences in health care utilization.
- The results of this study in a country with statutory comprehensive health care insurance need to be verified in a population with unequal access to health care.
