## Abstract

**Context:** Effects of modest alcohol consumption remain controversial. Mendelian randomization (MR) can help to mitigate biases due to confounding and reverse causation in observational studies, and evaluate the potential causal role of alcohol consumption.

**Objective:** This work aimed to evaluate dose-dependent effect of alcohol consumption on obesity and type 2 diabetes.

**Methods:** Assessing 408 540 participants of European ancestry in the UK Biobank, we first tested the association between self-reported alcohol intake frequency and 10 anthropometric measurements, obesity, and type 2 diabetes. We then conducted MR analyses both in the overall population and in subpopulations stratified by alcohol intake frequency.

**Results:** Among individuals having more than 14 drinks per week, a 1-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency was associated with a 0.36-kg increase in fat mass (SD = 0.03 kg), a 1.08-fold increased odds of obesity (95% CI, 1.06-1.10), and a 1.10-fold increased odds of type 2 diabetes (95% CI, 1.06-1.13). These associations were stronger in women than in men. Furthermore, no evidence was found supporting the association between genetically increased alcohol intake frequency and improved health outcomes among individuals having 7 or fewer drinks per week, as MR estimates largely overlapped with the null. These results withstood multiple sensitivity analyses assessing the validity of MR assumptions.

**Conclusion:** As opposed to observational associations, MR results suggest there may not be protective effects of modest alcohol consumption on obesity traits and type 2 diabetes. Heavy alcohol consumption could lead to increased measures of obesity as well as increased risk of type 2 diabetes.

<span id="page-0-2"></span><span id="page-0-1"></span><span id="page-0-0"></span>Alcohol consumption has a profound influence on public health (1, 2). In addition to acute alcohol intoxication, risky behaviors, injuries, and miscarriage and stillbirth among pregnant women, it is widely recognized that excessive alcohol intake can increase risks of multiple chronic diseases and lead to severe health problems (1, 3–5). The Dietary Guidelines of Americans 2020 to 2025 recommend limiting alcohol intake to 7 drinks per week for women and 14 drinks per week for men (6). However, it remains controversial whether modest alcohol

consumption may have protective effects against some adverse health outcomes.

<span id="page-0-4"></span><span id="page-0-3"></span>Multiple observational studies have been conducted to assess the potential dose-dependent effects of alcohol consumption (7–14). Many studies reported a U-shaped or J-shaped trend where light-to-moderate drinkers had better health outcomes compared to nondrinkers and heavy drinkers (7–11). However, modest alcohol consumption can be associated with confounders such as a healthier lifestyle and higher

Owing to these potential confounding effects, possible reverse causation, and a lack of explicit biological mechanisms, associations detected in observational studies are not able to unequivocally support modest alcohol consumption as a risk-reducing behavior. Fully eliminating such biases is hardly achievable in observational studies, and ethical concerns render the execution of randomized controlled trials difficult. In fact, a recent large-scale, randomized controlled trial of moderate alcohol consumption was terminated because of multiple concerns regarding the study design and other ethical issues [\(15\)](#page-8-0).

<span id="page-1-0"></span>A promising approach to help mitigate these biases and assess potential causal effects of alcohol consumption without randomized controlled trials is mendelian randomization (MR) [\(16\)](#page-8-0). MR implements a causal inference framework using genetic variants to derive an instrumental variable. The causal effect of an exposure (ie, alcohol consumption) on an outcome (eg, type 2 diabetes) is estimated as the ratio of the magnitude of the instrumental variable-outcome association to the magnitude of the instrumental variable-exposure association. MR requires instrumental variable assumptions ([16,](#page-8-0) [17](#page-8-0)): (i) the instrumental variable is strongly associated with the exposure, (ii) the instrumental variable is associated with the outcome only via the exposure (ie, no horizontal pleiotropy), and (iii) the instrumental variable is not associated with any confounders. These assumptions can be met by selecting genetic variants that are significantly associated with alcohol intake frequency from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) and that affect genes directly responsible for alcohol metabolism. Furthermore, because genetic variants are randomly assigned from the parents to the offspring at conception and this random allocation process occurs before the formation of any traits and development of any diseases, MR can effectively reduce the risk of confounding effects and reverse causation [\(16,](#page-8-0) [17](#page-8-0)). Recent studies have used MR to reveal that any amounts of alcohol consumption may lead to increased cardiovascular disease risks [\(18,](#page-8-0) [19](#page-8-0)), and that heavy alcohol consumption likely increases the risk of type 2 diabetes ([20](#page-8-0), [21\)](#page-8-0). However, the effect of light-tomoderate alcohol consumption remains unclear because of limited statistical power.

<span id="page-1-4"></span><span id="page-1-3"></span><span id="page-1-1"></span>Therefore, in this study, based on up to 408 540 European ancestry participants in the UK Biobank, we tested the association between alcohol intake frequency and multiple health outcomes, including 10 anthropometric measurements, obesity, and type 2 diabetes. We assessed both observational and MR evidence in the overall population and in subpopulations defined with respect to alcohol intake frequency. Using recently developed robust nonlinear MR approaches, we examined the potential causal effect of alcohol consumption in a dosedependent manner.

# **Materials and Methods**

## Study Cohort

<span id="page-1-5"></span>We leveraged the UK Biobank for this study ([22\)](#page-8-0). Between 2006 and 2010, approximately 500 000 participants aged 40 to 69 years enrolled in the UK Biobank at multiple recruitment centers in the United Kingdom. After enrollment, the participants underwent anthropometric measurements, responded to questionnaires about lifestyle and medical history, and consented to health care record access. During the follow-up, health care records were retrieved from the Hospital Episode Statistics database and the national death and cancer registries. In this study, the end date of follow-up was March 31, 2020, or the date of death if a participant died before March 31, 2020. Blood samples were collected and were used for genome-wide genotyping. Ethics approval for the UK Biobank study was obtained from the North West Centre for Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382). All UK Biobank participants provided informed consent at recruitment.

This study focused on 10 quantitative anthropometric measurements obtained on recruitment—height, weight, fat mass, fat-free mass, waist circumference, hip circumference, body mass index (BMI), fat mass index (FMI), fat-free mass index (FFMI), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR)—and 2 disease outcomes—obesity and type 2 diabetes. Individuals having a BMI of 30 or greater at recruitment were considered to have obesity. Individuals having an International Classification of Diseases 10 code of E11, a self-reported physician-made diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (data fields 1220, 1223, or 2443), or a measured glycated hemoglobin A1c level greater than or equal to 48 mmol/mol, or taking antidiabetic medications ([23\)](#page-8-0) (medication codes 1140868902, 1140874646, 1140874674, 1140874718, 1140874744, 1140883066, 1140884600, 1141152590, 1141157284, 1141168660, 1141171646, 1141173882, or 1141189090 in data field 20003) at recruitment or during the follow-up were considered type 2 diabetes patient cases.

<span id="page-1-6"></span>The UK Biobank consists of participants of diverse genetic ancestries. However, this study was restricted to participants of European ancestry because the nonlinear MR analyses were not sufficiently powered for subpopulations of non-European ancestries due to their relatively small sample sizes.

## Quantification of Alcohol Consumption

<span id="page-1-2"></span>At recruitment, the UK Biobank participants completed a questionnaire assessing types of alcoholic drinks they would consume at least once a month and the corresponding intake frequencies. We estimated the weekly pure alcohol intake for each individual with 1 pint of beer or cider = 6 grams (g), 1 standard glass of white or red wine = 16.8 g, 1 glass of fortified wine = 14.08 g, 1 shot of spirits or liquor = 8 g, or 1 glass of other alcohol drinks = 12 g ([18\)](#page-8-0). We then converted the pure alcohol intake to the unit of standard drink, where 1 standard drink = 14 g of pure alcohol ([6\)](#page-8-0). The population was stratified with respect to alcohol intake frequency based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines [\(6](#page-8-0)): (i) nondrinkers, (ii) more than 0 and 3.5 drinks or fewer per week, (iii) more than 3.5 and 7 drinks or fewer per week, (iv) more than 7 and 14 or fewer drinks per week, and (v) more than 14 drinks per week.

### Observational Associations

We tested the association between alcohol intake frequency and each of the 10 quantitative anthropometric measurements by linear regression. We also tested whether alcohol intake frequency was associated with obesity and type 2 diabetes by logistic regression. These analyses were performed separately in the entire population excluding nondrinkers, and in each stratum defined earlier based on alcohol intake frequency. Observational association testing did not include nondrinkers (former drinkers and long-term abstainers) to reduce the potential effect of confounding effects and reverse causation. We did not differentiate between long-term abstainers and former drinkers because of possible measurement errors and the lack of validity of reported abstention (24).

<span id="page-2-0"></span>Two regression models were fitted for each health outcome. The first baseline model adjusted for the fixed effects of age, sex, and recruitment center, while the second model additionally adjusted for the fixed effects of smoking status (data field 20116), total household income (data field 738), education qualification (data field 6138), employment status (data field 6142), and physical activity measured in summed metabolic equivalent of task minutes per week (data field 22040).

#### Mendelian Randomization

<span id="page-2-3"></span><span id="page-2-2"></span><span id="page-2-1"></span>We performed MR analyses to assess whether the genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency was associated with the anthropometric measurements or disease risks. We first selected genetic instruments for alcohol intake frequency based on a large-scale meta-analysis of GWASs including up to 226 223 individuals predominantly of European ancestry from 30 study cohorts (25). We used the meta-analysis result that did not include the UK Biobank to avoid sample overlap and overfitting in our analyses. We retained independent genetic variants demonstrating genome-wide significance with a P value less than  $5 \times 10^{-8}$  and pairwise linkage disequilibrium  $r^2$  less than 0.01 based on the European ancestry linkage disequilibrium reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project (26). We mapped these genetic variants to known genes by ANNOVAR (27) (Supplementary Table S1 (28)). We chose 2 independent variants—rs1229984, a nonsynonymous variant in ADH1B, and rs1302808, a variant near ADH1C and ADH7—to derive a genetic risk score. This weighted genetic risk score was a sum of risk alleles, weighted by their estimated effect sizes in GWAS. The catalytic efficiency of these alcohol dehydrogenase genes determines the accumulation rate of the toxic acetaldehyde, which affects the pleasantness of drinking and influences alcohol intake frequency (29–31). We did not use other genetic variants since they map to genes having multifactorial functions (Supplementary Table S1 (28)), which may increase the risk of directional horizontal pleiotropy. To assess potential bias, we performed additional MR analyses using a weighted genetic risk score consisting of all 6 genome-wide significant variants (Supplementary Notes (28)).

<span id="page-2-5"></span>In the entire population excluding nondrinkers, we performed 2-stage least-squares regression to derive MR estimates. Specifically, we first regressed alcohol intake frequency on the genetic risk score, and obtained the genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency. An F-statistic was derived based on the magnitude of association between the genetic risk score and alcohol intake frequency,

$$F = \left(\frac{N - k - 1}{k}\right) \left(\frac{R^2}{1 - R^2}\right)$$

<span id="page-2-6"></span>where N represents the sample size, k represents the number of instruments (in this study k = 1 genetic risk score), and  $R^2$  represents the proportion of variance in alcohol intake frequency explained by the genetic risk score. An F-statistic greater than 10 would indicate a low risk of weak instrument bias in MR analyses (32, 33). We then regressed each anthropometric

measurement on the genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency by linear regression, as well as obesity and type 2 diabetes on the genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency by logistic regression. The MR estimates were scaled to reflect the association between any outcome with a 1-standard-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency.

<span id="page-2-7"></span>Next, we investigated potential nonlinear effects of alcohol intake. As noted in existing studies, stratification in nonlinear MR analyses should be based on residualized exposure variable after regressing out the effect of the genetic instrument to avoid collider bias (19, 34, 35). Therefore, in women and men separately, we regressed alcohol intake frequency on the genetic risk score after excluding nondrinkers, and recentered the residualized alcohol intake frequency to have the same mean as the original alcohol intake frequency. The population was stratified with respect to the residualized alcohol intake frequency: (i) more than 0 and 3.5 drinks or fewer per week, (ii) more than 3.5 and 7 drinks or fewer per week, (iii) more than 7 and 14 drinks or fewer per week, and (iv) more than 14 drinks per week (Supplementary Table S2 (28)). However, because the residualized alcohol intake frequency was highly correlated with the original alcohol intake frequency with a Pearson correlation greater than 0.99, the interpretation of these strata would not change. Two-stage least-squares regression was performed in each stratum with the measured alcohol intake frequency as the exposure variable. MR estimates in nonlinear MR analyses with a P value of less than .001 (ie, 0.05/12 biomarkers or disease outcomes/4 strata) were considered statistically significant. We recognize such a threshold is likely overly conservative, given the nonindependence of these variables.

To examine whether the MR estimates were sex- or age-specific, we repeated all MR analyses separately in women and men, and in individuals younger than 60 years and those 60 years or older. All MR analyses adjusted for the fixed effects of age, sex, recruitment center, and the first 10 genetic principal components. We performed Steiger filtering for each test to examine whether the causal direction (alcohol intake-to-health outcome) was correct (36).

<span id="page-2-8"></span><span id="page-2-4"></span>Sensitivity analyses are described in the Supplementary Notes (28). All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.5.

#### **Observational Associations**

We first assessed observational associations based on selfreported alcohol intake frequency. As a result, while the associations in the overall population (excluding nondrinkers) were weak, all weight and body size measures—total weight, fat mass, FFM, BMI, FMI, FFMI, waist circumference, hip circumference, and WHR—were negatively correlated with increased alcohol intake frequency among individuals having more than 0 and 3.5 drinks or fewer per week and were positively correlated with increased alcohol intake frequency among heavier drinkers (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S3 (28)). These associations were consistent between the baseline model that adjusted for age, sex, and recruitment center and the model that additionally adjusted for smoking status, total household income, education qualification, employment status, and physical activity (see Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S3 (28)).

Furthermore, increased alcohol intake frequency was significantly associated with a lower prevalence of obesity and type 2 diabetes among individuals having more than 0 and 3.5 drinks or fewer per week. In this stratum, a 1-drink-per-week increase in alcohol intake frequency was associated with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.92 for obesity (95% CI, 0.90-0.94; P value =  $3.3 \times$  $10^{-14}$ ) and an OR of 0.87 for type 2 diabetes (95% CI, 0.84-0.90; P value =  $7.9 \times 10^{-15}$ ). After adjusting for the effects of smoking status, total household income, education qualification, employment status, and physical activity, a 1-drink-per-week increase in alcohol intake frequency was still associated with an OR of 0.91 for obesity (95% CI, 0.89-0.94; P value =  $2.6 \times 10^{-11}$ ) and an OR of 0.88 for type 2 diabetes (95% CI, 0.84-0.91; P value =  $1.3 \times 10^{-9}$ ). In other strata where individuals took more than 3.5 drinks per week, associations between alcohol intake frequency and these disease outcomes were not detectable or had only a small magnitude (see Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S3 (28)).

## Validity of Genetic Instrument Employed in Mendelian Randomization

A genetic risk score built on 2 variants, rs1229984 and rs1302808 (Supplementary Table S1 [\(28\)](#page-8-0)), was used as the genetic instrument for MR analyses. This genetic instrument was significantly associated with alcohol intake frequency in all unstratified and stratified analyses, with F-statistics largely exceeding 10 (Supplementary Table S4 ([28](#page-8-0))). Importantly, among nondrinkers, this genetic risk score was not significantly associated with any of the anthropometric measurements or disease outcomes (Supplementary Table S5 [\(28\)](#page-8-0)), suggesting a low risk of directional horizontal pleiotropy. In contrast, a genetic risk score aggregating all independent genome-wide significant variants identified in the alcohol intake frequency GWAS (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Table S5 ([28\)](#page-8-0)) demonstrated significant associations (*P* value <.001) with height, weight, FFM, and hip circumference, suggesting the presence of directional horizontal pleiotropic effects. Steiger filtering supported correct causal direction in all MR analyses (Supplementary Tables S6-S15 (28)).

#### Mendelian Randomization-estimated Associations Between Alcohol Intake Frequency and Anthropometric Measurements

MR estimates assessing the association between alcohol intake frequency and anthropometric measurements were obtained based on the overall population excluding nondrinkers as well as in each stratum defined based on residualized alcohol intake frequency (Supplementary Methods and Fig. 2). Importantly, MR analyses showed that genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency was not associated with adult height (see Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S6 (28)). For other anthropometric measures, nonlinear MR analyses indicated that moderate-to-heavy alcohol intake (>7 drinks per week) was associated with a larger body weight and body size. Notably, for individuals having more than 14 drinks per week, although genetically increased alcohol intake frequency was associated with increased FM, FFM, waist circumference, and hip circumference, the magnitude of association was larger for FM than FFM, and larger for waist circumference than hip circumference (see Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S6 (28)). Specifically, a 1-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency in this stratum was associated with a 0.36-kg increase in FM (SD = 0.03 kg; P value =  $3.0 \times 10^{-36}$ ) and a 0.16-kg increase in FFM (SD = 0.02 kg; P value =  $7.8 \times 10^{-12}$ ), as well as a 0.45-cm increase in waist circumference (SD = 0.04 cm; P value =  $1.9 \times 10^{-32}$ ) and a 0.29-cm increase in hip circumference (SD = 0.03 cm; P value =  $3.8 \times 10^{-25}$ ). On the contrary, genetically predicted alcohol intake did not demonstrate any discernible association with these anthropometric measurements among individuals with light-to-moderate alcohol intake ( $\leq 7$  drinks per week).

In sex-stratified analyses, genetically predicted alcohol consumption was more strongly associated with the anthropometric measurements in women than in men (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S7 (28)). For instance, a 1-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency was associated with a 1.17-kg and 0.36-kg increase in FM for women having more than 14 drinks per week (SD = 0.16 kg; P value =  $9.3 \times 10^{-14}$ ) and women having more than 7 and 14 or fewer drinks per week (SD = 0.10 kg; P value =  $3.0 \times 10^{-4}$ ), respectively. In comparison, a 1-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency was associated with an FM increase of 0.27 kg for men having more than 14 drinks per week (SD = 0.03 kg; P value =  $3.9 \times 10^{-25}$ ), and 0.19 kg for men having more than 7 and 14 or fewer drinks per week  $(SD = 0.05 \text{ kg}; P \text{ value} = 3.6 \times 10^{-5})$ . On the other hand, all MR-estimated associations were largely consistent between individuals younger than 60 years and those 60 years or older (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S8 (28)).

#### Mendelian Randomization-estimated Association of Alcohol Intake Frequency With Obesity and Type 2 Diabetes

The unstratified MR analyses indicated that genetically increased alcohol intake was associated with increased risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes in the overall population (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S9 (28)). These associations were most pronounced for individuals having 14 or more drinks per week, where a 1-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency was associated with a 1.08-fold increased odds of obesity (95% CI, 1.06-1.10; P value = 1.6 ×  $10^{-17}$ ) and a 1.10-fold increased odds of type 2 diabetes (95% CI, 1.06-1.13; P value =  $2.2 \times 10^{-9}$ ), respectively. In contrast, MR estimates overlapped with the null in strata of 7 or fewer drinks per week (see Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S9 (28)).

Again, the MR-estimated associations appeared to have a larger effect size for women than men who were heavy drinkers, but were largely consistent between individuals younger than 60 years and those 60 years or older (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4, and Supplementary Tables S10 and S11 (28)). Specifically, a 1-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency for women having 14 or more drinks per week was associated with a 1.37-fold increased odds of obesity (95% CI, 1.23-1.51; P value =  $1.8 \times 10^{-9}$ ) and a 1.36-fold increased odds of type 2 diabetes (95% CI, 1.09-1.71; P value =  $6.8 \times 10^{-3}$ ), but was associated with only a 1.05-fold increased odds of obesity (95% CI, 1.04-1.07; P value =  $1.2 \times 10^{-10}$ ) and a 1.07-fold increased odds of type 2 diabetes (95% CI, 1.05-1.10; P value =  $1.8 \times 10^{-8}$ ) for men in the same stratum.

#### Sensitivity Analyses

Primary analyses were repeated using 1 of the 2 genetic variants included in the genetic risk score, respectively.

Encouragingly, all MR estimates were largely consistent (see Supplementary Notes, Supplementary Fig. S5, and Supplementary Tables S12 and S13 (28)), although the *ADH1C-ADH7* variant rs1302808 was less strongly associated with alcohol intake frequency, which reduced the power of MR analyses based only on this variant.

Furthermore, MR analyses were conducted for incident type 2 diabetes after excluding individuals who had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes before or at recruitment (see Supplementary Notes and Supplementary Table S14 (28)). While these analyses were less powered than the primary analyses, we found that a 1-drink-per-week increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency for individuals having 14 or more drinks per week was associated with a 1.09-fold increased hazard (95% CI, 1.04-1.15; P value =  $8.1 \times 10^{-4}$ ). Meanwhile, no evidence of risk reduction associated with increased alcohol intake was observed for individuals having more than 0 and 3.5 or fewer drinks per week (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.93; 95% CI, 0.68-1.27; P value = .67) and individuals having more than 3.5 and 7 or fewer drinks per week (HR = 1.04; 95% CI, 0.94-1.14; P value = .47).

Last, all nonlinear trends identified in primary analyses were confirmed using the doubly ranked stratification approach (see Supplementary Notes (28)), as an increase in genetically predicted alcohol intake frequency among individuals in the highest deciles was significantly associated with adverse health outcomes, whereas MR estimates largely overlapped with the null in the lowest deciles (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Supplementary Table S15 (28)).

#### **Discussion**

There has been a long-standing debate on the effects of light-to-moderate alcohol consumption on health outcomes (3, 7–11). In this study, based on the European ancestry participants in the UK Biobank, we tested whether and how alcohol intake frequency was associated with 10 anthropometric measurements and risks of obesity and type 2 diabetes. In addition to observational association testing, we performed MR analyses to assess the potential causal effect of alcohol consumption both in the overall population and in subpopulations stratified by alcohol intake frequency. We confirmed that heavy alcohol consumption could increase measures of obesity and risk of type 2 diabetes. On the other hand, results from MR analyses indicated that there may not be protective effects of modest alcohol consumption on obesity traits and type 2 diabetes.

Consistent with previous studies (7–11), observational evidence indicated nonlinear (ie, U-shaped or J-shaped) trends of associations between alcohol consumption and obesity outcomes. Specifically, light-to-moderate drinkers on average had a lower BMI and WHR, and were less likely to be obese and to develop type 2 diabetes compared to nondrinkers and heavy drinkers. However, these associations may be confounded by various lifestyle, socio-environmental, and behavioral factors and may be subject to reverse causation (12–14). For example, it is unlikely that alcohol intake frequency of participants in this study, all aged 40 years or older at recruitment, would have a causal effect on adult height, yet statistically significant observational associations were detected among light-to-moderate drinkers. Accounting for potential confounders may help to mitigate such bias as indicated in our analyses. However, accurately measuring all confounding effects for a complex risk factor is hardly achievable at the population level. For instance, dietary habits may confound the relationship between alcohol intake and cardiometabolic outcomes, yet they are difficult to quantify.

<span id="page-7-0"></span>On the other hand, our MR analyses by design could largely reduce the risk of confounding effects and reverse causation. The use of variants affecting alcohol dehydrogenase genes to construct the genetic instrument could further reduce any bias due to horizontal pleiotropy. Specifically, alcohol dehydrogenase metabolizes alcohol to acetaldehyde, which then breaks down to acetate. The aforementioned alcohol intake-reducing alleles can lead to more efficient alcohol dehydrogenase, thereby accelerating the accumulation of the toxic acetaldehyde. This results in facial flushing, nausea, and increased heart rate, and directly protects against heavy drinking ([31](#page-9-0), [37](#page-9-0), [38\)](#page-9-0). Through MR, we found that genetically increased alcohol intake frequency was associated with increased BMI, WHR and risk of obesity for individuals having more than 7 drinks per week. Although both FM and FFM were predicted to increase as a result of increased alcohol intake, the potential effect on FM was substantially stronger. Genetically increased alcohol intake frequency was significantly associated with increased risk of type 2 diabetes for individuals having more than 14 drinks per week, while suggestive evidence of risk increment was also observed among individuals having more than 7 and 14 or fewer drinks per week. These adverse effects appeared to be consistent between individuals younger than 60 years and those 60 years or older, but may be stronger in women than in men. Based on these findings, we may hypothesize that obesity, one of the leading risk factors for type 2 diabetes ([39, 40](#page-9-0)), plays a mediating role in the association between alcohol intake and type 2 diabetes among heavy drinkers. However, mediation analyses for multiple complex exposures in nonlinear MR require methodological innovations. Future investigations to elucidate the relationship between alcohol intake, adiposity, and type 2 diabetes may be warranted.

<span id="page-7-2"></span><span id="page-7-1"></span>Importantly, even though the genetic instrument was sufficiently strong, we did not observe any evident protective effects of increasing alcohol intake on any health outcomes under investigation for light-to-moderate drinkers having 7 or fewer drinks per week. It is known that an MR estimate may be biased toward confounded observational associations [\(41](#page-9-0)), when both the genetic instrument-exposure association and the genetic instrument-outcome association are estimated in the same study population. However, although most observational associations for light-to-moderate drinkers were statistically significant, all MR estimates in these 2 strata, more than 0 and 3.5 or fewer drinks per week and more than 3.5 and 7 or fewer drinks per week, overlapped with the null. This suggests that even if there exists a protective effect of light-to-moderate alcohol consumption, the magnitude of such an effect would be small, given that the population strata created in this study had a minimum sample size of approximately 18 000. These findings were strongly supported by sensitivity analyses using the doubly ranked stratification approach, where no MR estimate suggested a protective effect of light-to-moderate alcohol consumption.

Our study has important limitations. First, all findings in this study were based on European ancestry participants from the UK Biobank. It has been shown that the UK Biobank participants were healthier, less obese, and less likely <span id="page-7-4"></span><span id="page-7-3"></span>to smoke and drink compared to the general population in the United Kingdom [\(42](#page-9-0)). Meanwhile, the genetic instrument identified from a meta-analysis of GWASs predominantly of European ancestry may have limited generalizability. For instance, alcohol intake frequency in East Asian ancestry populations can be strongly influenced by polymorphisms in the aldehyde dehydrogenase gene *ALDH2* ([30,](#page-9-0) [43](#page-9-0)), where aldehyde dehydrogenase catalyzes the oxidation of aldehyde to acetate. However, these polymorphisms rarely affect European ancestry populations. Hence, we believe that our work should be replicated in populations of diverse genetic ancestries and different demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. Our exploratory age-stratified analyses may introduce survivorship bias and cautions must be taken when interpreting these results, although MR estimates appeared to be largely consistent between individuals younger than 60 years and those 60 years or older. Moreover, while correct causal direction (alcohol intake-to-health outcome) was supported for all MR analyses, we did not conduct a formal assessment of reverse causation through reverse MR. This can be enabled by largescale GWASs of health outcomes in population strata defined with respect to alcohol intake frequency. Last, to ensure statistical power of nonlinear MR analyses, we did not create a narrower stratification of the population. We posit that improved characterization of dose-dependent effects of alcohol consumption may be pursued in future studies with a larger sample size, in particular, for ascertaining detrimental or protective effects of light-to-moderate alcohol intake that are weak. Future investigations should also warrant evaluating the effects of alcohol consumption on noncardiometabolic disease outcomes.

In conclusion, human genetics evidence supports that heavy alcohol consumption can lead to increased BMI, WHR, faster accumulation of body fat, and can increase risks of obesity and type 2 diabetes. These effects may be stronger in women than in men. On the other hand, light-to-moderate alcohol consumption may not protect against these adverse health outcomes.
