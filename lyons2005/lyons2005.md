# **Health and Financial Strain: Evidence from the Survey of Consumer Finances**

Angela C. Lyons\* and Tansel Yilmazert

Using data from the Survey of Consumer Finances, this study examines the relationship between health status and financial strain controlling for the dual endogeneity between the two factors. Simultaneous two-stage probit models are estimated for self-reported health status and three measures of financial strain. The results from all three models indicate that poor health significantly increases the probability of financial strain. There is little evidence that financial strain contributes to poor health. The findings suggest that severe health conditions may result in larger financial burdens while large financial burdens are unlikely to accelerate a decline in health status. In the end, health may be contributing to widening financial disparities, especially among the poor who are in poor health.

#### **1. Introduction**

A large body of literature has examined the relationship between health and various socioeconomic factors such as education, income, and wealth.' These studies find a strong positive correlation between health and socioeconomic status (SES). Yet, there is little consensus on the direction of causality, and strong evidence exists that poor health can be both a cause and consequence of SES just as SES can be a cause and consequence of poor health.

On the one hand, some studies find that poor health affects socioeconomic status such that individuals who are in poor health work fewer hours or are unemployed, limiting their ability to accumulate income and wealth (e.g., Ettner 1996; Smith and Kington 1997a, b; Zagorsky 1999; Wu 2003). These studies typically find that serious health conditions have a larger affect on SES than less serious conditions. Other studies show that lower SES results in poor health (e.g., Caplovitz 1974;

### *2.* The Model

The interaction between socioeconomic status and health can be illustrated using the standard economic model of health (Grossman 1972). This model consists of an intertemporal utility function, a health production function, and a budget constraint. The intertemporal utility function represents preferences for an individual's level of health and consumption of another good at each time period. An individual's level of health at any point in time depends on (i) medical care and other factors that infiuence an individual's health such as diet, smoking, physical activities, and psychological stress; (ii) personal attributes such as age, family background, genetics, and education; and (iii) bealtb in the previous period. In this model, financial strain may lead to poor health for at least two reasons. As previously mentioned, it may increase an individual's psychological stress and/or result in fewer economic resources being devoted to health.

Similarly, health status may also affect the financial position of the household. As discussed in Smith (1999), individuals with poor health often work fewer hours or are perhaps unemployed. A reduction in labor supply decreases an individual's future eamings capacity and limits the household's ability to accumulate assets and, in tum, savings. Wbile poor health reduces current income and savings, it also increases out-of-pocket medical expenses. In such cases, savings may be depleted fairly quickly, placing financial strain on the household, which can threaten its overall financial well-being.

In the standard model, an individual's level of health depends on botb current *and* past health behaviors, personal attributes, income and wealth holdings, and prices. A past negative shock on the financial well-being of a family (e.g., economic recession) can be harmful to an individual's current health status, especially if it results in a decrease in the household's economic resources and the level of resources that can be devoted to current health. Poor health that results from a past negative shock can lead to a deterioration of the household's financial position and an increase in the household's financial strain. As previous research has suggested, this additional financial strain may affect health, which may in tum affect financial strain, and so on.

We conclude that, in terms of the financial strain measures we considered in this article, poor health has the largest effect on the percentage of delinquent households. Since one-third of the households in our sample has liquid assets/income < 0.25, the elasticity for this financial strain measure is small, even though its marginal effect is the largest among the three financial measures we considered.

In terms of the marginal effect of financial strain on poor health, we continue to observe at all education levels that the impact of financial strain is not significant for most measures of financial strain. The only exception is that the marginal effect of being delinquent is positive and significant for those with a high school education or less, perhaps suggesting that the impact of financial strain on poor health may be more important for those with lower levels of education and thus lower incomes. It is important to acknowledge that while the impact of financial strain on health is statistically insignificant, the numbers may have some economic significance. Note that, for those with a high school education or less, the effects of health on financial strain and financial strain on health are potentially of equal magnitude.

### *5.* **Conclusions**

Using cross-sectional data from the Survey of Consumer Finances, this study moves beyond traditional measures of socioeconomic status and examines the relationship between the overall financial position of the household and the self-reported health status of its head. Two-stage probit models have been estimated for the health status of the household head and three measures of financial strain controlling for the fact that financial strain can be both a cause and a consequence of poor health. The results are robust across all three measures such that poor health appears to significantly increase the likelihood of financial strain, indicating that serious health conditions may result in larger financial burdens. There is little evidence that financial strain contributes to poor health suggesting that it is unlikely that large financial burdens accelerate a decline in health status.

Using a more robust conceptualization of socioeconomic status, this study supports previous research that shows that the direction of causality is primarily from health to socioeconomic status rather than socioeconomic status to health (e.g.. Smith and Kington 1997a, 1997b). Those most likely to be affected are low-to-middle income families, especially the poor who are already in poor health. These results have important policy implications, especially with respect to the poor. It is well documented within the literature that the poor have worse health on average and a lower life expectancy than the nonpoor even after controlling for differences in socioeconomic status (Zagorsky 1999). If the gap in health inequality is contributing to household financial problems, it is likely also contributing to the growing gap in income and wealth inequality.

Those with lower incomes who are in poor health may find themselves in a vicious cycle. Their poor health affects their capacity to earn income and accumulate assets, which adds to their financial strain and affects their access to quality health care and ultimately their overall quality of life. The end result may be an increase in the level of dependency on government assistance and a reduction in overall household welfare. Providing more affordable and quality health care services to the poor may result in improved health outcomes and ultimately help to improve the economic well-being of the poor, especially those with serious health problems.

As with all studies that investigate the link between health and socioeconomic status, establishing a causal link between these two factors is difficult. While this study attempts to control for the endogeneity between financial strain and health, it has its limitations. Recall that we are using cross-sectional rather than longitudinal data. With cross-sectional data it is difficult to establish which came first—poor health or financial strain. The onset of a health condition may exacerbate already existing financial problems. Or, an already existing health problem may build up over time and result in increased financial stress. For this reason, we may be finding that financial strain does not significantly affect health because we are looking at individual points in time and not at how financial strain has accumulated over time to affect health.

With this said, the measures of financial strain used in this study were constructed to identify households that were under serious financial strain and had a greater likelihood of being insolvent. The financial problems facing these households did not happen overnight and likely provide an adequate reflection of how financial strain may have accumulated over time. In any case, the timing may be irrelevant since, regardless of whether it is a preexisting or new health condition, we find that poor health significantly contributes to household financial problems.

Recall also that we do not have information on individual's specific health conditions, especially those with serious health problems. It may be the case that financial strain may significantly increase the probability of poor health for those with serious health conditions. Even our results provide some evidence of support that financial strain can affect health for those with lower levels of education (i.e., the low income/poor). Those with lower levels of education are more likely than those with higher levels of education to be in poor health. Along these same lines, serious health conditions may result in larger increases in financial strain than those reported in this study. A growing body of research shows that serious health conditions can lead to significant and large declines in wealth (Smith 1998, 1999; Wu 2003).

Finally, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of the instruments. It is possible that our instruments are impacted by a number of "third factors" (e.g., early childhood SES) that drive both current financial strain and health habits. For this reason, one must be somewhat cautious in interpreting the findings.

As research moves forward in this area, longitudinal data collected specifically for the purpose of analyzing the relationship between household finances and health would be helpful, especially in addressing the issue of timing between health and financial strain. Research is also needed to investigate in more detail how household financial strain should be defined and measured. Does it matter whether a household is financially strained to the point that their portfolio puts them in a position of being insolvent? At what point does "financial strain" affect the health of the household? Does that point vary for different members of the household? Finally, a better understanding of the relationship between financial strain and health across individuals is needed, especially for those who are predisposed to illness and those with serious health conditions.
