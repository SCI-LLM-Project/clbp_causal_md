# Fear of Pain and Pain Intensity: Meta-Analysis and Systematic Review

#### Tobias Markfelder and Paul Pauli University of Würzburg

The fear avoidance model (FAM) represents a cognitive–behavioral explanatory approach for pain chronification. The core assumption is that fear of pain (FOP) following an acute pain experience facilitates the development of pain chronification, disability, and receding functionality. Thus, the model predicts a positive association between FOP and pain intensity in pain patients, which was frequently investigated; however, results were inconsistent. To highlight inconsistencies, we performed integrative statistical analysis aimed at evaluating the strength of the cross-sectional relation between FOP and pain intensity in clinical research and reviewing its moderation through demographic, pain-specific and psychosocial characteristics. To this end, we searched the databases PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science and included 253 independent effect size estimates (*N* - 42 463). The overall mean effect size was computed based on a random-effects model. By utilizing the artifact distribution method, we supplemented it with an analysis correcting for artifacts. The magnitude of the positive association equated to the threshold between a small to medium effect size, which was expected as the FAM predicts an indirect relation only. The association turned out to be stable across different FOP measures, but was significantly moderated by age, pain localization, first-time pain episode, pain onset, treatment status, and anxiety sensitivity. A potentially necessary differentiation of patient subgroups and suggestions for an adjusted methodological approach of future research are discussed.

#### *Public Significance Statement*

The chronification of pain is assumed to be crucially modulated by fear of pain, that is, pain catastrophizing leads to fear of pain and in the long run to pain-related disability. The inferred assumption that fear of pain is positively associated with pain reports in pain patients is confirmed by this meta-analysis. This suggests that successful pain treatment should target and moderate fear of pain, especially—as the performed moderator analyses revealed—in pain patients being of old age, having previous pain episodes, and having radiating or peripheral pain.

*Keywords:* fear of pain, kinesiophobia, meta-analysis, pain intensity, pain-related anxiety

Recent statistics about the prevalence of chronic pain and its consequences point to an immense societal problem; for example, a significant proportion of the population in most developed countries (6.8% to 50%) experiences some form of chronic pain [\(Turk](#page-36-0) [& Swanson, 2007\)](#page-36-0). Back pain, as the most common chronic pain localization [\(Breivik, Collett, Ventafridda, Cohen, & Gallacher,](#page-25-0) [2006\)](#page-25-0), has a lifetime prevalence of up to 84%, with 10% to 15% of the pain experiences persisting [\(Balagué, Mannion, Pellisé, &](#page-24-0) [Cedraschi, 2012;](#page-24-0) [Fourney et al., 2011;](#page-27-0) [McBeth & Macfarlane,](#page-31-0) [2002\)](#page-31-0). Contemporary psychological theories reject the outdated purely nociceptive explanation of the individual pain experience [\(Flor, Birbaumer, & Turk, 1990\)](#page-27-1) and emphasize the influence of psychosocial mechanisms of pain development, for example, negative emotions and biased cognitive processes [\(Gatchel, Peng,](#page-27-2) [Peters, Fuchs, & Turk, 2007;](#page-27-2) [Staats, Hekmat, & Staats, 1996\)](#page-35-0). In the context of musculoskeletal pain, the fear avoidance model (FAM) can be considered both most advanced and influential [\(Crombez, Eccleston, Van Damme, Vlaeyen, & Karoly, 2012;](#page-26-0) [Pfingsten & Schöps, 2004\)](#page-33-0).

# **Fear Avoidance Model**

The FAM offers a cognitive–behavioral explanation of why some acute musculoskeletal pain experiencers do not recover but struggle with ongoing pain [\(Lethem, Slade, Troup, & Bentley,](#page-30-0) [1983\)](#page-30-0). An advanced version of the model postulates a selfcontained, consecutive sequence of constructs, which unidirectionally follow each other, that is, pain catastrophizing leads to FOP and in the long run to pain-related disability [\(Vlaeyen & Linton,](#page-37-0) [2000\)](#page-37-0). The reference to the FAM in the guidelines for the prevention of low back pain [\(Burton et al., 2006\)](#page-25-1) and therefrom derived therapy elements emphasize its relevance [\(Baez, Hoch, & Hoch,](#page-24-1) [2018;](#page-24-1) [Vlaeyen, Morley, Linton, Boersma, & de Jong, 2012\)](#page-37-1).

Based on novel findings, the original model is continuously refined incorporating additional constructs and alternative path arrangements (e.g., [Asmundson & Taylor, 1996;](#page-23-0) [Crombez et al.,](#page-26-0) [2012;](#page-26-0) [Curtin & Norris, 2017;](#page-26-1) [López-Martínez, Ramírez-Maestre,](#page-31-1) [& Esteve, 2014;](#page-31-1) [Norton & Asmundson, 2003;](#page-32-0) [Pincus, Vogel,](#page-33-1) [Burton, Santos, & Field, 2006;](#page-33-1) [Schütze, Rees, Preece, & Schütze,](#page-34-0) [2010;](#page-34-0) [Turk & Okifuji, 2002;](#page-36-1) [Wideman et al., 2013\)](#page-37-2).

However, most FAM proposals consider FOP to play a prominent role. The initial definition of FOP as kinesiophobia, that is, an excessive, irrational fear of physical movement and activity [\(Kori,](#page-29-0) [Miller, & Todd, 1990\)](#page-29-0), relates it to the traditional concept of a phobia and anxiety disorders. Despite new measurement instruments differently accentuating the construct [\(McCracken, Zayfert,](#page-31-2) [& Gross, 1992;](#page-31-2) [McNeil & Rainwater, 1998;](#page-31-3) [Waddell, Newton,](#page-37-3) [Henderson, Somerville, & Main, 1993\)](#page-37-3), its understanding is still linked to phobia, as for example research methods [\(Roelofs, Pe](#page-34-1)[ters, Zeegers, & Vlaeyen, 2002;](#page-34-1) [Schoth, Nunes, & Liossi, 2012\)](#page-34-2) and therapy strategies [\(Bailey, Carleton, Vlaeyen, & Asmundson,](#page-24-2) [2010;](#page-24-2) [Jamani & Clyde, 2008\)](#page-29-1) are deducted from phobia research. Similar to phobic fear, FOP is considered to be the result of Pavlovian and operant conditioning, that is, interoceptive or proprioceptive stimuli (conditional stimuli [CS]), which are associated with a pain experience (unconditional stimulus [US]), will elicit a conditioned fear response (CR; [De Peuter, Van Diest, Vansteen](#page-26-2)[wegen, Van den Bergh, & Vlaeyen, 2011;](#page-26-2) [Vlaeyen & Linton,](#page-37-4) [2012\)](#page-37-4). Fear causes defensive mobilization characterized by selective attention, increased arousal, and safety seeking escape/avoidance behavior, which influence pain intensity and in the long run increase the likelihood of pain chronification. Besides, observational learning [\(Helsen, Vlaeyen, & Goubert, 2015\)](#page-28-0) and verbal instruction [\(Vlaeyen et al., 2009\)](#page-37-5) may foster such associations. Escape and avoidance behaviors (R) are strengthened via a reduction or nonappearance of the conditioned fear response (CR), and the conditioned fear response (CR) is maintained (negative reinforcement; [De Peuter et al., 2011;](#page-26-2) [Vlaeyen & Linton, 2012\)](#page-37-4). However, some researchers called for a redefinition of FOP with less reference to the concept of a phobia [\(Bunzli, Smith, Schütze,](#page-25-2) [& O'Sullivan, 2016;](#page-25-2) [Crombez et al., 2012;](#page-26-0) [Wideman et al., 2013\)](#page-37-2).

The underlying assumption that FOP develops after pain experiences and potentially influences succeeding pain experience suggests a positive relation between FOP and pain intensity (FOP–PI relation). Therefore, this correlation has been interpreted frequently as evidence for the validity of FOP measures, although there still exists no coherent definition which correlation is realistic and necessary to validate a FOP measure [\(McCracken &](#page-31-4) [Dhingra, 2002;](#page-31-4) [McCracken et al., 1992;](#page-31-2) [Simons, Sieberg, Carpino,](#page-35-1) [Logan, & Berde, 2011;](#page-35-1) [Waddell et al., 1993\)](#page-37-3). We expect that this meta-analysis, which considers various measures representing FOP, will lead to a benchmark for future validations.

# **FOP–PI Relation**

Findings that FOP and pain intensity significantly predict each other in the short term suggest a close relation [\(Vangronsveld,](#page-36-2) [Peters, Goossens, & Vlaeyen, 2008\)](#page-36-2), and several theoretical models as well as structural equation models assume a direct relation without any other interposed constructs [\(Asmundson & Taylor,](#page-23-0) [1996;](#page-23-0) [Gheldof et al., 2010;](#page-28-1) [López-Martínez et al., 2014;](#page-31-1) [Martin,](#page-31-5) [Halket, Asmundson, Flora, & Katz, 2010;](#page-31-5) [Pincus et al., 2006;](#page-33-1) [Thibodeau, Fetzner, Carleton, Kachur, & Asmundson, 2013\)](#page-35-2). However, according to the FAM model by [Vlaeyen, Kole-Snijders,](#page-37-6) [Boeren, and van Eek \(1995\),](#page-37-6) FOP and pain intensity are not directly related but indirectly via pain catastrophizing and a cascade of events related to defensive responding and/or negative affect. Explicit hypotheses regarding the relation ranged from positive without further specification [\(Askary-Ashtiani, Ebrahimi-](#page-23-1)[Takamejani, Torkaman, Amiri, & Mousavi, 2014;](#page-23-1) [Coons, Had](#page-26-3)[jistavropoulos, & Asmundson, 2004;](#page-26-3) [de Souza, Marinho, Siqueira,](#page-26-4) [Maher, & Costa, 2008;](#page-26-4) [Keogh, McCracken, & Eccleston, 2006\)](#page-29-2) to positive with moderate strength [\(Inrig, Amey, Borthwick, & Bea](#page-29-3)[ton, 2012;](#page-29-3) [Wei et al., 2015\)](#page-37-7). Based on the outlined indirect FOP–PI relation and the multitude of potentially intermediate factors we expect at best a medium effect size.

# **Moderators of the FOP–PI Relation**

The literature suggests a wide variety of moderators of the FOP–PI relation, aside from pain catastrophizing; however, a combined moderator analysis is still required. Therefore, this meta-analysis also examined constructs which were repeatedly linked to the FAM as possible moderators. Revealing the strength and validity of such moderators seems highly relevant for research and practice. Moderators may help to differentiate facets of the heterogeneous construct of FOP and to substantiate modifications of the FAM. We examined moderators belonging to four research areas: type of FOP-instrument, demographic characteristics of the sample, pain-related characteristics, and relevance for FAM or other psychosocial constructs.

### **Type of Instrument**

Various psychometric measures represent or at least distinctly overlap with the construct FOP [\(McCracken, Gross, Aikens, &](#page-31-6) [Carnrike, 1996;](#page-31-6) [Pincus, Smeets, Simmonds, & Sullivan, 2010\)](#page-33-2); for example, fear of pain [\(McNeil & Rainwater, 1998\)](#page-31-3), pain-related anxiety [\(McCracken et al., 1992\)](#page-31-2), fear-avoidance beliefs [\(Waddell](#page-37-3) [et al., 1993\)](#page-37-3), and pain-related fear of movement/(re)injury or kinesiophobia [\(Kori et al., 1990\)](#page-29-0). These measures are based on assumptions referring to differences between fear and anxiety [\(Barlow, 2000;](#page-24-3) [Carleton & Asmundson, 2009;](#page-25-3) [Carleton, Asmund](#page-25-4)[son, Collimore, & Ellwanger, 2006;](#page-25-4) [Larsen, Taylor, & Asmund](#page-30-1)[son, 1997;](#page-30-1) [Leeuw et al., 2007;](#page-30-2) [Zvolensky, Goodie, McNeil,](#page-38-0) [Sperry, & Sorrell, 2001\)](#page-38-0), diverging fear and anxiety responses [\(Hugdahl, 1981;](#page-29-4) [McCracken et al., 1996;](#page-31-6) [Vowles, Zvolensky,](#page-37-8) [Gross, & Sperry, 2004\)](#page-37-8), heterogeneous target populations regarding age [\(McCracken et al., 1992;](#page-31-2) [Pagé, Fuss, Martin, Escobar, &](#page-33-3) [Katz, 2010\)](#page-33-3) or pain localization [\(Swinkels-Meewisse, Swinkels,](#page-35-3) [Verbeek, Vlaeyen, & Oostendorp, 2003;](#page-35-3) [Turk, Robinson, Sher](#page-36-3)[man, Burwinkle, & Swanson, 2008\)](#page-36-3), varying designs [\(Turk et al.,](#page-36-3) [2008\)](#page-36-3), inhomogeneous theoretical foundation [\(Lundberg, Grimby-](#page-31-7)[Ekman, Verbunt, & Simmonds, 2011;](#page-31-7) [Wideman et al., 2013\)](#page-37-2), and diverse pain-specific differences [\(Bunzli, Smith, Watkins, Schü](#page-25-5)[tze, & O'Sullivan, 2015;](#page-25-5) [Burwinkle, Robinson, & Turk, 2005;](#page-25-6) [Meroni et al., 2015;](#page-32-1) [Walton & Elliott, 2013\)](#page-37-9). Here we examine the FOP–PI relation for all FOP scales, related scales, and their subscales, which we could identify on the basis of meta-analyses and reviews in the field. This general approach allows to clarify the meaningfulness of reported differences between measures (e.g., Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia [TSK] vs. Fear of Pain Questionnaire [FPQ; [Dehghani, Sharpe, & Nicholas, 2010\]](#page-26-5) or TSK versus Pain Anxiety Symptoms Scale [PASS; [Peters, Vlaeyen, & Weber,](#page-33-4) [2005\]](#page-33-4)) or between subscales of one measure (e.g., PASS-subscales [\[Coons et al., 2004;](#page-26-3) [Peters et al., 2005;](#page-33-4) [Sánchez, Martínez, Miró,](#page-34-3) [& Medina, 2011;](#page-34-3) [Vowles et al., 2004\]](#page-37-8), TSK-subscales [\[Wei et al.,](#page-37-7) [2015\]](#page-37-7), or Fear Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire-subscales [FABQ; [Al-Obaidi, Beattie, Al-Zoabi, & Al-Wekeel, 2005;](#page-23-2) [Cleland, Fritz,](#page-25-7) [& Childs, 2007;](#page-25-7) [Crombez, Vlaeyen, Heuts, & Lysens, 1999;](#page-26-6) [Gay,](#page-27-3) [Horn, Bishop, Robinson, & Bialosky, 2015;](#page-27-3) [Rostami et al., 2014\]](#page-34-4)). Significant distinctions would advance the differentiation of the relational structure of the measures and their subscales.

# **Demographic Features**

**Sex.** Sex differences regarding the FOP–PI relation were revealed with higher correlations for males than females regardless of equal FOP severity [\(Edwards, Augustson, & Fillingim, 2000;](#page-27-4) [McCracken & Houle, 2000;](#page-31-8) [Robinson et al., 2005;](#page-34-5) [Vowles et al.,](#page-37-8) [2004\)](#page-37-8). Related research pointed to a closer relation between pain intensity and anxiety for males than females [\(Edwards, Augustson,](#page-27-5) [& Fillingim, 2003;](#page-27-5) [Ramírez-Maestre & Esteve, 2014;](#page-33-5) [Riley, Rob](#page-34-6)[inson, Wade, Myers, & Price, 2001\)](#page-34-6). Similarly, experimental pain studies with healthy participants revealed a stronger relation both between FOP and pain intensity [\(Frot, Feine, & Bushnell, 2004;](#page-27-6) [Thibodeau, Welch, Katz, & Asmundson, 2013\)](#page-36-4) and between general anxiety and pain intensity for males compared with females [\(Dickens, Jayson, & Creed, 2002;](#page-26-7) [Fillingim, Keefe, Light, Booker,](#page-27-7) [& Maixner, 1996;](#page-27-7) [Jones, Zachariae, & Arendt-Nielsen, 2003\)](#page-29-5). A recent study reported that such effects depend on the used FOP measures with pain intensity correlating with the PASS-20 in males and with the TSK in females [\(Kreddig & Hasenbring, 2017\)](#page-30-3). Therefore, our moderator analysis regarding sex was performed for all FOP measures together as well as separately for PASS and TSK.

**Age.** Age may moderate the FOP–PI relation for several reasons. First, [Cappucci and Simons \(2015\)](#page-25-8) suggested that children and adolescents are especially susceptible to emerging FOP and pain chronification due to an enhanced interoceptive awareness. Second, younger age is associated with stronger pain catastrophizing [\(Turner, Mancl, & Aaron, 2004\)](#page-36-5), which was proposed to be a precursor of FOP [\(Leeuw et al., 2007\)](#page-30-2). Third, elderly pain patients seemed to have relatively less FOP and a rather indifferent approach to personal pain experiences [\(Cook, Brawer, & Vowles,](#page-26-8) [2006\)](#page-26-8), and a subgroup of elderly pain patients appeared to have a conspicuous affective impairment in relation to pain intensity [\(Cook & Chastain, 2001\)](#page-26-9). Finally, [Kovacs et al. \(2007\)](#page-30-4) presumed that elderly populations suffer from more serious comorbidities than just pain and therefore show a reduced FOP and pain intensity association. Based on these findings, the FOP–PI relation was expected to show greater effect sizes for childhood and adolescence and smaller effect sizes for old age, both compared with young and middle adulthood.

**Nationality.** [Kovacs et al. \(2007\)](#page-30-4) assumed differences in FOP, particularly fear avoidance beliefs, between southern European compared with Anglo American and northern European populations because of a stronger influence of pain intensity compared with fear avoidance beliefs on disability and quality of life for the former [\(Kovacs et al., 2004;](#page-30-5) [Kovacs, Abraira, Zamora, & Fernán-](#page-30-6) [dez, 2005;](#page-30-6) [Kovacs, Muriel, et al., 2005\)](#page-30-7), whereas the opposite seems true for the latter [\(Camacho-Soto, Sowa, Perera, & Weiner,](#page-25-9) [2012;](#page-25-9) [Fritz, George, & Delitto, 2001\)](#page-27-8). Therefore, we considered nationality as a moderator of the FOP–PI relation.

# **Pain Characteristics**

**Treatment status.** FOP is influenced by the anticipated pain experience [\(Ciccone & Just, 2001;](#page-25-10) [Murphy, Lindsay, & Williams,](#page-32-2) [1997;](#page-32-2) [Vlaeyen & Linton, 2000\)](#page-37-0), which may vary with treatment status. FOP–PI correlations were found to be lower at the start compared with after completion of a treatment [\(Benore, D'Auria,](#page-24-4) [Banez, Worley, & Tang, 2015;](#page-24-4) [Goldfinger, Pukall, Gentilcore-](#page-28-2)[Saulnier, McLean, & Chamberlain, 2009;](#page-28-2) [Kamper et al., 2012;](#page-29-6) [Kernan & Rainville, 2007;](#page-29-7) [Staerkle et al., 2004\)](#page-35-4) presumably owing to an unrealistic low or high pain anticipation before treatment [\(Bunzli, Smith, Schütze, & O'Sullivan, 2015\)](#page-25-11), which becomes adjusted during treatment. Therefore, we expect a stronger FOP–PI relation when assessed before compared with after treatment.

**Pain region.** Originally the FAM specifically addressed back pain [\(Vlaeyen & Linton, 2000\)](#page-37-0), and some FOP measures were exclusively tailored for back pain sufferers [\(Waddell et al., 1993\)](#page-37-3). Nonetheless, FAM and FOP were also examined for other pain locations, for example, orofacial pain [\(Randall et al., 2017\)](#page-33-6), neck pain [\(Bot et al., 2005;](#page-24-5) [Nederhand, Ijzerman, Hermens, Turk, &](#page-32-3) [Zilvold, 2004;](#page-32-3) [Vangronsveld, Peters, Goossens, Linton, &](#page-36-6) [Vlaeyen, 2007\)](#page-36-6), shoulder and arm pain [\(George & Hirsh, 2009;](#page-28-3) [George & Stryker, 2011;](#page-28-4) [Hart et al., 2009;](#page-28-5) [Kromer, Sieben, de Bie,](#page-30-8) [& Bastiaenen, 2014;](#page-30-8) [van der Windt, Kuijpers, Jellema, van der](#page-36-7) [Heijden, & Bouter, 2007\)](#page-36-7), hand fractures [\(Keogh, Book, Thomas,](#page-29-8) [Giddins, & Eccleston, 2010\)](#page-29-8), trigger finger [\(Kennedy, Vranceanu,](#page-29-9) [Nunez, & Ring, 2010\)](#page-29-9), lower extremity pain [\(George & Stryker,](#page-28-4) [2011;](#page-28-4) [Hart et al., 2009;](#page-28-5) [Piva et al., 2009;](#page-33-7) [Ross, 2010\)](#page-34-7), heel pain [\(Cotchett, Lennecke, Medica, Whittaker, & Bonanno, 2017\)](#page-26-10), headache [\(Black, Fulwiler, & Smitherman, 2015\)](#page-24-6), dyspareunia [\(Thom](#page-36-8)[tén & Linton, 2013\)](#page-36-8), vestibulodynia [\(Desrochers, Bergeron,](#page-26-11) [Khalifé, Dupuis, & Jodoin, 2009;](#page-26-11) [Ekdahl, Ida, Engman, & Linton,](#page-27-9) [2017\)](#page-27-9), and complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS; [de Jong,](#page-26-12) [Vlaeyen, de Gelder, & Patijn, 2011\)](#page-26-12). Comparisons between back and neck pain patients indicated differences in FOP severity [\(Ulug˘,](#page-36-9) [Yakut, Alemdaroglu, & Yilmaz, 2016\)](#page-36-9) and the relations between FOP, pain intensity, and disability [\(Bunketorp, Lindh, Carlsson, &](#page-25-12) [Stener-Victorin, 2006\)](#page-25-12). Other studies reported a weaker FOP–PI relation for cervical pain compared with lower back pain patients [\(Cleland et al., 2007;](#page-25-7) [George, Fritz, & Erhard, 2001\)](#page-28-6), leading to speculations that either the used FOP measure does not capture the specific neck pain experience or that the FAM is not generally valid because of disorder-specific differences in anxiety and avoidance response; in consequence, the use of a neck-specific FOP measure was advised [\(Pedler & Sterling, 2011\)](#page-33-8). Similarly, the role of the FAM for shoulder and limb pain was discussed [\(George &](#page-28-3) [Hirsh, 2009;](#page-28-3) [Vlaeyen & Morley, 2004\)](#page-37-10). [Kromer et al. \(2014\)](#page-30-8) assumed limb pain to be related to a distinct body sensation and pain specific cognitions due to its peripheral location, and several studies indeed yielded lower FOP–PI correlations for peripheral pain compared with central pain; for example, back pain [\(Mintken,](#page-32-4) [Cleland, Whitman, & George, 2010;](#page-32-4) [Simon, Stryker, & George,](#page-35-5) [2011;](#page-35-5) [van Baar, Dekker, Lemmens, Oostendorp, & Bijlsma, 1998\)](#page-36-10). Therefore, we expected the effect size for the FOP–PI relation to be higher for central pain locations, that is, back pain, compared with peripheral pain locations, for example, cervical pain.

**Pain duration.** The effect of pain duration on FOP and the FOP–PI relation is unclear. FOP may be inherently present without current pain experience [\(Linton, Buer, Vlaeyen, & Hellsing, 2000\)](#page-30-9) or it may have emerged during an initial acute pain episode [\(Boersma & Linton, 2005;](#page-24-7) [Sieben, Vlaeyen, Tuerlinckx, & Porte](#page-35-6)[gijs, 2002;](#page-35-6) [Vangronsveld et al., 2008\)](#page-36-2). Thus, some studies found somewhat more severe FOP for acute pain patients [\(George et al.,](#page-28-6) [2001\)](#page-28-6), others for chronic pain patients [\(Askary-Ashtiani et al.,](#page-23-1) [2014;](#page-23-1) [Grotle, Vøllestad, & Brox, 2006;](#page-28-7) [Grotle, Vøllestad, Veierød,](#page-28-8) [& Brox, 2004;](#page-28-8) [Laufer, Abu Elheiga-Na'amne, & Rozen, 2012;](#page-30-10) [Mintken et al., 2010\)](#page-32-4). Studies directly comparing FOP–PI correlations reported significant correlations for chronic populations only [\(Ciccone & Just, 2001;](#page-25-10) [Gómez-Pérez, López-Martínez, &](#page-28-9) [Ruiz-Párraga, 2011;](#page-28-9) [Grotle et al., 2004\)](#page-28-8). Consequently, we hypothesized that the FOP–PI effect size is especially strong for chronic pain samples.

**First-time pain episode.** The common definition of acute pain is restricted to the duration of the current episode [\(Merskey &](#page-32-5) [Bogduk, 1994\)](#page-32-5). However, previous pain episodes influence the current pain experience [\(Sieben, Portegijs, Vlaeyen, & Knottnerus,](#page-35-7) [2005\)](#page-35-7) as do previous, especially unsuccessful treatment efforts [\(van der Windt et al., 2007\)](#page-36-7). Consequently, previous pain episodes may lessen the FOP–PI relation. Hence, we expected an especially strong FOP–PI relation in the case of first-time pain sufferers.

**Type of pain onset.** Based on findings of relatively higher FOP-values in pain patients with a sudden compared with a gradual pain onset [\(Crombez, Vlaeyen, et al., 1999;](#page-26-6) [George et al.,](#page-28-6) [2001;](#page-28-6) [Nederhand et al., 2004;](#page-32-3) [Vlaeyen, Kole-Snijders, Rotteveel,](#page-37-11) [Ruesink, & Heuts, 1995\)](#page-37-11), [Cleland et al. \(2007\)](#page-25-7) discussed whether the process of pain chronification differs between these patient groups. Confirming this assumption, a higher order analysis of the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK-11) revealed that at least one item is only suitable for neck pain sufferers with a sudden pain onset [\(Walton & Elliott, 2013\)](#page-37-9). Consequently, we analyzed the type of pain onset as a moderating variable.

# **FAM Moderators**

The FAM allows to speculate about multiple conceivable moderators which possibly influence the FOP–PI relation. The examined moderators included pain catastrophizing [\(Sullivan et al.,](#page-35-8) [2001\)](#page-35-8), depression [\(Leeuw et al., 2007\)](#page-30-2), anxiety sensitivity [\(Reiss,](#page-33-9) [1991\)](#page-33-9), hypervigilance [\(Peters, Vlaeyen, & Kunnen, 2002\)](#page-33-10), subjective disability [\(Zale, Lange, Fields, & Ditre, 2013\)](#page-38-1), pain acceptance [\(McCracken & Eccleston, 2005\)](#page-31-9), and pain-related selfefficacy [\(Ayre & Tyson, 2001\)](#page-24-8). Although only longitudinal data will allow conclusive testing of the FAM, we still think that cross sectional data is useful too. This opinion is strengthened by daily diary recordings of FOP and pain intensity suggesting that both events occur without time lag [\(Sieben et al., 2002\)](#page-35-6) and that reciprocal daily prediction of each other pointed to a relevant short-term relation [\(Vangronsveld et al., 2008\)](#page-36-2). Therefore, we think that the performed analyses using cross-sectional data is still useful to examine the outlined FAM moderators [\(Black et al.,](#page-24-6) [2015;](#page-24-6) [Cappucci & Simons, 2015;](#page-25-8) [Cook et al., 2006;](#page-26-8) [Fish, Hogan,](#page-27-10) [Morrison, Stewart, & McGuire, 2013;](#page-27-10) [Gay et al., 2015;](#page-27-3) [Martin et](#page-31-5) [al., 2010;](#page-31-5) [Söderberg et al., 2015;](#page-35-9) [Zale et al., 2013\)](#page-38-1).

# **Method**

#### **Identification of Studies**

**Search strategies.** We utilized multiple search strategies to identify articles for this meta- analysis, that is, we searched PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science databases incorporating all studies published until May 26, 2016 (including online first publications). We searched titles, abstracts, and keywords for terms based on the names of the preselected FOP measures and their respective abbreviations (see [Appendix Table A1](#page-38-2) for search strategies). We also checked the included studies for additional yet not discovered citations (cross-referencing). Finally, all studies citing the original publication of any FOP measure or particular influential publications were collected via the above mentioned databases and Google Scholar.

**Abstract processing.** Application of the keyword search and cross-referencing identified 5,362 studies, which we imported into Endnote citation manager. After eliminating 2,864 duplicate articles, we checked titles and abstracts of the remaining 2,498 articles for relevant content. The following criteria were necessary for further inclusion: (a) the study reported empirical data; (b) the research was quantitative; (c) the language was English or German; (d) the abstract named pain or fear or any related construct (e.g., anxiety, catastrophizing, and disability); (e) the publication was peer-reviewed. We excluded 669 irrelevant abstracts, which resulted in 1,829 potentially relevant articles.

**Article processing.** We obtained the 1,829 residual articles as digital copies and closely reviewed them to identify relevant studies employing measures for FOP and pain intensity as well as providing effect size measures of their relation or sufficient information to compute an effect size measure. This evaluation was not possible for 195 articles which we could not access directly. Of these, we could identify authors of 173 publications and asked them for either a digital copy of their publication or a summary of the relevant data. Some provided the results of a subsequently computed correlation analysis which was not part of the original publication.

We filtered the remaining 402 articles according to the following specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, which considered target population characteristics and methodological features to ensure comparability of studies: (a) the study population suffered pain at the time of assessment which also could be experimentally induced pain simulating the clinical pain, that is, a back pain patients asked to lift an object. Healthy samples with experimentally induced pain and samples drawn from the general population were excluded; (b) the pain experience was not manipulated by interventions, that is, by systematically administering an analgesic; (c) both FOP and pain intensity were analyzed independently, that is, not merged with other measures to composite scores; (d) single item questionnaires of FOP were rejected as we intended to compare commonly used measures and their underlying constructs and single items would have been difficult to assign to a construct; (e) pain intensity and FOP were measured at the same time; (f) partial or semipartial correlations as a result of multiple or hierarchical regressions were omitted; (g) a sample was included only once [\(Beelmann & Bliesener, 1994;](#page-24-9) [Hunter & Schmidt, 2004\)](#page-29-10); consequently, we applied an adapted heuristic by [Wood \(2008\)](#page-38-3) to all potentially relevant source samples to uncover the recycling of data (see [Table 1\)](#page-4-0). If we inferred multiple studies to be at least partially based on the same sample we hierarchically checked three criteria to determine one of them for inclusion in the meta-analysis, which incorporated a holistic coverage of FOP or pain intensity, a larger sample size, and an earlier date of publication.

[Figure 1](#page-5-0) provides the flow diagram based on the PRISMA Statement [\(Moher, Liberati, Tetzlaff, & Altman, 2009\)](#page-32-6). Finally, we included data from 227 studies (*N* - 42,463) in the metaanalysis.

# **Selection of FOP and Pain Intensity Measures**

The meta-analysis did not aim at incorporating all measures which claim to reflect the construct FOP or any part of its facets, as a joint interpretation of such results would have been complicated, but focused on established measures which were identified by considering reviews, meta-analyses and relevant textbooks [\(Antony & Stein, 2009;](#page-23-3) [Asmundson, Norton, & Norton, 1999;](#page-23-4) [George, 2006;](#page-27-11) [Keefe, Rumble, Scipio, Giordano, & Perri, 2004;](#page-29-11) [Lundberg et al., 2011;](#page-31-7) [McCracken et al., 1996;](#page-31-6) [Turk & Wilson,](#page-36-11) [2010;](#page-36-11) [Zale et al., 2013\)](#page-38-1). More recently published measures [\(George, Valencia, Zeppieri, & Robinson, 2009;](#page-28-10) [Jensen, Ward,](#page-29-12) [Thorn, Ehde, & Day, 2017;](#page-29-12) [Monticone, Vernon, Brunati, Rocca, &](#page-32-7) [Ferrante, 2015;](#page-32-7) [Neblett, Mayer, Hartzell, Williams, & Gatchel,](#page-32-8) [2016;](#page-32-8) [Springer, George, & Robinson, 2016\)](#page-35-10) were not considered because they are not yet established and have rarely been applied.

[Table 2](#page-5-1) gives an overview of all included FOP and pain intensity measures. Information about structure and content of every measure can be inferred from its original publication. We included only pain intensity measures which specifically assessed the intensity of pain, for example, "How much pain do you have right now?". This includes measures that enquire pain intensity at certain points in time (e.g., last 24 hr, last week) and during certain activities (e.g., during walking, during sitting). We did not consider questions, which assess interference or impact on functioning.

#### **Selection of Moderators**

Based on our literature review, we identified and developed hypotheses about relevant moderators of the FOP–PI relation (see Introduction), which we analyzed as follows:

**Type of instrument.** We analyzed every FOP measure and its subscales categorically, but admitted only subscales in accordance with the most common factor structure to allow for a joint contentrelated interpretation. In case of an alternative factor structure, we used the total value of a FOP measure only.

**Demographic features.** As part of the categorical moderator analysis we compared samples of solely females (*k* - 17) and males (*k* - 6). Because of a low number of effect sizes for the male sex, we additionally analyzed the proportion of females within a study as a continuous moderator (*k* - 249). As [Kreddig and](#page-30-3) [Hasenbring \(2017\)](#page-30-3) explicitly reported diverging moderating effects of sex for the PASS and the TSK we repeated the continuous moderator analysis individually for the relationship between these instruments and pain intensity.

Following our formulated hypotheses regarding age (see the Introduction), we defined three stages of age, that is, childhood and adolescence until an age of 18 years (*k* - 14), young and middle adulthood from 19 to and 64 years of age (*k* - 222), and old age starting from 65 years (*k* - 11). **Pain characteristics.** We examined treatment status effects by comparing homogenous samples regarding two distinct categories, *treatment seeking* (examined at the initial visit of a pain treatment facility, k = 181) versus *in treatment* (examined after completed clinical diagnostics, a final diagnosis and the introduction of some form of therapy, k = 14).

We tested pain duration effects by means of one categorical and two continuous variables. The categorical variable was *acute pain* (pain duration < three months, k = 25) versus *chronic pain* (pain duration > three months, k = 146; Merskey & Bogduk, 1994; Nachemson & Andersson, 1982). The examined continuous variables were the proportion of the sample with an acute pain duration < three months (k = 202) and the mean pain duration (k = 159); the latter analysis avoids the controversial time-referenced definition of acute versus chronic pain (Dunn, Von Korff, & Croft, 2012; Verhaak, Kerssens, Dekker, Sorbi, & Bensing, 1998).

The analysis of pain region effects contrasted samples with back pain (k=124) versus headache and shoulder and limb pain (k=27); samples were included if the respective region was reported to be the primary pain location. We also entered the proportion of people with back pain into metaregression to include also numerous inconsistently affected samples (k=185). Another continuous moderator was the proportion of people with pain radiating into neighboring body locations (k=39). Regarding the moderating effect of different locations along the back, we compared samples with primary cervical (k=25) versus primary lumbo-sacral pain (k=90).

We analyzed the proportion of people with a first-time pain episode in the current body location as a continuous moderator (k = 19).

We analyzed type of pain onset effects continuously on the basis of the proportion of patients with a sudden pain onset (k = 46); a categorical comparison was not possible because of an insufficient number of samples with a gradual onset only (k = 3).

**FAM moderators.** We tested the moderating effects of the FAM-related constructs pain catastrophizing (k = 81), depression (k = 86), anxiety sensitivity (k = 7), hypervigilance (k = 10), subjective disability (k = 131), pain acceptance (k = 12), and pain-related self-efficacy (k = 8) using metaregression.

#### **Coding Study Characteristics**

Coded variables were ordered by the categories demographic characteristics, pain characteristics, FAM-related measures, FOP measures and pain intensity measures, and statistical information. Demographic characteristics were proportion of female sex, predominant nationality (determined by the country of recruitment), age (measured on a continuous scale, using only the reported mean), and age range. Pain characteristics were mean pain duration (measured on a continuous scale, using only the reported mean), proportion of acute pain sufferers (defined as pain lasting shorter than three months), treatment status, proportion of patients with first-time pain episode only, pain location (according to the taxonomy of the International Association for the Study of Pain, Merskey & Bogduk, 1994), proportion of major pain locations (head, shoulder and upper limbs, lower limbs, back, cervical back, lumbo-sacral back), proportion of radiating pain, and proportion of sudden pain onset. Regarding FAM-related questionnaires we coded type of measure and reported means. The included variables were broad-ranging; the requirements for inclusion were that the source authors stated in their method section that the measure represents a particular construct and that it was utilized in at least five studies for that purpose (see Table 3 for an overview of all measures). For the *FOP and pain intensity measures* we gathered type of measure, intercorrelations between measures, and Cronbach's alpha as an indicator of internal consistency. With respect to *statistical information* we coded sample size, correlation coefficient, type of correlation and, in the absence of an effect size estimate concerning the relationship, other statistical values that we could use to compute a point-biserial correlation coefficient, for example, p values, t values, t values, and standardized mean differences (i.e., Cohen's t). In the case of pairwise comparisons, we noted the quantitative ratio of subgroups to compute a coefficient which corrected for artificial dichotomization.

If the method section indicated that correlation analyses were conducted but no results were reported or in the case of inconsistencies, we tried to contact the source author and requested further information. Only in case of unclarified studies, we took other measures as outlined in the next section or labeled the data as missing.

We formally tested the reliability of our coding frame on a random sample of 20 studies to provide external validity for our coding. Because of varying levels of measurement of variables, we computed Krippendorff's  $\alpha$  reliability estimate; 42 of 44 variables (95,45%) have reliabilities  $\alpha \ge .800$  and thus indicate reliable agreement ( $M_{\alpha} = .98$ ,  $SD_{\alpha} = .10$ ).

In addition, we developed grading criteria, to assess the quality of studies. See Appendix Table A2 for a description of criteria. Implications for statistical analysis and interpretation of results are referred to where appropriate.

#### **Overview of Analytic Strategies**

**Overall meta-analytic model.** We decided for a random-effects model to compute an effect size estimate for the FOP–PI relation. Source studies varied distinctly in multiple features so that fixed-effects model assumptions, that is, functionally identical studies sharing a common effect size, were not met (Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, & Rothstein, 2010; Hedges & Vevea, 1998).

The performed meta-analyses followed the procedure delineated by Hunter and Schmidt (2004). The first analysis cumulated correlation coefficients across studies while correcting for sampling error only and delivered the average sample size weighted effect size  $(\bar{r})$ . The second analysis corrected correlation coefficients for artificial dichotomization individually for every study and for unreliability of both FOP and PI measures by using reliability distributions. It resulted in the average sample size weighted effect size corrected for artificial dichotomization and measurement error  $(\bar{p})$  as a population estimate. Measurement error was not corrected individually as only one third (33.5%) of the correlation coefficients was linked with sample specific reliability information for the FOP measure. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were computed as indicators of significance, that is, a nonoverlap with zero can be interpreted as a significant difference from zero associated with a Type I error of 5%.

Additionally, we constructed 90% credibility intervals around the average effect size corrected for all artifacts. If the credibility intervals overlap zero, true variance caused by moderators is indicated [\(Whitener, 1990\)](#page-37-19).

**Moderator analysis.** For categorical univariate moderator analyses we repeated the above procedure for every subgroup. We compared 95% confidence intervals between all subgroups of a potential moderator. We could assume a significant difference and thus a moderation if they were nonoverlapping. This parallel moderator analysis was tainted with a possible confounding of multiple moderators. We conducted a partial hierarchical moderator analysis to rule out an effect of confounding [\(Hunter &](#page-29-10) [Schmidt, 2004\)](#page-29-10). We narrowed it down to significant moderators according to the parallel analysis. A full hierarchical moderator analysis was restrained by the insufficient quantity of available effect sizes.

We tested continuous moderators by applying weighted least square-meta regression (method of moments) and examined every moderator individually. Subsequently, we simultaneously entered all significant moderators (*p* .05) into a regression model.

**Small-study effect.** The samples massively varied in size, ranging from 10 to 2,727 participants. We utilized two analyses to assess a potential bias by the small-study effect [\(Greco, Zangrillo,](#page-28-13) [Biondi-Zoccai, & Landoni, 2013\)](#page-28-13), for example, whether sample size significantly influenced the FOP–PI relation. First, we calculated a bivariate correlation analysis between sample size and effect size. Second, we realized a categorical moderator analysis and contrasted samples with a size equal to or smaller than the median (*N* - 100) with samples that were larger than the median. Because we expected no significant influence we lifted the level to - .20 and followed the approach of an equivalence test to reduce the type II error. Consequently, for the categorical moderator analysis we compared 80% confidence intervals to determine significance.

**Effect size measures.** [Hunter and Schmidt \(2004\)](#page-29-10) recommended restricting the included effect size measures to Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients. However, owing to numerous moderator variables and the ambition to perform a hierarchical categorical analysis and a multiple metaregression, we considered every applicable effect size measure as important. Therefore, we adopted several formula to convert parameters from pairwise comparisons like *d* [\(Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, & Rothstein, 2009;](#page-24-15) [Cohen, 1988;](#page-25-19) [Ellis, 2010;](#page-27-21) [Hedges, 1981\)](#page-28-14) and <sup>2</sup> values [\(Rosenthal](#page-34-14) [& DiMatteo, 2001\)](#page-34-14) as well as odds ratios [\(Borenstein et al., 2009\)](#page-24-15) into point-biserial correlation coefficients. These formulas are confined to pairwise comparisons with a single degree of freedom. In case of an excess quantity of degrees of freedom we either merged or removed subgroups to allow for the application of the above formula. One study had to be excluded as it only reported a *p* value but no direction of the relation [\(Kennedy et al., 2010\)](#page-29-9).

**Inclusion of subgroups.** In the case of separately reported information and statistical figures for subgroups of the overall population, every subgroup was incorporated into the metaanalysis as an independent population. In the case of both separately and mutually reported information and statistical figures, the approach was conditional on the criterion of the subgroup differentiation. [Hunter and Schmidt \(2004\)](#page-29-10) argued in favor of independently incorporating subgroups if the differentiating criterion is considered to be a moderator, that is, age or sex.

**Aggregation of effect sizes.** Only one effect size per population must be included in the meta-analysis [\(Beelmann & Bliesener,](#page-24-9) [1994;](#page-24-9) [Hunter & Schmidt, 2004\)](#page-29-10). We applied a formula provided by [Nunnally \(1978\)](#page-32-16) for aggregating multiple dependent effect sizes into a single composite. The formula requires knowledge of the intercorrelation between the pain intensity and FOP measures. Because not all studies reported the essential intercorrelation we established a distribution of intercorrelations based on all studies which are part of the meta-analysis. An unreported intercorrelation was substituted from the distribution. This procedure is regarded superior to averaging as a means of aggregating effect sizes [\(Beelmann & Bliesener, 1994\)](#page-24-9). The composite effect size was attributed the largest sample size of its constituting effect sizes. For the correction of the error of measurement, both sides of the composite effect size needed a reliability coefficient comparable to Cronbach's alpha of individual measures. If pain intensity or FOP was represented by a single measure its reliability was taken from the respective reliability distribution. Otherwise we generated a reliability coefficient using a formula by [Mosier \(1943\)](#page-32-17) as recommended by [Hunter and Schmidt \(2004\).](#page-29-10) If neither an intercorrelation was reported nor could be substituted from the distribution, effect sizes were averaged. In case of an averaged effect size Cronbach's alpha of the involved measures were averaged as well to compute a reliability coefficient.

We identified two cases of publications based on overlapping populations [\(Rusu, Kreddig, Hallner, Hülsebusch, & Hasenbring,](#page-34-17) [2014;](#page-34-17) [Swinkels-Meewisse, Roelofs, Verbeek, Oostendorp, &](#page-35-18) [Vlaeyen, 2006\)](#page-35-18), but the reported correlation coefficients referred to different measures. Consequently, we treated them as multiple correlation coefficients within one sample and aggregated them to a composite score. All the coded information stemmed from the study with the larger sample size.

**Substitution of insufficient statistical information.** In cases of correlation analyses without reporting correlation coefficients or corresponding *p* values, that is, only significant levels were reported, we intercalated a correlation coefficient. For three studies reporting a significant positive correlation coefficient based on a two-sided significance level of *p* - .05 [\(de Gier, Peters, &](#page-26-17) [Vlaeyen, 2003;](#page-26-17) [Guclu, Guclu, Ozaner, Senormanci, & Konkan,](#page-28-16) [2012;](#page-28-16) [Walker, Losco, Armson, Meyer, & Stomski, 2014\)](#page-37-20), correlation coefficients were derived from the significance level as a conservative substitute. Five studies that reported nonsignificant FOP–PI relations without information on the relations' directions [\(Aslan Telci, Yagci, Can, & Cavlak, 2013;](#page-23-11) [Filardo et al., 2016;](#page-27-22) [Pruneti et al., 2014;](#page-33-18) [van Wilgen, Stewart, Patrick Stegeman,](#page-36-16) [Coppes, & van Wijhe, 2010;](#page-36-16) [Walker et al., 2014\)](#page-37-20) were replaced by null findings.

*z***-transformation.** The majority of moderator variables was applicable for categorical or continuous analysis without further transformation. However, for questionnaires referring to the FAM constructs we applied *z*-standardization to make different questionnaires referring to the same construct comparable. We controlled that utilized rating scales were congruent for every measure, otherwise the reported mean was linearly transformed. We only performed *z*-standardization and analysis if a minimum of five reports per questionnaire were available to prevent an excessive fragmentation of a construct.

**Publication bias.** [Hunter and Schmidt \(2004\)](#page-29-10) recommended testing for a potential publication bias. We adopted four common procedures of file-drawer analysis. First, we computed a fail-safe *N* for every single meta-analysis using the formula provided by [Orwin \(1983\).](#page-32-18) It states how many missing studies with null findings would be necessary to reduce the mean effect size to a predefined level. We set this critical level to *rcritical* - .1, referring to a trivial effect size [\(Cohen, 1992\)](#page-25-20). Because of limited space only salient findings are reported. Second, a funnel plot was consulted as a visual indicator for publication bias [\(Richard & Pillemer,](#page-34-18) [1984\)](#page-34-18). Traditionally, it plots sample size versus effect size. Following recommendations by [Borenstein et al. \(2009\)](#page-24-15) we replaced sample size with standard error to make asymmetries more apparent. In the absence of publication bias, the plot is supposed to resemble a symmetrical inverted funnel. Asymmetries or skewed shapes are likely to occur due to a lack of studies with a small sample size reporting small effect sizes. These are less likely to be published than studies reporting significant effect sizes or having a considerable sample size [\(Hunter & Schmidt, 2004\)](#page-29-10). Third, exceeding the visual examination of the funnel plot we applied the trim-and-fill procedure [\(Duval & Tweedie, 2000\)](#page-26-18) as a rank-based, objective measure. It assumed that asymmetry of the funnel plot was completely caused by unpublished studies. Based on characteristic values for symmetry, it added single data points to achieve total symmetry. The resulting alteration of the overall effect size hinted at whether publication bias impinged on interpretation [\(Borenstein et al., 2009\)](#page-24-15). Fourth, we applied Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry. Please note however that publication bias should not be overestimated especially in the context of correlational meta-analysis [\(Dalton, Aguinis, Dalton, Bosco, &](#page-26-19) [Pierce, 2012\)](#page-26-19). In addition, we checked whether the effect size of the FOP–PI relation was unrelated to any hypotheses of the source study and thus should not be subjected to publication bias.

**Outlier analysis.** Following the critical reception of eliminating outliers and influential cases by [Hunter and Schmidt \(2004\),](#page-29-10) we renounced outlier and influence diagnostics for the estimation of the overall effect size as well as the categorical moderator analysis. However, weighted least square regression is especially susceptible to extreme cases, potentially distorting results of the continuous moderator analysis [\(Hunter & Schmidt, 2004\)](#page-29-10). To reveal bias in the metaregression we individually identified outliers and overly influential cases for all subgroups of the continuous moderator analysis. Subsequently, we repeated every analysis after their exclusion and discuss whether conclusions are considerably altered. We utilized studentized deleted residuals, DFFITS and Cook's distance as diagnostic measures [\(Viechtbauer, 2010\)](#page-37-21). Underlying criteria for extreme values were specified by [Viechtbauer](#page-37-22) [and Cheung \(2010\).](#page-37-22)

All analyses were conducted using adapted macros originally provided by [Field and Gillett \(2010\)](#page-27-23) and [Wilson \(2005\)](#page-37-23) for SPSS, as well as the metafor package for R [\(Viechtbauer, 2010\)](#page-37-21).

# **Estimation of the Average Correlation Between Pain Intensity and FOP**

The results of this analysis are summarized in Tables 5–8. The sample size weighted mean correlation amounted to  $\bar{r} = .28$ , which is equivalent to a small effect size according to the classification by Cohen (1992). Approximately 38% of the observed variance of effect sizes could be retracted to sampling error. The sample size

weighted mean correlation corrected for unreliability of both the FOP and PI instruments and artificial dichotomization of continuous variables amounted to  $\bar{p}=.32$ , which indicates a moderate effect size. A proportion of approximately 36% of the observed variance of corrected effect sizes was accounted for by sampling error, error of measurement, and artificial dichotomization. Considering that both ratios of the variance due to sampling error and all artifacts to the observed variance in the (corrected) effect sizes were far less than 75%, the existence of a true moderator is at least conceivable. A file-drawer analysis (fail-safe N) indicated 461 null findings to be required to lower the overall effect size of the FOP–PI relation to a trivial level. Therefore, with regard to this meta-analysis comprising only 253 samples the present result can be considered stable.

**Type of instrument.** For the different FOP instruments (i.e., PASS, FABQ, TSK, FOPQ) the sample size weighted mean correlations with PI were between  $\bar{r}=.25$  and .31, corrected for all artifacts between  $\bar{p}=.26$  and .33. Similarly, pairwise overlapping of 95% confidence intervals between all subscales of the PASS, FABQ, and TSK speak against moderating effects by distinctive subscales of these questionnaires.

**Sex.** A direct comparison between female only  $(\bar{r} = .27, \bar{p} = .31)$  and male only samples  $(\bar{r} = .33, \bar{p} = .40)$  yielded no hints on a moderating effect by sex. # Hierarchical Moderator Analysis and Multiple Meta-Regression

The performed hierarchical moderator analysis only considered subgroups that differed significantly in the categorical parallel moderator analysis. These were for the moderation by age the young and middle adulthood versus old age, and for the moderation by treatment status the treatment seeking versus being in treatment subgroups. After a hierarchical analysis identical to the parallel analysis, both treatment status subgroups were compared within both age subgroups. Regarding the young to middle adulthood subgroup the result mirrored the parallel analysis as the nonoverlapping 95% confidence intervals point to a significant moderation by treatment status with treatment seeking samples ( $\bar{r} = .28, \bar{p} = .32$ ), showing a weaker relation than samples in treatment ( $\bar{r} = .36, \bar{p} = .40$ ). In addition, for young to middle aged adults being in treatment all observed variance could be ascribed to sampling error, which made further moderation very unlikely. A comparison of treatment status subgroups within the old age made no sense due to an insufficient quantity of available

<sup>\*</sup> p < .05.

samples (k = 3). We conclude that moderation by treatment status is independent of the patients' age.

The performed multiple metaregression considered the three significant variables from linear metaregression, that is, proportion of back pain, proportion of radiating pain, and proportion of first-time pain episode. No result could be inferred because of a lack of samples for which all variables were coded (k=7). Borenstein et al. (2009) suggest a 10 to 1 ratio of studies to covariates, which our multiple metaregression falls below.

#### **Influence of Extreme Sample Size**

Study sample sizes varied from N=10 to 2,727 patients. The correlation between the sample size and the strength of the reported FOP–PI correlation coefficient was not significant, r=.04, p>.20. Overlapping 80% confidence intervals for both sample size weighted mean correlation and mean correlation corrected for all artifacts pointed to no significant difference between populations containing less than or equal to 100 participants (k=126,  $\bar{r}=.27$ ,  $\bar{p}=.30$ ) and populations consisting of more than 100 participants (k=127,  $\bar{r}=.29$ ,  $\bar{p}=.32$ ).

#### **File-Drawer Analysis**

The funnel plot for the full meta-analytic data set resembled a symmetrical inverted funnel. Consequently, there was no indication of a publication bias concerning nonsignificant studies with a small sample size. The trim-and-fill procedure intercalated 17 data points which were all located to the right of the sample size weighted mean correlation. Contradicting the typical pattern of publication bias (Borenstein et al., 2009), there was a lack of small to medium-sized studies with a significant correlation coefficient to form a completely symmetrical funnel. The alteration of the sample size weighted mean correlation from  $\bar{r}=.28$  to .29 was irrelevant for the overall interpretation. A nonsignificant Eggers's regression test indicated no funnel plot asymmetry (regression intercept = -0.8323, p=.4053). In addition, 40% of effect sizes (102/253) were unrelated to any hypotheses of their respective studies and thus should not be subjected to publication bias.

#### Discussion

The current meta-analysis on the association between fear of pain (FOP) and pain intensity (PI) is based on data from clinical pain samples summing up to more than 43,000 cases. The main goals were (a) to establish the magnitude of the FOP–PI relation and (b) to identify meaningful moderators of this relation based on differences between (sub-)populations.

#### **FOP-Pain Intensity Relation**

The effect size of the mean FOP–PI relation can be classified between small and moderate indicating a valid and relevant association. Multiple methods of file-drawer analysis suggests a stable effect, for example, 461 null findings are required to lower the overall effect size of the FOP–PI relation to a trivial level. However, there is sufficient unexplained variance suggesting that other constructs also have meaningful effects on both FOP and PI and their relation as the indirect association and the multitude of potentially intermediate factors suggest. Future studies may ex-

plore additional psychosocial constructs in the context of the FAM to better understand FOP and its relation with PI. Previous publications point to the importance of pain catastrophizing (e.g., Desrochers et al., 2009; Domenech, Sanchis-Alfonso, López, & Espejo, 2013; George, Calley, Valencia, & Beneciuk, 2011; George & Hirsh, 2009; Hirsh, George, Riley, & Robinson, 2007; Kennedy et al., 2010; Keogh et al., 2010; Woby, Roach, Urmston, & Watson, 2007), anxiety sensitivity (e.g., Asmundson & Taylor, 1996; Martin, McGrath, Brown, & Katz, 2007; McCracken & Keogh, 2009; Zvolensky et al., 2001), pain-related acceptance (e.g., McCracken, 1998; McCracken, Gauntlett-Gilbert, & Eccleston, 2010; McCracken, Gutiérrez-Martínez, & Smyth, 2013), depression (e.g., Kadimpati, Zale, Hooten, Ditre, & Warner, 2015; Knaster, Estlander, Karlsson, Kaprio, & Kalso, 2012), neuroticism (e.g., Goubert, Crombez, & Van Damme, 2004; Kadimpati et al., 2015), and psychological flexibility (e.g., McCracken & Vowles, 2007; Vowles, McCracken, Sowden, & Ashworth, 2014; Wicksell, Lekander, Sorjonen, & Olsson, 2010). #### **Moderator Analyses**

Using categorical moderator analysis and linear metaregression we identified multiple demographic (age) and pain-specific (treatment status, pain region, and first-time pain) characteristics which significantly moderate the FOP–PI relation. In addition, we ascertained that for a specific FOP measure (PASS) the FOP–PI relation was moderated by sex, and after the elimination of outliers found evidence that the type of pain onset and anxiety sensitivity were significant moderators too. In the following section, we discuss the theoretical significance of these findings.

Type of instrument. Our analyses revealed no differences between the examined FOP questionnaires (PASS, TSK, FABQ, FOPQ) regarding their relation to pain intensity and therefore confirm the construct validity of these FOP measures. Importantly, we also revealed no significant differences between the various subscales of FOP measures regarding their relation with pain intensity, a finding that questions several previous assumptions and reports. For example, we did not confirm the assumption that diverging components of anxiety and fear responses as assessed by the PASS subscales differentially relate to pain intensity (Coons et al., 2004; Goldfinger et al., 2009), and previous reports using stepwise regression that, despite comparable correlation coefficients between the PASS subscales and pain intensity, only the cognitive subscales explains a significant unique amount of variance of pain intensity (Sánchez et al., 2011; Vowles et al., 2004). Similarly, our analyses did not confirm previous reports of divergent relations between specific FABQ subscales and pain intensity (Al-Obaidi et al., 2005; Cleland et al., 2007; Crombez, Eccleston, [Baeyens, Van Houdenhove, & Van den Broeck, 1999;](#page-26-13) [Gay et al.,](#page-27-3) [2015;](#page-27-3) [Rostami et al., 2014\)](#page-34-4). These discrepancies between FABQ subscales may be explained by considering other variables, as some studies suggested that the FOP–PI relation depends on vocational factors such as employment status [\(Pfingsten, Kröner-](#page-33-12)[Herwig, Leibing, Kronshage, & Hildebrandt, 2000;](#page-33-12) [Swinkels-](#page-35-18)[Meewisse et al., 2006\)](#page-35-18), type of insurance [\(Cleland, Fritz, &](#page-25-27) [Brennan, 2008\)](#page-25-27), and financial support during occupational incapacity [\(Rostami et al., 2014\)](#page-34-4). Examining these work-related variables as moderators makes sense in view of the fact that the FABQ-W targets work-related cognitions [\(Waddell et al., 1993\)](#page-37-3).

Although we found no differences between FOP questionnaires or their subscales regarding the FOP–PI relation, the existing literature suggests that it is still necessary to further explore their applicability for specific subpopulations and bring them more into line with a consolidated theoretical FAM framework. In addition, associations between specific aspects of FOP and differential brain network activity in different pain subgroups may be considered; for example, differential amygdala-periaqueductal gray functional connectivity in low back pain patients (see [Meier et al., 2017\)](#page-32-26).

**Sex.** Neither the categorical analysis nor the metaregression indicate a moderation of the FOP–PI relation by sex. However, a specific metaregression for the PASS only confirmed previous PASS findings of an increased FOP–PI relation in males [\(Edwards](#page-27-4) [et al., 2000;](#page-27-4) [McCracken & Houle, 2000;](#page-31-8) [Robinson et al., 2005;](#page-34-5) [Vowles et al., 2004\)](#page-37-8), whereas the assumed increase TSK-PI relationship in females could not be confirmed [\(Kreddig & Hasen](#page-30-3)[bring, 2017\)](#page-30-3). We conclude that firm conclusions about sex effects on the FOP–PI relation require more data from males or larger samples with equal sex distributions as well as usage of multiple FOP measures.

**Age.** First, we confirmed the hypothesis of a weaker FOP–PI relation for the old aged as compared with young and middle adulthood. Thus, our results support that pain intensity and FOP at least partially uncouples with increasing age, although our data do not allow us to decide whether this is related to a more indifferent approach of the elderly regarding pain experiences [\(Cook &](#page-26-9) [Chastain, 2001\)](#page-26-9) or a loss of importance of FOP [\(Kovacs et al.,](#page-30-4) [2007\)](#page-30-4). The zero overlapping 90% credibility intervals of the old age pointing to the existence of a true moderator within this age group possibly is an artifact resulting from two intercalated null findings [\(Aslan Telci et al., 2013;](#page-23-11) [Filardo et al., 2016\)](#page-27-22). All studies in the old age group employed the standard version of a FOP measure. However, research is in disagreement regarding whether FOP measures should be applied without adaptations according to old age [\(Larsson et al., 2014;](#page-30-14) [Quint, 2007;](#page-33-28) [Quint, Raich, &](#page-33-29) [Luckmann, 2011;](#page-33-29) [Sions & Hicks, 2011\)](#page-35-29).

Second, we did not confirm the hypothesis of a stronger FOP–PI relation in childhood and adolescence versus young and middle adulthood. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as only 14 independent correlation coefficients were found for childhood and adolescence which were based on seven different FOP measures, and three of these measures were actually conceptualized for adults and employed either in their standard version [\(Astfalck, O'Sullivan, Straker, & Smith, 2010;](#page-24-19) [Keogh et al., 2006;](#page-29-2) [Wilson, Lewandowski, & Palermo, 2011\)](#page-37-29) or verbally simplified to be easier understandable for children [\(Martin et al., 2007\)](#page-31-23). Both methods are not adequate to depict the reality of life of children [\(Huguet, McGrath, & Pardos, 2011\)](#page-29-13).

We conclude that especially childhood and adolescence as well as old age require further research. Age distributed samples and well evaluated, age-adapted FOP measures are essential [\(Fisher,](#page-27-31) [Heathcote, Eccleston, Simons, & Palermo, 2018\)](#page-27-31).

**Nationality.** Our moderator analysis regarding hypothesized differences between southern European versus northern European and Anglo American countries yielded nonsignificant findings challenging a previous report of [Kovacs et al. \(2007\).](#page-30-4) Because the null-finding of [Kovacs et al. \(2007\)](#page-30-4) in southern European was based on a relatively old sample (mean age of 80.4 years), we think that age influences have to be considered as our meta-analysis points to a significantly weaker FOP–PI relation in the old aged (see above). At present, nationality effects on the FOP–PI relation have to be considered absent.

**Treatment status.** Our analyses revealed that the FOP–PI relation is significantly stronger for patients in treatment than for treatment seeking patients, and according to the hierarchical moderator analysis this moderation is not confounded by age. Thus, our results support the idea that pain treatment synchronizes the FOP–PI relation, possibly because of its effects on pain expectations. However, this effect may be related at least partially to pain pathology, as the treatment seeking samples mostly had back pain while the patients in treatment frequently had specific pain syndromes (e.g., complex regional pain syndrome, CRPS; [Simons et](#page-35-21) [al., 2016\)](#page-35-21), temporary pain conditions (e.g., postoperative pain; [George, Lentz, Zeppieri, Lee, & Chmielewski, 2012\)](#page-28-19), and progressive diseases (e.g., arthritis; [Lööf et al., 2015\)](#page-30-29). Indeed, [Vlaeyen](#page-37-4) [and Linton \(2012\)](#page-37-4) argue that the relevance of FOP may be different for nonpredictable, regional, or nonspecific pain syndromes owing to its primary evolution from conditioning. The inadequacy of the TSK for fibromyalgia patients, which led to an adjusted version, is indicative of that [\(Burwinkle et al., 2005\)](#page-25-6). Unfortunately, we could not realize a moderator analysis regarding diagnoses due to a lack of precise information on diagnosis. Thus, we suggest for future research to either recruit homogenous samples in terms of diagnosis or at least precisely report all occurring diagnoses.

**Pain duration.** First, neither our categorical moderator analysis nor the metaregression support previous reports of a stronger FOP–PI relation for chronically as compared with acutely affected pain populations. However, our analyses differentiated acute and chronic pain based on a common definition of pain duration [\(Merskey & Bogduk, 1994\)](#page-32-5), whereas a further subdivision of both acute [\(Sieben et al., 2002;](#page-35-6) [Vangronsveld et al., 2008\)](#page-36-2) and chronic pain [\(Boersma & Linton, 2005\)](#page-24-7) on the basis of more elaborated criteria may be considered by future studies. Second, our metaanalysis revealed indications that first-time pain patients versus patients with recurrent onset are characterized by a stronger FOP–PI relation. However, conclusions have to be cautious as the definition of an acute pain episode in the analyzed studies was usually restricted to its current duration without considering previous episodes. Thus, a comparison of acute and chronic pain patients each with their first pain episode would be more informative.

The previous reports pointing to a stronger FOP–PI relation for chronic pain patients [\(Ciccone & Just, 2001;](#page-25-10) [Gómez-Pérez et al.,](#page-28-9) [2011;](#page-28-9) [Grotle et al., 2004\)](#page-28-8) have to be questioned for several reasons. [Ciccone and Just \(2001\)](#page-25-10) lacked a clear inclusion criterion defining a first-time pain episode for their acute pain patients. Additionally, they employed the FABQ-W although their acute and chronic pain patients significantly differed in terms of their current occupational status. [Gómez-Pérez et al. \(2011\)](#page-28-9) defined acute pain as a pain episode lasting up to six months. On top of that, their acute and chronic pain populations were different in terms of their primary diagnosis with acute pain patients suffering exclusively from musculoskeletal pain whereas more than one third of their chronic pain patients had some sort of neuropathic pain. [Grotle et al. \(2004\)](#page-28-8) ensured a first-time pain episode but the moderation may have been provoked by selecting rather extreme groups regarding pain duration (under three weeks vs. over one year) or differing population characteristics, for example, proportion of radiating pain and proportion of sudden pain onset; both are suggested as moderators by our meta-analysis.

In sum, future research examining changes in the FOP–PI relation and different stages of fear avoidance owing to pain duration needs to specifically select and examine patients subdivided on the basis of pain duration but comparable in other variables.

**Pain region.** The performed categorical moderator analysis yielded a nonsignificant tendency in accordance with the hypothesis that the FOP–PI relation is stronger in patients with back pain versus pain in other peripheral regions, that is, limb pain and headache. This tendency was substantiated by the significant results for two continuous moderators, that is, the FOP–PI relation increased with the proportion of back pain among pain locations and decreased with the proportion of additional pain radiating from the center of the body. These findings support the assumption that bodily sensations and pain-related cognitions differ between patients with peripheral or central pain locations [\(Kromer et al., 2014\)](#page-30-8). They also support previous findings of differences between back pain patients with and without additional radiating pain regarding pain intensity, pain-related affect and behavioral coping strategies [\(Simmonds, Lee, Etnyre, &](#page-35-20) [Morris, 2012\)](#page-35-20).

Our meta-analyses revealed within the back pain sample no weaker FOP–PI relation for cervical pain as compared with lower back pain, as suggested by multiple previous studies [\(Bunketorp et](#page-25-12) [al., 2006;](#page-25-12) [Cleland et al., 2007;](#page-25-7) [Dedering & Börjesson, 2013;](#page-26-24) [George et al., 2001;](#page-28-6) [Misterska et al., 2015\)](#page-32-20). However previous studies are problematic because, for example, [Cleland et al. \(2007\)](#page-25-7) compared the FOP–PI relation of their cervical pain population with actually above average correlation coefficients for lower back pain, for which they missed to give a source. [George et al. \(2001\)](#page-28-6) allowed for multiple pain locations, and a subsequent study which applied the same FOP instrument but excluded multiple pain locations did not detect a difference between cervical and lower back pain patients [\(Simon et al., 2011\)](#page-35-5). The populations of [Bun](#page-25-12)[ketorp et al. \(2006\),](#page-25-12) [Dedering and Börjesson \(2013\),](#page-26-24) and [Misterska](#page-32-20) [et al. \(2015\)](#page-32-20) contained a considerable proportion of pain patients with radiating pain, and we speculate that this variable was crucial for the diverging results. These results encourage efforts for differentially considering pain locations [\(Huis 't Veld, Vollenbroek-](#page-29-28)[Hutten, Groothuis-Oudshoorn, & Hermens, 2007\)](#page-29-28).

Studies with experimental pain induction in various body regions [\(Bishop, Horn, George, & Robinson, 2011;](#page-24-28) [Parr et al., 2012\)](#page-33-30) also found bogy region dependent relationships between indices of experimental pain induction and psychosocial constructs such as FOP and anxiety sensitivity [\(Robinson, Bialosky, Bishop, Price, & George,](#page-34-29) [2010\)](#page-34-29). Thus, we recommend to consider the modulating role of pain region and radiating pain in FAM. In addition, a moderator analysis using experimental data to target effect of pain location on the FOP–PI relation seems promising.

**First-time pain episode.** We revealed an increased FOP–PI relation for first-time pain experiencers supporting the hypothesis that the first pain experience is especially important for the FOP–PI relation. This also implies that FOP-related cognitions are not completely generated anew with additional pain episodes. In line with this, research hinted at the persistence of FOP and its behavioral effects after the ease of a pain episode [\(Pedler & Sterling, 2011;](#page-33-8) [Thomas,](#page-36-29) [France, Lavender, & Johnson, 2008\)](#page-36-29). Some authors even argued in favor of selecting pain patients with their first time pain episode only for research on the FAM [\(Sieben et al., 2005\)](#page-35-7). This, however, contradicts the usual practice and likely restricts possible findings. For example, extinction of FOP via exposure does not generalize across different movements but strengthened the exception to the rule [\(Crombez et al., 2002\)](#page-26-29), and at the same time FOP seems to generalize beyond the originally affected pain location—at least in experimental research [\(Meulders et al., 2014;](#page-32-27) [Meulders, Vansteenwegen, &](#page-32-28) [Vlaeyen, 2011;](#page-32-28) [Niederstrasser et al., 2015;](#page-32-29) [Niederstrasser et al., 2014\)](#page-32-30). Because the first time pain episode compared with other continuous moderators explained the highest amount of variance, that is, 38.85%, we conclude that this variable has to be considered as relevant for all future studies.

**Type of pain onset.** Our analyses do not strongly support the assumption that a sudden pain onset is linked to a stronger FOP–PI relation [\(Cleland et al., 2007\)](#page-25-7) because we revealed a moderating effect of type of pain onset only after elimination of two outliers. A thorough examination of the source studies points to a crucial difference in the studied population which may explain findings: Sudden pain onset is almost exclusively linked to a specific, tissue damaging incident, for example, an accident [\(Bunketorp et al., 2006\)](#page-25-12), whereas gradually arising pain, for example, a hereditary pain syndrome, usually has no easily understandable cause [\(Celletti, Castori, La Torre, & Camerota,](#page-25-23) [2013\)](#page-25-23). Interviews with chronic back pain patients emphasized that they repeatedly ponder about the cause of the pain [\(Bunzli, Smith,](#page-25-11) [Schütze, et al., 2015\)](#page-25-11), suggesting that this may be a crucial variable affecting the FOP–PI relation. In addition and most importantly, sudden pain onset may be associated with symptoms of posttraumatic stress, which may play a crucial role within the context of the FAM [\(López-Martínez et al., 2014\)](#page-31-1). Experimental pain models indicate that posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is associated with increased pain sensitivity, [\(Moeller-Bertram et al., 2014\)](#page-32-31) which may be a moderator of the FOP–PI relation [\(Gay et al., 2015\)](#page-27-3). Thus, differences in pain sensitivity may relevant in explain the moderating effect of type of pain onset on the FOP–PI relation. This speculation is further supported by the result that even pain patients with a traumatic experience independent of pain showed an above average FOP–PI relation [\(Cho,](#page-25-25) [Heiby, McCracken, Moon, & Lee, 2011\)](#page-25-25). To substantiate this speculation we need research on the interaction of posttraumatic stress symptoms, pain sensitivity and the FOP–PI relation.

**FAM moderators.** Our analyses found no indication that any psychosocial and FAM-related constructs had a moderating effect on the FOP–PI relation before the exclusion of outliers. After exclusion of outliers, an increase in anxiety sensitivity (AS) was associated with a decrease in the FOP–PI relation. This finding supports efforts to incorporate AS into the FAM and explore its role for FOP [\(Asmund](#page-23-0)[son & Taylor, 1996;](#page-23-0) [Cappucci & Simons, 2015;](#page-25-8) [McCracken &](#page-31-30) [Keogh, 2009\)](#page-31-30). However, the observed moderation effect has to be treated cautiously because it emerged after the elimination of an outlier and was based on six independent effect sizes only. In addition, it may be questioned whether a continuous moderator analysis was suitable for the construct, as some research suggested a taxonomic structure [\(Bernstein et al., 2006;](#page-24-29) [Bernstein, Zvolensky, Weems,](#page-24-30) [Stickle, & Leen-Feldner, 2005\)](#page-24-30) or distinctive categories [\(Asmundson](#page-23-16) [& Norton, 1995;](#page-23-16) [Asmundson, Norton, & Veloso, 1999;](#page-23-7) [Esteve &](#page-27-32) [Camacho, 2008;](#page-27-32) [Keogh & Cochrane, 2002;](#page-29-29) [Keogh & Mansoor, 2001;](#page-29-30) [Watt, Stewart, Lefaivre, & Uman, 2006\)](#page-37-33). More research around AS in the context of FOP is necessary to gain a broader database and to apply more suitable methods of analysis.

No psychosocial or FAM-related construct was confirmed convincingly as a moderator of the FOP–PI relation. This unexpected result may be attributable to the analyses' exploratory nature or several methodological deficits. Many psychosocial and FAM-related constructs were represented by several measures, the number of aggregated measures ranged between 1 (AS, acceptance, vigilance) and 10 (disability), and for some measures or their subscales construct validity was questioned [\(Hirsh et al., 2007\)](#page-29-21). Nevertheless, our criterion of a minimum number of five independent applications ensured that a measure was not uncommon in the context of FOP research. Finally, we have to consider that the performed moderator analysis was restricted to cross-sectional data, although the FAM describes a development process [\(Vlaeyen & Linton, 2000\)](#page-37-0), indicating that correlations and moderations may appear in longitudinal data only. Thus, our findings strongly call for longitudinal studies on the FOP–PI relation.

#### **Limitations and Strengths**

Our meta-analysis has a comprehensive basis of 253 independent samples consisting of a total of *N* - 42,463 pain sufferers. Thus, its database exceeds a comparable meta-analysis regarding the relationship between disability and FOP [\(Zale et al., 2013\)](#page-38-1) to a considerable degree. However, the data are solely cross-sectional as the research of FOP lacks prospective designs [\(Wideman et al., 2013\)](#page-37-2). Consequently, causal interpretation of the results is not possible. Only prospective designs are able to solve the disagreement whether the FOP–PI relation is unidirectional and circular [\(Vlaeyen & Linton, 2000\)](#page-37-0) or bidirectional [\(Wideman et al., 2013\)](#page-37-2). Our literature search was focused on English and German publications only; however, the consensual language for important scientific publications is English and therefore we are convinced that overlooked studies published in other languages were few and very unlikely would affect our main conclusions. Because our primary goal was to examine the FOP–PI relation in general, we included all studies assessing FOP and PI, also studies with chronic and acute pain patients and studies with experimentally induced pain. The moderator analyses indicated no effect of these factors on the FOP–PI relation; however, we admit that the heterogeneity of studies included might lead to an underestimation of the FOP–PI relation in specific subgroups. Similarly, we acknowledge that the variety of treatments performed in the included studies might have affected our moderator analyses.

Our analyses did not focus on a specific or single FOP measure, because our aim was to compare multiple conceptualizations of FOP and as a result advance knowledge on the need to differentiate or merge FOP measures. However, all measures of FOP are based on self-report, although the ability to reflect upon and quantify one's own anxiety and fear is questioned [\(McNeil & Vowles, 2004\)](#page-31-31). Promising approaches are behavioral measures of FOP as the procedure introduced by [Verbunt, Huijnen, and Köke \(2009\),](#page-36-30) and brain imaging studies suggesting that specific brain responses and/or distinctive functional connectivity may be indicators of FOP [\(Meier et al., 2017\)](#page-32-26). We think that these approaches—when validated and utilized more frequently—will become important and promising indicators of FOP.

The performed moderator analysis is at risk of a confounding effect as categorical and continuous moderators were tested separately and not entered into a combined model. Categorical moderators were at least partially hierarchically analyzed to ensure independence of significant moderators; however, a full hierarchical moderator analysis was restrained by an insufficient quantity of available effect sizes. Similarly, continuous moderators were entered individually into a regression model, but a joint analysis of continuous moderators was obstructed by a lack of effect sizes. In addition, we estimated, by means of a second meta-analysis correcting for artifacts, a result under ideal testing conditions. To this end, we adopted the reliability distribution method by [Hunter and Schmidt \(2004\)](#page-29-10) due to a lack of individual reports of reliability of pain intensity and FOP measures on study-level. Finally, we examined specific hypotheses about potential moderators of the FOP–PI based on our screening of more than 20 years of research regarding FOP and the FAM.

# **Future Research**

The proportion of unexplained variance of the FOP–PI relation is indicative of the existence of additional moderators which were not tested as part of this meta-analysis. Because the first-time pain episode compared with other continuous moderators explained the highest amount of variance, that is, 38.85%, this variable has to be considered as highly relevant for future studies. We speculate that the first pain experience is especially important for the FOP–PI relation because it is crucial for FOP-related cognitions which at least partially survive the ease of the pain [\(Pedler & Sterling, 2011;](#page-33-8) [Thomas, France,](#page-36-29) [Lavender, et al., 2008\)](#page-36-29) and are not completely generated anew with additional pain episodes. Future research should also follow the suggestion of [Muris, Vlaeyen, and Meesters \(2001\)](#page-32-32) to differentiate between permanent or at least frequent pain and sporadic pain as the frequency of the pain experiences likely influences the development of FOP. Another important moderator may be the underlying or assumed reason for the pain. [LeMay et al. \(2011\)](#page-30-25) reason that cancer pain as compared with noncancer pain facilitates FOP as pain may be considered a crucial indicator of the potentially life-threatening disease progression by cancer patients. Therefore, a moderator analysis regarding various, specific diagnoses, including cancer, fibromyalgia and CRPS, which here failed due to few studies, has to be addressed in the future. The results of [Cleland et al. \(2008\)](#page-25-27) further suggest that the type of insurance, namely private health insurance and workers= compensation, moderates the FOP–PI relationship too. However, it is unclear whether this moderation is specific for the American health care system or can be transferred to other countries with divergent framework conditions for health insurance.

Recently some promising results [\(Gay et al., 2015\)](#page-27-3) indicated that pain sensitivity, which was operationalized as temporal summation of pain (TSP) from thermally induced pain, moderates the FOP–PI relation; a higher pain sensitivity was linked to a stronger relation. However, only very few studies of this meta-analysis dynamically captured pain sensitivity [\(Alappattu et al., 2015;](#page-23-13) [Gay et al., 2015;](#page-27-3) [George & Hirsh, 2009\)](#page-28-3). Therefore, research regarding the role of pain sensitivity within the context of the FAM needs to be intensified.

Considerable research attempted to place FAM in a motivational framework based on evidence that FOP and avoidance behavior depends on the context of goals. For example, [Claes, Karos, Meul](#page-25-29)[ders, Crombez, and Vlaeyen \(2014,](#page-25-29) [2015\)](#page-25-30) showed that avoidance behavior following a cue predicting pain may be attenuated by the presence of a valued competing goal. [Claes, Vlaeyen, & Crombez](#page-25-31) [\(2016\)](#page-25-31) further demonstrated that pain-related fear and pain avoidance behavior is modulated by cues predicting dissimilar or similar outcome. Thus, the FOP–PI relation might depend considerably on existing personal goals, which need to be assess to understand the FOP–PI relation.

The current meta- and moderator-analyses can only be considered a first step in the evaluation of FAM. As a next step, in seems important to test the potential pathways between FOP and PI discussed in the literature, and mediation analyses might be a valuable approach to achieve this. Some studies suggest a rather direct FOP–PI association. For example, [Arntz and Claassens \(2004\)](#page-23-17) demonstrated that the meaning people attach to a stimulus influences its experienced painfulness. Alternatively, the FOP–PI relation might be mediated by factors like attentional focus, appraisal, catastrophizing, arousal, or stress. For example, [Sullivan et al. \(2001\)](#page-35-8) argue that catastrophizing crucially contributes to pain-related fear, which then leads to avoidance of activity and subsequent disability. In turn, [Glombiewski et al.](#page-28-30) [\(2015\)](#page-28-30) presented evidence that in patients with lower-back pain asked to perform potentially harmful movements only those exhibiting high psychophysiological arousal indicated by strong increase in skin conductance response and interbeat-interval showed elevated fear of movement, pain-related anxiety, and catastrophizing. Related are findings that exposure to acute or repeated stress can produce maladaptive neurobiological changes in pathways associated with pain processing, resulting in stress-induced hyperalgesia [\(Jennings, Okine,](#page-29-31) [Roche, & Finn, 2014\)](#page-29-31). We suggest that future meta-analyses should examine if and to what extend these mediating factors contribute to the FOP–PI relation.

# **Conclusions**

We extensively examine the FOP–PI relation by means of multiple meta-analyses, offer an overview of the current state of knowledge in the field of FOP and FAM research, and provide innovative impulses for future research in terms of unifying the concept of FOP and differentiating pain patient subgroups. The overall result points to a small to moderate effect size of the FOP–PI relation, which makes other influences conceivable. In consequence, the FAM should be refined by incorporating the summarized findings and by introducing new promising moderators.
