# The Prevalence of Smoking and Implications for Quality of Life Among the Community-Based Elderly

Colleen J. Maxwell, MA John P. Hirdes, PhD

Although the adverse health consequences associated with smoking among middle-aged adults are well known, the significance of smoking among the elderly is less clear. We used data from three national surveys with representative samples of the noninstitutionalized Canadian population 15 years of age and older to investigate the prevalence and consequences of smoking among the elderly. The results showed a lower prevalence of smoking among older age groups, a higher prevalence and higher consumption rates among elderly men than among elderly women, a greater likelihood among elderly nonsmokers for men to be former smokers and for women never to have smoked, and lower consumption rates among elderly men and women than among younger individuals. Results from multi-

variate logistic regression models suggested an increased risk among elderly male and female smokers for poorer health ratings, respiratory problems, and selected medication use. These models also showed an increased risk for impairment in mobility and high life stress among elderly male smokers and for low happiness and dissatisfaction with social relationships among elderly female smokers, compared with persons who had never smoked. These preliminary findings suggest that smoking among elderly persons may be associated with several negative quality-of-life outcomes, but the direction of this relationship is not clear. Nonetheless, these results provide support for the initiation of smoking cessation programs for elderly individuals. [Am J Prev Med 1993;9:338–45]

The negative health effects of smoking among middle-aged adults are well known and have been discussed extensively in the literature. However, the effects of smoking among the elderly are less clear and subject to some debate. Most studies of smoking among older individuals have focused on smoking as a risk factor for mortality, and generally lower relative risks (RRs) for all-cause and cause-specific mortality (e.g., from cardiovascular disease and lung cancer) were reported for older age groups. Those results may reflect, at least in part, a selection effect through which a number of smokers are removed from the population by death at earlier ages, leaving a disproportionate number of relatively healthy smokers. Also, the higher death rates from all causes in those 75 years of age and older may produce a "ceiling effect" that reduces the RRs

For example, RRs among elderly smokers compared with nonsmokers vary between 1.5 and 2.0 for all-cause mortality<sup>7,10–13</sup> and between 1.5 and 2.4 for mortality resulting from coronary heart disease.<sup>4,9,11,14</sup>

The associations of smoking among the elderly with quality-of-life outcomes such as morbidity, disability, and well-being have received less attention in the literature. However, some have argued that smoking represents one of the "few remaining pleasures" for an elderly individual 16,17 and that smoking cessation would so profoundly reduce an older person's quality of life that the costs of the reduction in risk would far exceed the benefits. 18,19 Some investigations have suggested only weak associations of smoking among the aged with subsequent disability, 20 poor self-rated physical and emotional health, 21 and ischemic heart disease. 22 Others have reported significant associations of smoking with physical and emotional health, 23,24 and recent findings from several longitudinal and case-control studies suggest that older smokers continue to experience an increased risk for several illnesses, including cardiovascular dis-

ease,<sup>25–27</sup> cerebrovascular disease,<sup>28,29</sup> chronic bronchitis, peptic ulcers, and cancer of several organs.<sup>30</sup> Further, smoking among older individuals has also been associated with adverse physiological changes such as impaired pulmonary function, decreased bone mineralization, and reduced muscle strength.<sup>30</sup>

To address the policy implications of smoking among the elderly and the need for health promotion initiatives, it is useful no examine the magnitude of risk associated with the behavior and the size and attributes of the exposed population. Our analyses emphasize the possible associations of smoking with quality-of-life outcomes, since considerable evidence is already available regarding mortality. In addition, the prevalence of smoking among elderly Canadians is examined using data from three national surveys: the 1985 General Social Survey (GSS), the 1986 Survey of Smoking Habits (SSH), and the 1989 National Alcohol and Drug Survey (NADS). This provides an opportunity to replicate the findings reported for the United States using data from the Established Populations for Epidemiologic Studies of the Elderly (EPESE).<sup>23</sup> Such comparisons are sseful because they permit increased confidence in the generalability of findings to the population of interest.

## **METHODS**

Samples. The GSS, SSH, and NADS were conducted by Statisacs Canada and involved representative samples of the noninstitutionalized Canadian population 15 years of age and older living in the 10 provinces. The GSS was conducted in the fall of 1985 and involved telephone interviews for subjects 15-64 years of age and face-to-face interviews for subjects 65 years of age and older. The GSS includes information on the health stams (e.g., activity limitations, health satisfaction, and chronic health problems), health behaviors (e.g., smoking, alcohol use, physical activity), health care use, support networks, and social participation of the elderly. Random-digit dialing techniques were used to obtain the sample of persons 15-64 years. Indiwiduals 65 years and older were identified from randomly selected households used in the Canadian Labour Force Survey (LFS), which is the largest continuing household survey conducted by Statistics Canada. All Canadians 15-64 years are eligible for inclusion in the LFS, except residents of the Yukon and Northwest Territories, Indian Reserves, institutions, and the Armed Forces (about 2% of the population). The individuals who are cycled out of the LFS at 65 in various years can be

assumed representative of the community-based elderly population. Moreover, those 65 years of age and older were oversampled to increase the reliability of findings for this group. Of the 13,295 subjects selected, there were 2,095 nonresponses, for a response rate of 84.2%, and a final sample of 11,200 persons, including 3,130 elderly respondents. A comprehensive review of the GSS survey design and methodology appears elsewhere.<sup>31,32</sup>

The SSH was conducted as a supplement to the LFS in December 1986. Since 1964, supplements to the LFS have provided data on the smoking behavior of Canadian adults. The SSH used two of the six LFS rotation groups, and each person in the household in these rotation groups was asked about personal smoking habits. Approximately 95% of the interviews for the SSH were conducted by phone (the remainder in person), and about 30% of the responses were based on proxy information. In total, 32,500 persons were selected from 18,600 LFS households, and 30,799 responded to the SSH (including 4,135 individuals 65 years of age or more), yielding a response rate of about 95%. A more detailed description of the 1986 SSH appears elsewhere.<sup>33</sup>

The NADS was a telephone survey conducted in March 1989 to collect information on attitudes, knowledge, and behavior related to tobacco, alcohol, and drug use among Canadians. The NADS also includes information on health ratings, perceived satisfaction with social relationships, life stress, and various sociodemographic variables. Random-digit dialing was used to obtain a sample of households, and one respondent was randomly selected from all household members 15 years and older. Of the 14,791 households contacted, 11,634 persons responded (including 1,758 individuals 65 years of age or older), for a response rate of 78.7%. Further details on the methodology of the 1989 NADS have been reported elsewhere.<sup>34</sup> Table 1 summarizes the survey dates, unweighted sample sizes, response rates, and percentage of responses based on proxy interviews for each of the three national surveys.

Measures. Respondents to the GSS, SSH, and NADS who indicated that they smoked cigarettes daily at the time of the survey were classified as "current smokers," respondents who were not current smokers but who reported smoking cigarettes daily in the past were classified as "former smokers," and respondents who reported never having smoked cigarettes were classified as "never smokers." Those who reported themselves to be daily smokers were also asked to indicate the number of cigarettes smoked per day.

The sociodemographic characteristics examined included gender, age, marital status, level of education, and household income range (available for GSS and NADS respondents only). Where necessary, response categories for sociodemographic variables were collapsed to make the surveys more comparable.

Information regarding outcome variables such as health status, satisfaction with social relationships, and psychological well-being was available for the GSS and NADS but not for the SSH. Self-rated health was assessed for both GSS and NADS respondents. The GSS also included information related to satisfaction with health, lifetime history of heart trouble (including heart attack, angina, heart failure, or rheumatic heart disease) and hypertension, current respiratory problems (including asthma, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, persistent cough, or shortness of breath), and disability. The measures of disability examined were the number of disability days reported in the past two weeks and self-rated impairment in specific activities (i.e., trouble in walking 400 meters without resting, climbing stairs, carrying a 5-kilogram object 10 meters, standing for long periods of time, bending down to pick up an object from the floor, cutting toenails, using fingers to grasp or handle, and reaching above head). In addition, these specific activities were combined into a summary variable for the presence of any impairment. A derived overall activity limitation variable was available based on various impairments or restrictions in performing activities because of a long-term physical condition or health problem.

We also examined information on medication use in the NADS for elderly respondents. Respondents were asked about their medication use in the 30 days prior to the survey, and responses were collapsed into five general categories: analgesic medications (aspirin or similar pain reliever, codeine, demerol, or morphine), central nervous system medications (tranquilizers such as Valium, diet pills or stimulants, antidepressants, or sleeping pills), respiratory medications (allergy, cough, or cold remedies), cardiovascular medications (medicine for the heart or

blood pressure), and gastrointestinal medications (stomach remedies or laxatives).

The psychosocial variables examined for GSS respondents included self-rated happiness, life satisfaction, and overall level of satisfaction with family and friends. For NADS respondents, the variables examined included self-rated life stress and perceived helpfulness of family or friends.

Analysis. We analyzed the GSS, SSH, and NADS data using cross-tabulations by age and gender. To ensure valid population estimates, a weight variable is provided in the GSS, SSH, and NADS that adjusts for nonresponse and specifies the number of persons in the population the sampled individual represents (see Wong<sup>35</sup> for additional discussion). However, since the sample sizes for the weighted values in these data sets are very large, even small differences are likely to be statistically significant. Thus, to take the effects of weighting into account without inflating the sample size, we used a "down-weighted" variable in the bivariate and multivariate analyses, obtained by multiplying the sample weight by the ratio of the unweighted total sample size to the weighted total sample size (data available on request).

The bivariate associations between smoking and quality-of-life variables in the GSS and NADS were examined through unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We used logistic regression models with age, education, marital status, and household income included as covariates to estimate adjusted ORs and CIs for quality-of-life outcome measures associated with smoking at the bivariate level. We retained only covariates significant at the .05 level in the final models.

However, the prevalence of smoking was still greater than 20% for men 65–69 years of age in each of the surveys.

The percentage of current male smokers was generally highest in the GSS and lowest in the SSH. Conversely, the percentage of current female smokers was usually highest in the NADS and lowest in the SSH. In all three surveys, the older age groups of male respondents were mostly composed of former smokers, but for female respondents, persons who had never smoked constituted the largest group. Moreover, while the 15–44-year-old group had the largest percentage of persons who had never smoked among male respondents, the percentage of persons who had never smoked among women was generally higher among older age groups.

As shown in Table 3, most daily smokers reported smoking between 10 and 29 cigarettes per day, and few respondents indicated that they smoked 30 or more cigarettes daily. For both age groups (i.e., <65 and  $\ge 65$ ), men were more likely to be heavy smokers than women, and women were more likely to report being light smokers. In addition, individuals younger than 65 years of age were generally more likely to be heavy smokers, and those 65 and older were generally more likely to be light smokers.

# DISCUSSION

The results from all three surveys showed a lower prevalence of smoking among older age groups, a higher prevalence and higher consumption rates among elderly men than among elderly women, a greater likelihood among elderly nonsmokers for men to be former smokers and for women never to have smoked, and lower consumption rates among elderly men and women than among younger individuals. Although the associations were not always consistent for men and women, smoking was significantly related to several negative health and qualityof-life outcomes. For example, daily cigarette smoking was associated with an increased risk of poor self-rated health and respiratory problems among elderly men and women; with an increased risk of impairment in mobility, high life stress, and analgesic medication use among elderly men; and with an increased risk of low happiness, dissatisfaction with social relationships, and use of medications affecting the central nervous and gastrointestinal systems among elderly women. Moreover, these associations were independent of the effects of marital status, education, household income, and age.

The estimates of the prevalence of smoking among the elderly from the GSS, SSH, and NADS indicate that current smokers comprise a relatively small percentage of the elderly population, and the rates obtained are consistent with U.S. results reported for the EPESE.<sup>23</sup> However, among future elderly cohorts one might expect the percentage of female smokers to be larger, since recent cohorts of young women are less likely to have never smoked than previous cohorts.<sup>36–38</sup>

The somewhat higher prevalence rates of smoking in the NADS than in the GSS and SSH were unexpected, since prevalence rates have generally declined over time. One explanation may be that it was less socially undesirable to identify oneself as a smoker in the context of NADS questions that dealt with use of alcohol and licit and illicit drugs, whereas the SSH dealt only with smoking. Although the overall impact of proxy responses on estimates of smoking among elderly individuals remains uncertain, underestimation of smoking prevalence may have been more extensive in the SSH than in the GSS or NADS, since the SSH was the only survey in which proxy responses for smoking-related questions were allowed (see Table 1).

Gender differences in smoking behavior may affect the ability to detect associations with outcomes of interest. For example, nonsmokers may include a larger percentage of men who have quit smoking for health reasons than women. Also, the lower consumption rate for female smokers than for men (see Table 3) could lead to less pronounced declines in quality-of-life outcomes among female smokers because of lower lifetime exposure levels.

Age differences in the number of cigarettes smoked are also of interest, since these may lead to lower RRs of poor health for elderly smokers compared with estimates obtained from younger populations. However, the age differences in the number smoked may result from a number of factors. First, mortal-

ity among heavy smokers tends to be relatively high, and those who smoke less are more likely to survive to old age. Second, elderly smokers may cut down on consumption rates, since the cost of cigarettes would represent a larger proportion of disposable income than for younger age groups. Third, elderly smokers may more likely experience symptoms of the negative effects of smoking on health, and greater sensitivity to the consequences of smoking may motivate older individuals to lower their consumption levels.

Arguments for the expected benefits of smoking from the perspective of the individual have typically included quality-of-life outcomes, such as enhanced relaxation; reduced anxiety, boredom and depression; maintenance of autonomy; and improved concentration.39-42 However, the associations of smoking with such quality-of-life outcomes among older populations have not been investigated quantitatively. Results from this study indicate that elderly male smokers had a higher risk for high life stress, and that elderly female smokers had a higher risk for unhappiness and dissatisfaction with social relationships than those who had never smoked. Stephens<sup>24</sup> found similar evidence of a possible association between a number of health practices (including not smoking) and emotional health among elderly respondents to the 1978-1979 Canada Health Survey. Despite the limitations of the cross-sectional GSS and NADS data, the results shown in Tables 5 and 6 contradict the notion that smoking has positive effects on quality of life among older persons. Instead, the results suggest two possible patterns: (1) smoking leads to negative quality-of-life outcomes for the elderly; or (2) elderly individuals who have a low quality of life are more likely to continue to smoke. In both cases one might argue that encouraging cessation among elderly smokers would be useful.

Although the ORs for negative health outcomes among elderly smokers are less pronounced than those reported for younger populations, 6,10,14,21,22,27,43 smoking among elderly respondents in this study was associated with poor self-rated health and respiratory problems. Our evidence implies that elderly smokers may be more likely to experience trouble walking and climbing stairs and to use analgesic, central nervous system, and gastrointestinal medications. The increased risk among female smokers for the use of central nervous system medications (e.g., antidepressants and tranquilizers) and gastrointestinal medications is not surprising, since others have found smoking to be positively associated with depressive symptoms<sup>23</sup> and peptic ulcer disease.1 The lack of observed associations of smoking with other indicators of physical health (e.g., heart problems, hypertension, and some disability measures) is unexpected and may be the result of methodological limitations of the survey data. For example, differential attrition rates among smokers and nonsmokers (related to the higher death rate among smokers) may mask the association of smoking with morbidity and disability in both cross-sectional and longitudinal research. 15 Consequently, the magnitude of the risk incurred by elderly smokers in terms of morbidity and disability may be underestimated, particularly in cross-sectional studies.

The higher risk of respiratory problems and the related impairments in certain activities of daily living have important quality-of-life implications for older individuals. For example, a persistent cough or shortness of breath may limit an elderly person's ability to participate in certain social activities. However, although the logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex,

income, marital status, and education, other possible confounding variables that were not controlled for in the analysis (e.g., presence of arthritis or some other disabling condition) may influence mobility. Nonetheless, since respiratory symptoms may be reversed after stopping smoking, even among the elderly,<sup>44</sup> smoking cessation interventions among older individuals may be appropriate.

The next step for research in this area should involve the use of longitudinal data to examine the effects of smoking cessation on various quality-of-life outcomes. Future efforts to clarify further the many health consequences of continued smoking and the benefits of cessation in old age should attempt to examine both physical and psychological outcomes, as well as possible cumulative effects of smoking on other lifestyle risks (e.g., alcohol use) among the elderly. Moreover, such investigations should consider the effects of gender differences in lifetime exposure to smoking on risk estimates for quality of life among the elderly.
