## Introduction

Over the last 20 years low back pain has caused one of the largest absolute increases in the global health burden [\[1](#page-5-0)]. Improved management options are necessary to curb this upward trajectory. Recently some researchers have suggested that managing sleep may be a promising intervention target for people with low back pain but the evidence is inconsistent [\[2,](#page-6-0) [3\]](#page-6-1). For example, a randomised controlled trial that investigated the effect of hypnotic medicines for treating insomnia reported improvements in sleep and a clinically meaningful decrease in low back pain intensity [\[4](#page-6-2)]. However, a systematic review of interventions to improve sleep quality in people with low back pain that included hypnotic medications and cognitive behavioural therapy, found no clear benefit on pain intensity [\[5](#page-6-3)].

The relationship between sleep quality and pain may be bidirectional [\[6](#page-6-4), [7](#page-6-5)], meaning that high pain intensity is associated with poor sleep quality, and poor sleep quality is associated with high pain intensity. For people with low back pain, those who report high pain intensity are almost twice as likely to report poorer sleep quality than those with lower pain intensity, and self-report sleep quality is strongly related to subsequent low back pain intensity [8–[11](#page-6-6)]. Understanding the potential of managing sleep quality to produce clinically meaningful reductions in pain intensity requires evidence that this relationship is causal.

Accurate and reliable measures of sleep quality are required to examine the relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity [[12](#page-6-7)]. Most published research that has investigated the relationship between sleep quality and low back pain has used self-report measures [\[10](#page-6-8)]. Fewer studies have been published that have used objective measures of sleep quality such as those provided by polysomnography (PSG) [\[13](#page-6-9), [14](#page-6-10)]. PSG records brain waves, blood oxygen levels, breathing and heart rate as well as eye and leg movements during sleep [[15](#page-6-11)]. PSG analysis classifies sleep into stages that describe light sleep and deep sleep, deep sleep includes slow wave sleep (SWS) and rapid eye movement (REM). Insufficient slow-wave sleep (SWS) is thought to have a key role in maintaining or increasing pain intensity [\[13,](#page-6-9) [16,](#page-6-12) [17\]](#page-6-13), but whether this marker of sleep quality is important for people with low back pain is unclear [[18,](#page-6-14) [19](#page-6-15)]. To date the relationship between subjective sleep reports and PSG appears inconsistent for people with low back pain [[20\]](#page-6-16), for example sleep onset latency (SOL) and number of awakenings measured by sleep diaries were poorly correlated with PSG results [\[21\]](#page-6-17). Subjective and objective assessment tools measure different aspects of an individual's sleep experience [\[22\]](#page-6-18), and together could provide a comprehensive understanding of sleep quality. A complete assessment of the relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity should include both tools [\[10](#page-6-8)].

To date, effective treatments are limited because of insufficient evidence on underlying mechanisms [[23](#page-6-19)]. Until recently assumptions or implicit theories have informed treatment targets for low back pain rather than empirical evidence for well-defined causal mechanisms [\[24\]](#page-6-20). This study aimed to investigate the relationship between sleep quality and next-day pain intensity for people with low back pain, and whether there was any evidence that this relationship was causal. Sleep quality was measured using subjective (self-report) and objective (PSG) measures.

# Methods

We conducted an analysis of an observational study that investigated sleep quality in people with low back pain [[15](#page-6-11)]. The methods and results for the observational study have been described elsewhere. In brief, the aim was to assess the criterion validity of two different accelerometers, an Actiwatch and an Armband by measuring sleep parameters in a sample of patients with low back pain and comparing the instruments' recordings of sleep/wake to those of the PSG [\[15\]](#page-6-11). People with non-specific low back pain were recruited to spend a night in a sleep laboratory for a sleep assessment, these findings were compared with an assessment of sleep disturbance measured using the portable instruments. The authors concluded that both the Armband and Actiwatch provided valid measures of total sleep time [[15](#page-6-11)]. The University of New South Wales (UNSW) Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) approved (Approval number 09–2009/12,100) the study before data collection, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. We prospectively registered the study protocol for this secondary analysis on the Open Science Framework [\(https://osf.io/5wrqa/](https://osf.io/5wrqa/)) and reported the study following the STROBE recommendations [[25](#page-6-21)]. Deviations to our protocol are listed in [Appendix 1.](#page-7-0)

#### Setting and participants

Study participants were recruited from both primary care and the community in the Sydney metropolitan area. Eligible participants were 18 years of age or older, fluent in English, and reported nonspecific low back pain of any duration. Non-specific low back pain was defined as pain not attributable to any specific disease process (e.g., infection, inflammatory condition, cancer) or structural pathology (e.g., fracture, nerve root compression), reported between the 12th rib and buttock crease with or without leg pain [[26](#page-6-22), [27](#page-6-23)]. Participants who had undergone spinal surgery in the preceding six months, were diagnosed with a sleep disorder for which they were receiving care, were diagnosed with a mental health condition that was uncontrolled, or were rotating night shift workers were excluded.

#### Procedure and measures

Following eligibility screening, participants were scheduled for an overnight sleep study at the Woolcock Institute of Medical Research sleep clinic, Sydney, where participants completed baseline questionnaires. The baseline questionnaires included questions on age, sex, height and weight to calculate body mass index (BMI), caffeine, alcohol and cigarette consumption, exercise participation over the preceding 24 h, currently taking pain medications or sleep medications, history of obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), duration of the current episode of low back pain and pain intensity. Pain intensity was measured on an 11-point numeric rating scale (NRS) [\[28\]](#page-6-24) (range 0–10). Participants also completed the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS21) [[29](#page-6-25)]; (range 0–21 for depression, anxiety, and stress, respectively), and the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) [\[30](#page-6-26)] (range from 0 to 24).

Measures of sleep quality: The exposures representing sleep quality were identified from evidence (self-report sleep quality) [[6](#page-6-4)] and theory (the proportion of time spent in SWS) [[13](#page-6-9), [16](#page-6-12), [17\]](#page-6-13).

Self-report sleep quality was measured using a 10 cm visual analogue scale, taken from the Pittsburgh Sleep Diary [[31](#page-6-27)], where the 0 cm point indicated "very bad sleep quality" and 10 cm indicated "very good sleep quality". Participants were asked to rate their sleep quality upon waking in the morning to assess the previous night's sleep in the sleep clinic.

The proportion of time spent in SWS was measured using PSG following a single night in the sleep clinic. The contemporary standard technique was used for the PSG recording and included measurements obtained from the following: electroencephalogram (EEG) central channel (C3-M2, C4-M1) and occipital channels (O1-M2, O2-M1), bilateral electrooculogram (EOG), chin electromyogram (EMG), bilateral tibialis anterior EMG, Lead II electrocardiogram (ECG), nasal air-flow (pressure derived), fingertip pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), snoring using a PTAF lite pressure transducer sensor and body position using a body position sensor. The analysis was performed using the Sandman system (Tyco Healthcare, Colorado, USA). The output was scored as 30-s epochs using the current American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) criteria [\[32](#page-6-28)]. Each epoch was assigned a stage of sleep or wake based on the EEG, EOG and EMG channels. The stages included; non-rapid-eyemovement (NREM) sleep and rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep. NREM was further classified into three stages, N1, N2 (light sleep), and N3 (deep sleep) referred to as slow-wave sleep (SWS). The proportion of time spent in SWS was calculated as a percentage of the overall total sleep time.

Outcome: Pain intensity was measured using an 11-point NRS, (scale 0 [no pain] to 10 [worst possible pain]). Participants rated their pain intensity in the morning after the night in the sleep clinic as current pain intensity upon waking.

Possible confounders: We drew directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for each exposure to identify potential confounders of the relationship between sleep quality (exposure) and next-day pain intensity (outcome). A simplified example of the DAG is presented in [Figure 1.](#page-2-0) We followed the steps suggested by VanderWeele [[33](#page-6-29)] using the software Dagitty, version 3.0 [\[34](#page-6-30)] to identify confounders of each relationship [\[12\]](#page-6-7). Using expert content knowledge and published literature, we sequentially added variables to the DAG in four steps; Step 1, demographic variables; Step 2, co-morbidities; Step 3, pain specific variables; and Step 4, sleep specific variables. Full details of the steps involved in each DAG are available in the Appendices; [Appendix 2](#page-7-0) DAG to identify possible confounders of the relationship between self-report sleep quality and next-day pain intensity and [Appendix 3](#page-7-0) DAG to identify possible confounders of the relationship between the proportion of time in SWS and next-day pain intensity. Accounting for the identified set of variables minimises confounding bias between sleep quality and next-day pain intensity [\[12\]](#page-6-7).

#### Data collection

Data were collected over three sequential periods to account for the assumption of temporal precedence: baseline (T0), on arrival at the sleep clinic, overnight (T1) continuously for 4–12 h after arrival at the sleep clinic, next-day (T2) between 12 and 14 h after arrival at the sleep clinic. Potential confounders were measured at T0, sleep quality at T1 and low back pain intensity at T2. The temporal precedence is outlined in [Figure 1.](#page-2-0)

#### Data analysis

We reported means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables, frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. We used the R environment for statistical computing version 3.6.1 to conduct the analyses [[35\]](#page-6-31). We constructed two linear regression models to explore the effect of sleep quality on next-day pain intensity. Self-reported sleep was entered as the independent variable in model 1 and the proportion of time spent in SWS in model 2. Pain intensity was dependent variable for both models. We assessed model assumptions, including linearity and model fit [[36\]](#page-6-32). We reported unstandardized regression coefficients and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) for each model before and after confounder adjustment. Analyses were conducted on complete cases and the probability threshold for statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

The effect of sleep quality on next-day pain intensity is presented in [Table 2.](#page-3-1) Next day pain intensity was lower for people with higher self-report sleep quality (−0.38 [95% CI −0.63 to −0.13]). # Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity. We found evidence that for people with low back pain improved sleep quality, both self-reported and objectively assessed, was associated with reduced next-day pain intensity. However, the effect was not robust to confounder adjustment; when we accounted for potential confounders the size of the effect decreased and the confidence interval crossed zero. The results therefore raise some questions about whether sleep quality causes low back pain intensity and therefore whether interventions that aim to improve sleep quality are likely to produce clinically meaningful reductions in pain intensity, of more than 1 point on a 10-point scale [\[28](#page-6-24)]. Further research is needed to understand the unmeasured common causes of sleep quality and pain intensity, which might lead to identifying new treatment targets for low back pain.

There is inconsistent evidence of benefit of managing sleep quality in people with pain conditions. For example, despite improvements in sleep quality, there was not a corresponding improvement in pain intensity when nonpharmacological agents were used to improve sleep, but there was when medicines were used to improve sleep [\[39](#page-6-35)]. A systematic review of non-pharmacological treatments for insomnia in participants with chronic pain found participants experienced a large improvement in self-report sleep quality but a small reduction in pain intensity [\[39](#page-6-35)]. In contrast, Goforth et al. [[4](#page-6-2)] demonstrated a significant decrease in pain intensity at 1-month follow-up when managing sleep quality with the medicine eszopiclone in patients with chronic low back pain. Similarly, a systematic review of medicines to improve self-report sleep quality reported promising evidence that the hypnotic medicine zolpidem, adjuvant to other analgesics, achieves a minimally clinically important difference in pain intensity postoperatively [\[40\]](#page-6-36). In combination with the available evidence, our results suggest that some other common cause (influenced by medicines but not non-pharmacological treatments) may drive the relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity.

Depression and symptoms of depression are consistently associated with a poor outcome in people with low back pain [\[41](#page-7-1)] may have an role in relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity. Atkinson et al. [\[42](#page-7-2)] measured sleep architecture in a group of patients with chronic low back pain, with (n=3) and without (n=4) depression. They reported that chronic low back pain patients with and without depression spent between 5 and 8% of their sleep time in SWS, compared to healthy adults who spend 20–25% in SWS. Although chronic low back pain was associated with pronounced sleep disturbance, including less time spent in SWS the role of depression in this relationship was not clear.

These findings must be interpreted with caution due to inconsistencies in PSG recordings. A systematic review including twenty-two studies that provided data on sleep disturbance measured using PSG in people with pain conditions found that individual studies objectively measuring sleep vary significantly [[18](#page-6-14)]. Unlike Atkinson et al. [[42\]](#page-7-2), Harman et al. [[43\]](#page-7-3) found limited evidence to support a difference in sleep architecture in participants with low back pain compared with control participants without low back pain. Their prospective observational study found that those with low back pain reported poor sleep quality relative to pain-free controls; however, only small differences in PSG recordings for the relative duration of each sleep stage.

Changes in brain function have also been implicated in maintaining pain [[44](#page-7-4)]. Interruption to SWS is considered one mechanism that influences this relationship [[43](#page-7-3)]. For example, in people with fibromyalgia, those with interrupted SWS displayed poorer physiological recovery and cerebral restoration when performing cognitive tasks than those without disrupted SWS [\[45\]](#page-7-5). The cognitive tasks challenged executive function, attention, memory and problem-solving [[45\]](#page-7-5). However, our results challenge the role of SWS in influencing pain intensity through changes in cognitive function.

The results of this study suggest that sleep quality may not be an appropriate treatment target to improve pain intensity in people with low back pain. These results should be considered in the context of a secondary analysis with a small sample size and a single night of PSG recording. Although people who report poorer sleep quality also report worse pain intensity, the underlying cause is still unclear. A key shared mechanism of low back pain and sleep quality may be dysregulation of thalamic gammaAminobutyric Acid (GABA). GABA, the central nervous system's major inhibitory neurotransmitter, is used to fine-tune neurotransmission. GABAergic neurons are widely distributed in the brain and spinal cord; 30% of the neurons in the central nervous system are GABAergic. It is known that some hypnotic medicines, for example zopiclone, achieve sedative effects by increasing GABA activity at the GABA-A receptor [\[46](#page-7-6)]. Researchers measured GABA concentration in the thalamus in humans with chronic pain [[47](#page-7-7), [48](#page-7-8)] and found that chronic pain was associated with decreased GABA concentration in the thalamus. There was also evidence of reduced grey matter volume in the medial prefrontal cortex of people with low back pain, further evidence of altered pain processing in people with low back pain [\[49](#page-7-9)]. Thalamic GABAergic neurons are also known to initiate sleep in non-REM sleep [\[50](#page-7-10)], providing a potential mechanism for the role of the thalamic GABA dysregulation on poor sleep quality. Planned mediation analysis to understand the role of GABA using magnetic resonance spectroscopy in the relationship between sleep and pain are necessary.

### Strengths and weaknesses

We prospectively registered the study protocol and transparently reported the study following the STROBE recommendations [\[25](#page-6-21)]. We accounted for different aspects of an individual's sleep experience by assessing sleep quality using both subjective and objective tools and collected data over three waves to satisfy temporal precedence. As current data is inconsistent, an optimal measure of sleep should record use both subjective and objective tools. Finally, we used a contemporary theoretical approach to identify confounding variables in the relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity and conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings.

A larger sample may have provided more precise estimates and influence our conclusions. In comparison with other PSG research we recruited a large sample, but the 95% confidence intervals for the effects after confounder adjustment were wide, suggesting uncertainty in the estimates. The exposure SWS was assessed over one night in a laboratory setting which does not exclude the potential for a 'first-night effect'. For example, the participant may have had disturbed sleep due to the unfamiliar environment of the sleep laboratory and the electronic leads attached to the head necessary for making EEG recordings [[13\]](#page-6-9). An alternative approach would be to record sleep quality over two nights, allowing for an "adaptation" night or using portable PSG and setting it up in the participants' homes. Sleep assessment over multiple nights are uncommon due to practical considerations, such as cost. It is not clear whether either option would be sufficient to minimise this first-night effect [[13\]](#page-6-9). In addition, we collected general information on whether or not the participants used pain and sleep medications, we did not explicitly collect data on opioid therapy in our sample, which may have influenced the PSG recordings [\[51](#page-7-11)]. We included a validated measure of self-reported sleep quality but it is a broad measure that lacks nuance and may be difficult to interpret. Other validated self-reported sleep quality measurement tools such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index or the Epworth Sleepiness Scale may have identified specific elements of sleep quality that are important in the relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity. Low back pain intensity rated by the participants included in this analysis was lower than similar populations in primary care and may not represent a typical presentation. It is unclear if the results would change in participants with higher pain intensity.

# Conclusions

The relationship between sleep quality and pain intensity is uncertain. Our results suggest that poor sleep quality does not lead to increased low back pain the following day. These results should be confirmed in larger samples, where PSG is recorded over multiple nights in a home environment using a portable device, and where confounding could be explored in variables that are more sensitively measured.
