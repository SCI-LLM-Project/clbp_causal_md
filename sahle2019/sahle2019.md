## American Journal of Preventive Medicine

## **RESEARCH ARTICLE**

# Association of Psychosocial Factors With Risk of Chronic Diseases: A Nationwide Longitudinal Study

Berhe W. Sahle, MPH, PhD, 1,2 Wen Chen, MSc, PhD, 3,4 Yohannes Adama Melaku, MPH, PhD, 5 Blessing J. Akombi, MPH, PhD, 1,6 Lal B. Rawal, MPH, MIRB, MEd, PhD, 1,7 Andre M.N. Renzaho, MPH, PhD<sup>1,8</sup>

Introduction: This study examines the prospective association between a range of psychosocial factors and common noncommunicable diseases.

Methods: In October 2018, nationally representative data were analyzed from 11,637 adults followed annually between 2003 and 2013. Participants reported on psychosocial factors they experienced in the 12 months preceding each wave. The onset of noncommunicable diseases was defined based on self-reported physician's diagnosis. Generalized estimating equations estimated the ORs and 95% CIs of psychosocial factors on noncommunicable diseases, controlling for other confounders.

**Results:** Social support index was inversely associated with the onset of anxiety or depression in men (OR=0.95, 95% CI=0.93, 0.98) and women (OR=0.96, 95% CI=0.95, 0.98) and with emphysema in women (OR=0.96, 95% CI=0.93, 0.99). Psychological distress was positively associated with the onset of heart diseases (OR=2.38, 95% CI=1.16, 4.89 for men; OR=2.30, 95% CI=1.10, 4.78 for women), emphysema (OR=1.11, 95% CI=1.03, 1.20 for men; OR=1.08, 95% CI=1.04, 1.12 for women), and circulatory diseases (OR=1.04, 95% CI=1.02, 1.08 for women). Financial stress increased the onset of anxiety or depression (OR=1.36, 95% CI=1.26, 1.63 for men; OR=1.30, 95% CI=1.10, 1.52 for women) and type 2 diabetes in women (OR=1.60, 95% CI=1.18, 2.18). Significant associations of parenting stress and the likelihood of the onset of anxiety or depression were only evident in women.

Conclusions: These findings suggest that several adverse psychosocial risk factors are independently associated with the onset of noncommunicable diseases.

## INTRODUCTION

Conventional risk factors for NCDs, including smoking, high blood pressure, and unhealthy diet, may not fully account for the burden of NCDs.<sup>2,3</sup> There is an increasing body of evidence linking psychosocial factors to the onset of cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and mental health.<sup>2–5</sup>

Previous studies on the association between psychosocial factors and NCDs did not include important behavioral and lifestyle factors in their analyses <sup>10,11</sup> and hence were unable to ascertain the independent contribution of psychosocial factors to NCDs. Although the clustering of psychosocial factors is common and may increase the likelihood of NCDs, <sup>5</sup> most of the studies <sup>9,12–14</sup> focused on the link between a single psychosocial factor and NCDs. Additionally, most studies were cross-sectional, <sup>14,15</sup> thus limiting their ability to demonstrate a temporal relationship between psychosocial factors and NCDs.

The authors analyzed data from a large, community-based, nationally representative sample followed annually from 2003 through 2013 to examine the prospective association between psychosocial factors and NCDs. The use of population-based longitudinal studies that capture a wide range of psychosocial, health, and lifestyle factors may provide a comprehensive evidence base for the independent contribution of psychosocial factors to the onset of NCDs.

## **METHODS**

#### Study Sample

This study used data from the Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia (HILDA) survey. The HILDA survey commenced in 2001 with a national probability sample of 13,969 individuals residing in 7,682 private dwellings. All members of those households aged ≥15 years constituted the basis of the panel to be interviewed in subsequent waves. Those living in institutions were excluded from the initial survey, but people who move into institutions in subsequent years remain in the sample. The total number of respondents in each wave was greater than the number of Wave 1 respondents, because nonrespondents in Wave 1 were interviewed successfully in later waves, new individuals added to the panel owing to changes in household composition, and all individuals in sample households who turned age 15 years were included in later waves. The wave-on-wave retention rate was very high, ranging from 86.9 in Wave 2 to 91 in Wave 11<sup>16</sup>

(Appendix Table 1, available online). A further 5,451 individuals (2,153 households) were added to the sample in Wave 11 as part of a general top-up to retain cross-sectional representativeness in the sample. Questions on NCDs appeared in data for Waves 3, 7, 9, and 13, whereas questions on psychosocial factors have been included in all waves since 2001. Details of the study design have been described previously. <sup>17,18</sup> Given the article's focus on NCDs, analysis was restricted to participants aged ≥21 years with non-missing values on psychosocial risk factors.

#### Measures

This study included 7 psychosocial risk factors: social support, psychological distress, stressful life events, parenting stress, financial stress, job quality, and work-family balance. Details of the measurement of psychosocial risk factors are provided hereafter. In HILDA, participants were asked 10 questions about the number of people with whom they share strong affectual attachments and about the perceived adequacy of these relationships. Responses to these questions were recoded, ranging from most lonely (-3) to least lonely (+3). The Social Support Index (SSI), which ranges from -30 to +30, was derived by summing responses to these 10 statements, where higher scores on the SSI suggest lower loneliness and lower SSI scores suggest a higher degree of loneliness. Details of the variables collected across each wave of HILDA are provided in Appendix Table 2, available online. Financial stress was assessed using a battery of 7 questions asking about participants' experience of specific events or financial hardship 12 months before the survey. A count of the number of stressful financial events (0-7) that occurred in the past year was used indicate the extent of financial stresses experienced. Details of the assessment and definition of psychosocial factors, disability, and health-related quality of life are given in Appendix Text 1, available online.

This study included 4 lifestyle factors: physical activity, alcohol consumption, dietary pattern, and smoking (Appendix Text 1, available online). Information on physical activity was collected across all waves by asking the frequency of participation in moderate or intensive physical activity for  $\geq$ 30 minutes in a week. This single item is commonly used in the literature to assess physical activity. Six precoded responses ranged from *not at all* to *every day*, which were categorized into not at all or less than once a week, 1–3 times a week, and 4 or more times a week. <sup>19</sup> Smoking habits were self-reported and categorized as never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker.

The NCDs included in the current study followed the Australian National Public Health Partnership's classification. In Waves 3, 7, 9, and 13, participants were asked the following question: Have you ever been told by a doctor or nurse that you have any of the following long-term health conditions? Please only include those conditions that have lasted or are likely to last for six months or more. Responses included depression/anxiety, T2DM, cancer, heart disease, circulatory diseases, and emphysema.

#### Statistical Analysis

The authors constructed a longitudinal data set through the linking of individual records from Waves 3, 7, 9, and 13. Generalized estimating equations with a logistic link and robust estimator were used to estimate the ORs and 95% CIs of psychosocial factors on onset of NCDs. To assess the role of explanatory variables in

the association between psychosocial factors and NCDs, 4 models were fitted by serially adding the following 4 groups of explanatory variables to the unadjusted associations: (1) sociodemographic (age, marital status, education, Statistics Socio-Economic Indexes), (2) lifestyle (smoking, alcohol consumption, dietary pattern, and physical activity), (3) BMI and high blood pressure (yes versus no), and (4) health-related and co-existing psychosocial factors. Longitudinal weights were applied to account for the loss of sample members from a longitudinal survey. 17 The authors also computed the proportion of confounding by comparing the OR of association before and after adjusting for these covariates. A significant interaction between sex and psychosocial risk factors was found before sex-stratified analysis. Participants who reported NCDs at Wave 3 were excluded from the analyses to minimize the probability of reverse causation. Those who entered the cohort in later waves were also excluded if they had NCDs at the time of entry. Psychosocial factors were checked for time-varying effects on onset of NCDs by including a linear product of each psychosocial factor by survey wave in a model together with the main effects. The analyses were carried out in October 2018. All analyses were conducted using Stata, version 15.0. Ethics approval was not required for the study as it used secondary data from the HILDA survey data set.

## **RESULTS**

Table 1 presents a summary of participant characteristics. The mean age at Waves 3 and 13 was 43.9 (SD=15.6) and 45.4 (SD=16.6) years, respectively, in men, and 44.3 (SD=15.9) and 45.0 (SD=16.5) years in women. Of Wave 3 respondents, 25% were born overseas. The incidence of onset of NCDs at Waves 3, 7, 9, and 13 is presented in Table 2.

Tables 3 and 4 show the association between longitudinal change in psychosocial factors and onset of NCDs. In the model adjusted for sociodemographics (Model 2), SSI was inversely associated with onset of T2DM, anxiety or depression, and emphysema. In the fully adjusted model (Model 5), SSI scores were inversely associated with the onset of anxiety or depression (per 1 SSI score: OR=0.95, 95% CI=0.93, 0.98 for men; OR=0.96, 95% CI=0.95, 0.98 for women) and with emphysema in women (OR=0.96, 95% CI=0.93, 0.99). The inverse associations between SSI score and T2DM in men and women were attenuated following adjustment for sociodemographic variables (Table 4). There were no significant associations between SSI and emphysema, heart diseases, circulatory diseases, or cancer in both sexes (Tables 3 and 4 and Appendix Table 3, available online).

In the fully adjusted model, psychological distress was positively associated with the onset of heart diseases (OR=2.38, 95% CI=1.16, 4.89 for men; OR=2.30, 95% CI=1.10, 4.78 for women), emphysema (OR=1.11, 95% CI=1.03, 1.20 for men; OR=1.08, 95% CI=1.04, 1.12 for women), and circulatory diseases (OR=1.04, 95%

CI=1.02, 1.08 for women) (Table 3 and Appendix Table 3, available online). Individuals who experienced personal stress were twice as likely to develop anxiety or depression compared with those who did not. The elevated risk of anxiety or depression in both sexes and circulatory diseases in women associated with family-related stress were attenuated after adjustment for confounders. Positive life events were inversely associated with circulatory diseases in women (OR=0.48, 95% CI=0.25, 0.90), whereas in men this association was attenuated when the model was adjusted by confounding variables (Table 3).

In the fully adjusted models, significant associations of parenting stress and the likelihood of the onset of anxiety or depression were only evident in women (OR=1.24, 95% CI=1.02, 1.51) (Table 4). Financial stress was positively associated with the onset of anxiety or depression (OR=1.36, 95% CI=1.26, 1.63 for men; OR=1.30, 95% CI=1.10, 1.52 for women) and T2DM (OR=1.60, 95% CI=1.18, 2.18 for women). Work—family balance and job quality were not significantly associated with NCDs in both sexes (Tables 3 and 4).

By contrast, the associations between several psychosocial risk factors and NCDs were explained by confounding variables. The proportion of confounding in the associations between psychosocial factors and onset of NCDs is shown in Appendix Tables 4 and 5, available online. In men and women, the inverse association between social isolation and T2DM, and positive life events and heart diseases, were explained by differences in sociodemographic and lifestyle factors (Table 4). The inverse associations between job quality and anxiety or depression in men, and the excess risk of circulatory disease in women because of family-related stress, were explained by lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption, smoking, dietary pattern, and physical activity (Tables 3 and 4).

## DISCUSSION

This study shows that several adverse psychosocial risk factors have sex-specific independent contribution to the onset of NCDs. This study adds to the few large-scale studies on psychosocial factors for NCDs but is unique in that it examines the longitudinal associations of psychosocial factors with NCDs while considering the effect of several other risk factors potentially linked to NCDs.

The results complement findings from previous studies<sup>9,12,21</sup> that reported a significant inverse association of social support with anxiety or depression in both sexes. The findings of a positive association between psychological distress and heart disease or circulatory diseases in men and women are consistent with previous studies, <sup>28–30</sup> although the patterns of the sex-specific associations differ between studies. Meta-analysis and cohort studies reported that psychological distress can increase the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke,<sup>30</sup> and death from CVD<sup>28,29</sup> and circulatory diseases in men and women.<sup>29</sup> However, some studies have found a positive association only in men. 11,13 A prospective cohort study found that psychological distress was associated with 61% higher risk of coronary heart disease in men, but not in women 11 Similar results were found in the Whitehall II Study, which reported a positive association between psychological distress and incident coronary heart disease in men but not in women.<sup>13</sup> Although the association of psychological distress with CVD has been studied extensively, 31,32 variation in the studies in terms of age at onset of distress, severity and duration of distress, and outcome measurement may contribute to the inconsistencies demonstrated in previous studies. 32,33 Alternatively, sex differences in access and utilization of services for mental health may affect the severity and outcomes of psychological distress.34 This study found consistent association between psychological distress and emphysema in both sexes, independent of smoking and BMI. This has been reported in previous studies, although the underlying mechanisms linking psychological distress and emphysema are unknown. Exclusion of participants who started smoking before the onset of psychological distress from the analysis did not change the results, suggesting that the results were not affected by a potential bidirectional link between smoking and psychological distress.<sup>35</sup>

Previous studies have shown consistently that personal stress is positively associated with anxiety or depression. This study strengthens this evidence by showing that this association is unlikely to be due to reverse causation because those with a history of anxiety or depression at Wave 3 were excluded from the analyses. Evidence suggests that the experience of stressful events increases rumination, a repetitive thought on the cause and consequences of stress without engagement in a coping mechanism, which is a well-established risk factor for depression and anxiety. Furthermore, stressful life events may increase anxiety sensitivity, known to be a robust risk factor for anxiety symptoms. 40

Previous studies have reported mixed results regarding the association between financial stress and heart or circulatory diseases.31,41 The INTERHEART study, which pooled data from 52 countries, showed that severe financial stress was associated with 33% higher odds of myocardial infarction.<sup>31</sup> Another prospective cohort study also reported a significant positive association of financial stress with risk of incident CVD, especially in men.<sup>41</sup> By contrast, a national study of 147,193 patients with mental disorders in the U.S. showed that financial hardship was not associated with mortality from heart disease.<sup>42</sup> In this study, financial stress consistently was associated with anxiety or depression, after adjustment for Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas and health and lifestyle factors. There is substantial evidence supporting the negative impact of financial stress on anxiety or depression. 10,43,44 The Australia's 2007 National Survey reported that the odds of depression were 3.33 (95% CI=1.87, 5.93) in those who experienced multiple financial hardships. 44 Similarly, other population-based studies have demonstrated the excess risk of anxiety or depression associated with financial stress. 10,43 In this study, financial stress was a strong predictor of T2DM in women independent of the effect of potential confounding variables, such as BMI and lifestyle factors. This study complements findings from previous studies, which reported a positive association between parenting stress and anxiety or depression in women. 48,49 The results further strengthen earlier studies in that the associations between parenting stress and anxiety or depression are not fully explained by confounding, including marital status, Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas, job quality, and social support. Compared with men, women spend more time on parenting, have more workload, and have less time for leisure, which could be one of the reasons for the risk of anxiety or depression related to parenting stress in women. 50,51 These findings showed that the associations between several psychosocial factors and NCDs were explained by differences in socioeconomic factors and lifestyles. This might be explained by the fact that poor SES and unhealthy lifestyle risk factors, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and poor dietary pattern tend to aggregate disproportionately among individuals with psychosocial factors, such as social isolation, financial stress, and psychological distress. 52-54 Overall, the results were similar when the models used unweighted data or when imputed values were used for missing covariates in the analyses.

<span id="page-9-8"></span><span id="page-9-2"></span><span id="page-9-1"></span><span id="page-9-0"></span>The strengths of this study include the use of a community-based, nationally representative sample, representation of overseas-born Australians, and the availability of data on a wide range of psychosocial factors. The HILDA survey also uses standardized data collection tools and methods.<sup>16</sup>

### Limitations

<span id="page-9-7"></span><span id="page-9-3"></span>This study has some limitations. Onset of NCDs was defined based on self-reported physician's diagnosis, which could lead to under-reporting of incident NCDs. Residual confounding is also possible owing to unmeasured variables such as environmental risk factors and medication use, or variables that might have been measured without sufficient accuracy. Furthermore, the sample was based on private dwellings, so people who were homeless or institutionalized were not included in the survey.

### <span id="page-9-5"></span><span id="page-9-4"></span>CONCLUSIONS

<span id="page-9-6"></span>The findings suggest that several psychosocial factors have sex-specific associations with NCDs, independent of health and lifestyle factors. SSI, psychological distress, financial stress, personal and parenting stress, and positive life events are significantly associated with the onset of NCDs. The benefits from interventions targeting psychosocial risk factors in terms of reducing the burden of NCDs require further investigation.
