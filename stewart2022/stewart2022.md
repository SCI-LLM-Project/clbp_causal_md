## **Risk Factors for Substance Use across the Lifespan**

Shelby A. Stewart , Amy L. Copeland and Katie E. Cherry

Department of Psychology, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, LA, USA

#### ABSTRACT

Substance use is a perennial public health concern with associated health risks and economic impacts on society. In this article, we present a selective review of the epidemiological and clinical literatures on alcohol and substance use from a lifespan developmental perspective. We compare and contrast risk factors for the initiation of use and the development of a substance use disorder in adolescence, young adulthood, middle-age and later life. During adolescence, alcohol use experimentation is at its peak. Specific risk factors have been identified including trauma and parenting style that can increase the risk of substance use for teenagers. Emerging adults and college students are likely to experiment with other substances in addition to alcohol such as nicotine, marijuana, cocaine, and prescription medication such as Adderall. Middle-age and older adults with alcohol and substance use in their developmental histories may have an undiagnosed alcohol use disorder. Others will develop a late-onset substance use disorder in older age, possibly due to a dearth of social support, coping with bereavement, and medical complication. Based on Social Cognitive Theory, the roles of expectancies and self-efficacy are hypothesized to impact substance use and the risk of substance use disorder across the lifespan. Implications of the present review for future research on age-specific risk factors in alcohol use in relation to underlying developmental processes are considered.

In this paper, we offer a selective review of the epidemiological and clinical literatures organized by chronological age of the participants to reveal different life stages and developmentally relevant patterns of alcohol and substance use across the lifespan. We recognize that genetic vulnerability may likely contribute to the development of a substance use disorder (see McGue et al., 2014; Savage et al., 2017; Sher et al., 2011, for reviews). Nevertheless, behavioral risk factors, or behaviors that increase the likelihood of the development of a substance use disorder (APA, 2013), warrant careful consideration by social scientists and practicing clinicians due to their dynamic nature (they may vary across an individual's lifespan—see Chassin et al., 2010; Schepis et al., 2020; Windle, 2016) and consequences for lifespan development and healthy aging. For instance, substance use greatly impacts healthy aging and functional ability, referring to the ability of an individual to meet his/her basic needs, make decisions, be mobile, build and maintain relationships, and contribute to society (World Health Organization; WHO, 2020). The process of healthy aging is particularly vulnerable to behavioral risk factors that impact functional decline, such as alcohol and substance use. Thus, there is a need to identify specific, age-related risk factors to assist in the prevention of substance use.

The present review is confined to alcohol and substance use as defined in the DSM 5 (APA, 2013). We begin this selective literature review by exploring the health impacts of alcohol and substance use which may threaten trajectories of healthy aging. For convenience in exposition, risk factors are examined at three developmental stages: adolescence, young adulthood, and middle-age to older adulthood. Within a theoretical context, Social Cognitive Theory postulates that learned behaviors such as alcohol and drug use are reinforced by a multitude of factors including other people, the environment, subjective effects of substance use, including experienced consequences of substance use, and an individual's own cognitions and emotions all which result in continued use (Bandura & Walters, 1977). Self-efficacy, a facet of Social Cognitive Theory, refers to an individual's belief that they can control their behavior, emotions, and motivations. The theoretically indicated roles of observational learning, expectancies, and self-efficacy by age group within the present review are considered and we conclude with suggestions for age-sensitive prevention and treatment strategies.

### **Alcohol and substance use: health risks and altered developmental trajectories**

Human development is a continuous process characterized by gains and losses over time (Cuddy et al., 2005; Heckhausen et al., 1989; Montepare et al., 2014; Posthuma & Campion, 2009). Given the growing number of elderly adults today and worldwide, maintaining health and psychosocial well-being across the lifespan emerges as an important challenge in the 21st century. Alcohol and substance use are associated with numerous health risks which impede the process of healthy aging, disrupt trajectories of healthy aging, and increase the likelihood of premature mortality. These health risks may be determined by the type of substance used, duration of use, and the existing health status of the individual (Doshi et al., 2017; Leadbeater et al., 2019; Lovinger, 2011; Peacock et al., 2018; Schimmel & Manini, 2020; Schulte & Hser, 2013). These risks may be proximal or distal, including changes in heart rate, appetite, and blood pressure, as well as the development of heart disease, mental illness, and cancer (for reviews, see Chen & Lin, 2009; Khalsa et al., 2008; Sher et al., 2011; Tsai et al., 2019). Among the major threats to healthy aging are alcohol and substance use disorder and addiction.

#### *Alcohol and substance use disorder*

According to the NSDUH, 19.7 million Americans (aged 12 and older) battled a substance use disorder in 2017. The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) found in 2018 that one out of every 8 adults struggled with both alcohol and drug use disorders concurrently. As a result, more than \$740 billion was in lost workplace productivity, healthcare expenses, and crime-related costs annually due to drug abuse and addiction in the United States.

Most would agree that alcohol use and dependence is a significant problem for society. Ample evidence has shown that behavioral genetics is an important risk factor for the development of a substance use disorder (Azimi & Connolly, 2022; Dick, 2011; Savage et al., 2017). For instance, twin studies have shown that addiction was moderately to highly heritable depending on the substance (Bevilacqua & Goldman, 2009; Ducci & Goldman, 2012; Goldman et al., 2005). Of note, peer influence does account for a significant amount of variance in the development of substance use in adolescence (see Dick et al., 2007; Savage et al., 2018; Walden et al., 2004). More recently, Hatoum et al. (2022) found genetic vulnerability in problematic alcohol use, problematic tobacco use, cannabis use disorder, and opioid use disorder and a genetic factor that accounted for some vulnerability across use disorders. Whether an underlying genetic susceptibility exerts a differential influence on the likelihood of developing a substance use disorder across the lifespan is a potentially important direction for future research (see McGue et al., 2014, for discussion).

#### *Addiction*

Long term use can also lead to addiction as well as changes in brain chemistry which threaten healthy aging. For example, heroin, a synthetic opioid agonist, can mimic neurotransmitters with similar structure in the brain and activate neurons. Tolerance can lead to abnormal pathways in the brain, as heroin does not fully activate the brain in the way a natural neurotransmitter can (National Institute on Drug Abuse; NIDA, 2018; Tomkins & Sellers, 2001). Cocaine and other stimulants can cause the brain to release abnormally large amounts of neurotransmitters, which can disrupt the normal process of neurotransmitter reuptake and thereby disrupt normal neural communication in the brain (Breiter et al., 1997; Daigle et al., 1988). Of note, this surge of neurotransmitters is of greater magnitude than typically released during healthy rewarding activity, such as listening to music or interacting socially with others. The significant surge in neurotransmitter activity and its magnitude are implicated in reinforcing the drug use (NIDA, 2018). In addition to the pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics of substances, alcohol and drug use behaviors are reinforced by environmental and social factors via observational learning as posited in Social Cognitive Theory. Prior to direct pharmacological experience with a substance, children have acquired beliefs or expectancies about the effects of alcohol, and these beliefs appear to play a role in emerging alcohol use (e.g. Pieters et al., 2010).

As substance use progresses, the brain becomes accustomed to these large surges of the neurotransmitter, dopamine, and the individual is compelled to seek out greater amounts of the substance to sustain the same desired effects. This occurs at the expense of healthier behaviors and rewards. This pattern illustrates the development of tolerance, a phenomenon in which larger quantities of a substance is necessary to produce the same effect or 'high'. As a person's tolerance increases, so does their dependence on the substance, a spiraling vulnerability regardless of one's age and stage of development. What does seem to differ across age groups, however, are the risk factors for initiation and maintenance of substance use, as discussed next.

#### **Risk factors in adolescence**

Adolescence is the apex of drug and alcohol experimentation and initiation. Research shows that children as young as nine years old have started experimenting with substances (D'Amico & McCarthy, 2006). Specifically, 7% of sixth graders report use of cigarettes in the past year, 12% of sixth graders report lifetime use of alcohol, and 2% of sixth graders report lifetime marijuana use (D'Amico et al., 2005; Johnston et al., 2005; Simons-Morton et al., 1999). Alcohol and tobacco are the substances of choice for most younger adolescents and middle-school aged children. Inhalants, such as household cleaners, are also a substance of choice for these age groups. Older adolescents and high-school aged youths commonly abuse synthetic marijuana and prescription medication such as Adderall in addition to alcohol and tobacco (NIDA, 2014). Some common risk factors for adolescent substance use include stressful early life experiences and trauma, authoritarian and permissive parenting, and association with drug-using peers (Nash et al., 2005; Shane et al., 2006), as discussed in turn next.

#### *Effects of trauma*

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs, cf Felitti et al., 1998) and harm in childhood have been linked to adverse physiological consequences and poor health outcomes as well as to higher alcohol or drug use (Morey & Segerstrom, 2015). Early childhood trauma can adversely affect a child's development in many ways, for example by increasing cortisol levels (Hoffmann & Jones, 2022), that can subsequently place the child at risk for substance use. Existing research shows an association between alcohol dependence, the development of trauma-related symptoms, and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) (Brady & Back, 2012; Clark et al., 1997; Lipschitz et al., 2000; Wu et al., 2010).

It is also the case, however, that adolescents with a substance use disorder were significantly more likely to experience a traumatic event (De Bellis, 2002; Giaconia et al., 2000). Giaconia et al. (2000) found that this vulnerability may be due to poor decision-making and engagement in risky behavior, such as walking in unsafe neighborhoods or hitchhiking. Further, exposure to traumatic events can compromise an adolescent's psychosocial functioning which can place them at increased risk for developing a substance use disorder based on pathways of use such as self-medication (Basedow et al., 2020; Giaconia et al., 2000; Khantzian, 1997). Certainly, as the number of experienced ACEs increases, so does the risk of developing a substance use disorder (Felitti et al., 1998; Lensch et at., 2021; Lindberg & Zeid, 2017).

In sum, traumatic experiences can increase an adolescent's risk for developing a substance use disorder, and this appears to be the case for female adolescents in particular. Likewise, adolescents with an existing substance use disorder are significantly more likely to experience trauma, and this appears to be the case regardless of sex.

#### *Parenting style*

Parenting style can significantly influence a child's decision to use substances. Research on parenting style, indicates that children raised by parents who ascribe to an authoritarian viewpoint were more likely to engage in alcohol and other substance use (Becoña et al., 2012). The authoritarian parenting style can be described as parenting in which parents are demanding, particularly in the domains of strictness and firmness, but exhibit little responsiveness in terms of warmth, acceptance, and involvement (Calafat et al., 2014). Alternatively, Becoña et al. (2012) found that children of parents with an authoritative parenting style, a style characterized by high demand and high responsiveness, were less likely to use alcohol, cigarettes, and cannabis. Studies have also reported that a neglectful parenting style, characterized by low demand and low responsiveness, is the most likely to be associated with alcohol and substance use among children. Lastly, permissive parenting, a style characterized by the low demand and high responsiveness, was also shown to be related to higher rates of substance use, but not to the extent of neglectful parenting. While alcohol and substance use initiation is problematic in adolescence, the rates of experimentation and use increase exponentially in young adulthood.

#### *Association with drug using peers*

Consistent with the tenets of Social Cognitive Theory, the influence of drug using peers has been shown to be instrumental in the experimentation of substance use in adolescence (Ford, 2008; Jackson, 2013; Martins et al., 2008; Vervaeke et al., 2008). Social Cognitive Theory postulates that behaviors like alcohol and drug use are reinforced by environmental and social factors via observational learning. Prior research indicates that adolescents with substance-using peers are more likely to endorse substance use than same-aged adolescents with peers who do not use substances (Trucco et al., 2011). Further, peer pressure from substance-using peers is associated with earlier alcohol and substance use experimentation (Gallegos et al., 2021; Keyzers et al., 2020). Aversion to substance use, peer disapproval of substance use, and parent disapproval of substance use are associated with lower likelihood of substance use (Norman & Ford, 2015). Alcohol expectancies have been shown to be predictive of future drinking behavior expectancies (Brown et al., 1985; Christiansen et al., 1989). Research has found that the positive expectations related to alcohol use, which have been shown to predict initiation of alcohol use, are developed during the critical period between third and fourth grade and fourth and fifth grade (Copeland et al., 2014). Self-efficacy acts as a protective factor among adolescent substance use (Uzun & Kelleci, 2018). This influence of peers and outcome expectancies shift from the initiation of use to the maintenance of substance use in young adulthood.

#### **Risk factors in young adulthood**

While adolescence is often a time associated with initiation and experimentation of substance use, young adulthood is frequently associated with increased alcohol use (Chassin et al., 2004; Griffin & Botvin, 2010; Quigley & Marlatt, 1996; Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). Because early experimentation may transition into habit, young adulthood is a time in which individuals are at risk for developing a substance use disorder (Jordan & Andersen, 2017; Kirisci et al., 2007; SAMHSA, 2015). During this age, an increase in substance use is associated with an increase in freedom and independence and use typically later declines following increased responsibility (Botvin & Griffin, 2007; Stone et al., 2012).

Binge drinking among young adults is of vital concern. Misperceived peer drinking norms act as a reinforcement for their own drinking behavior. In recent studies, 19.3 million young adults ages 18–25 identified as current alcohol users, of which 44% were identified as binge drinkers within the past month (Kanny et al., 2018; Wechsler et al., 2000). Bose et al. (2017) found that among young adults, only 37.9% reported any perceived harm associated with weekly binge drinking. Of note, this perception was lower than that among both alcohol users aged 12–17 (43.6%) and 26 and older (45.8%). In addition, Bose et al. found that 1 in 10 current young adult alcohol users meet criteria for an alcohol use disorder. Of note, Wellman et al. (2014) found that young adults without a college education were more likely to sustain their heavy drinking habits as they aged. Additional studies have established that rates of tobacco use, cannabis use, and other illicit drug use are higher among those young adults that do not attend college (Blanco et al., 2008; Johnston et al., 2006). However, while rates of sustained drinking may be lower in the non-college population, those who do attend college are more likely to experience negative consequences related to drinking (Patrick et al., 2020).

#### *College students*

Young adults ages 18–25 make up approximately 61.8% of all undergraduate college students (National Center for Education Statistics, 2018). Studies indicate that 85% of college students report drinking alcohol in the last month, and the average college student experienced an average of two binge episodes (5+ standard drinks for men and 4+ for women in one sitting) per week (Demartini & Carey, 2009; Vik et al., 2000). O'Malley and Johnston (2002) estimate that every 2 in 5 students are binge drinkers. Binge drinkers are of special concern in that they are significantly more likely to develop alcohol-related problems, such as legal problems, especially if binging occurs frequently (Wechsler et al., 2000). Binge drinkers can be characterized by goal-directed drinking behavior, specifically in relation to drinking in order to get drunk. To achieve this effect, binge drinkers consume larger amounts of alcohol at a high frequency (Wechsler et al., 1995). Problematic drinking can also lead to physical dependence such as blackouts, increased alcohol tolerance, and withdrawal symptoms (Gilles et al., 2006).

#### *Substance use and health effects*

Problematic alcohol use, specifically binge drinking, has been linked to adverse health effects as well as sleep problems. College students who experience problematic alcohol use report poorer health and health-related problems, like sleep deprivation (Demartini & Carey, 2009; Ham & Hope, 2003). Binge drinking is also associated with severe health risks, for instance, higher risk for motor vehicle accidents, and aggressive behavior—such as engaging in assault or battery, verbal altercation, or physical altercations—in comparison to those who consume alcohol without binging (Wechsler et al., 1995). In addition to alcohol abuse, marijuana and Adderall are also commonly abused. Approximately 25–33% of college students report using marijuana in the past month (Johnston et al., 2006), and 6.4% of college aged students report abusing Adderall within the past year (NSDUH, 2017).

#### *Risk perception*

Misperception of peer alcohol and substance use is a major risk factor for substance use among college students. This is particularly concerning given that prevalence of substance use problems reach an apex in young adulthood (Latvala et al., 2009; Stone et al., 2012). College students often overestimate rates of substance use among their peers (Lewis & Clemens, 2008; Martens et al., 2006; Perkins et al., 1999). Perceived drinking norms are associated with higher rates of alcohol consumption and the experience of alcohol-related problems among young adults, especially young adult males (Stone et al., 2012). This notion supports Social Cognitive Theory in that the perception of others' use plays an important role in the reinforcement of substance use (Mobley & Sandovel, 2008; Sutton, 2001). Beyond risk perception, other aspects of Social Cognitive

Theory, such as outcome expectancies and self-efficacy, also play a role in the initiation and maintenance of substance use among young adults.

For example, outcome expectancies related to substance use, or expectations regarding the positive and negative outcomes of substance use, have been found to predict use, consistent with Social Cognitive Theory. Prior studies have found that negative outcome expectancies are associated with abstinence (Gullo et al., 2017). The inverse is also true in that positive outcome expectancies predict use. In a study with college students and their alcohol and e-cigarette use, Roys et al. (2020) found that greater perceived benefits of e-cigarette use were associated with greater likelihood of trying e-cigarettes and higher rates of drinking. In a related study, college students who used both e-cigarettes and traditional combustible cigarettes were more likely to report greater positive smoking outcome expectancies compared to individuals using either e-cigarettes or combustible cigarettes alone (Peltier et al., 2020).

Further, self-efficacy, another aspect of Social Cognitive Theory, has shown to be predictive of abstinence in that higher self-efficacy of abstinence relates to a decrease in substance use (Cummings et al., 2010; Laudet & Stanick, 2010; Tracy et al., 2016). LaChance et al. (2009) found that greater self-efficacy, following a group session of motivational enhancement, was associated with positive drinking outcomes. Barsell et al. (2018) also found that higher rates of self-efficacy amongst college students were associated with lower rates of substance use.

Other risk factors for alcohol and substance use include college attendance and an independent living situation (e.g. dorms; off-campus apartment). In general, those young adults who move out of their parents' home are at a greater risk for developing a substance use disorder and the risk for problems exponentially increases for those who choose to live in a fraternity or dorm (Park et al., 2009; White et al., 2008). Drinking typically increases in college, however, students who attended college but lived at home with their parents are less likely to engage in risky drinking (White et al., 2006). By moving out of their childhood home, the individuals may change. Individuals who are monitored less frequently have an increased likelihood of engaging in substance use experimentation compared to those who are more closely monitored by their parental figures (Barnes et al., 2006; Bergman et al., 2019; Steinberg et al., 1994). Parental monitoring may prevent delay the initiation of alcohol and substance use while also decreasing other risk-taking behaviors in this age group (Rusby et al., 2018; Ryan et al., 2015). This relationship does not appear to generalize to tobacco use, as those who attended college are significantly less likely to use tobacco products than their non-college attending counterparts (White et al., 2009).

#### *Harm reduction*

Related to risk perception, the harm reduction approach is often used as a method to minimize adverse effects of substance use (Marlatt, 1996). For example, needle exchange programs seek to reduce infection transmission among young adults who use substances intravenously (Vidourek et al., 2019). Prior research has established the efficacy of interventions that focus on increasing risk perception to change behavior (Ferrer & Klein, 2015) and brief interventions like the Brief Alcohol Screening and Intervention for College Students (BASICS), aim to reduce the number of averse experienced consequences related to drinking, and also include a harm reduction approach (Dimeff et al., 1999).

#### **Risk factors in middle-age and older adults**

Life expectancy estimates at birth for the U.S. population in 2020 was 77.3 years (Arias et al., 2021). Among the older adult population, the 'oldest-old' (defined as 85 years of age and older) population is growing rapidly, and this age group has the greatest health care needs. This demographic reality has numerous implications for social scientists and senior service providers alike (Cherry et al., 2008). By the year 2030, all of those in the Baby Boom generation will be 65 years of age and older. Careful consideration of the social, economic, and political ramifications of an aging population, with special attention to the oldest-old, is vitally important, and healthy aging is a critical focus for behavioral research in the social sciences (Cherry et al., 2013, 2016).

In light of these national demographic trends, substance use among older adults and factors that place them at risk have emerged as a crucial topic of investigation (Gfroerer et al., 2003; Han et al., 2009). As one ages, the probability of suffering from an illness or chronic condition that leads to disability, immobility, or chronic pain, increases. Such health effects are likely to compromise one's ability to exert control over everyday activities and future events, which in turn may induce feelings of helplessness and depression, conditions that may prompt substance use (Khantzian, 1997; Newman, 2019). The number of adults over the age of 65 years estimated to need substance use treatment increased to nearly 4.4 million in 2020 from 1.7 million in 2001 (Mattson et al., 2017; Tampi et al., 2019). Barry and Blow (2016) predicted that the Baby Boom cohort rates of alcohol consumption will be higher than that of previous generations.

Those older adults who develop a substance use disorder typically fall into one of two groups who differ based on use pattern: early-onset or late-onset. Two-third of geriatric patients with alcohol use disorder fall into the early-onset group, characterized by initiating substance use in their 20 s or 30 s. Early-onset substance users are at increased risk to have chronic health consequences such as cancer as a result of their substance use (Schulte & Hser, 2013). The late-onset group makes up the remaining third of geriatric substance users. Individuals in the late-onset group began using substances as middle-aged adults in their 40 s or 50 s, typically in response to a stressful life event (Klimstra & Mahgoub, 2010).

#### *Psychosocial and medical comorbidities*

In addition to substance use disorder history, psychosocial problems are risk factors for the development of a substance use disorder among older adults. Psychosocial challenges such as bereavement, social isolation, and lack of social support, especially among females, place older adults at greater risk for substance use (Han et al., 2009; Simoni-Wastila & Yang, 2006). In contrast, social engagement, such as club membership and number of hours spent outside the home, and positive health behaviors such as medication adherence and physical exercise, contribute better health outcomes among older adults (Cherry et al., 2013). Perceived social support was also identified as a protective factor against adverse mental health outcomes among older adults in a longitudinal assessment of psychological well-being following a natural disaster (Cherry et al., 2015). Issues such as delirium, memory loss, cognitive impairment, and suicide are just some of the negative psychological effects of substance use in older adults (Colliver et al., 2006). Heavy alcohol use in older adults has been associated with a higher incidence rate of dementia and increased risk of cognitive impairment (Kuerbis et al., 2014). Older adults are also vulnerable to negative substance withdrawal symptoms due to their physical fragility and prevalence of comorbid mental illness in this population (Klimstra & Mahgoub, 2010).

In addition to psychosocial difficulties, older adults also experience a number of medical comorbidities which may increase the risk of developing a substance use disorder. Older adults metabolize medication differently from younger adults, which can leave them more sensitive to prescription medication and side effects (Cherry & Morton, 1989; Kalapatapu & Sullivan, 2010). The high number of medical comorbidities, such as chronic pain, experienced by older adults can lead to exposure to prescription drugs which may have a high potential for abuse such as stimulants, opioids, and benzodiazepines (Simoni-Wastila & Yang, 2006). Older adults are more likely to be exposed to prescription opioids, and among adults who misuse their prescription opioids, 40–50% obtained the opioid medication from a physician (Han et al., 2017; Schepis et al., 2018). It is estimated that approximately 25% of older adults have access to prescription medications with the potential for abuse (Simoni-Wastila & Yang, 2006).

Of concern, geriatric patients may be more susceptible to developing tolerance and dependence on opioids due to the longer duration of metabolization of the substance in the body (Klimstra & Mahgoub, 2010). Long-term psychoactive medication use can lead to an increase in depressive symptoms (Hogan et al., 2003). However, there is a general pattern in that the prevalence of psychiatric conditions decreases with age (Reynolds et al., 2015). There is some evidence to suggest that older adults have better abstinence treatment outcomes than younger adults (Crome et al., 2014; Satre et al., 2004). Prior studies have also shown that older women have higher abstinence rates post-treatment than their male counterparts (Grella & Lovinger, 2011; Rosen et al., 2008). Differences in treatment outcomes have not been examined in early-onset and late-onset groups, but in general early exposure to illicit substances is related to poorer health outcomes (Lofwall et al., 2005).

Outcome expectancies for substances, a critical aspect of Social Cognitive Theory, differ among older adults as compared to adolescents and young adults. For example, older adults are less likely to perceive their substance use as problematic (Wu & Blazer, 2011). Nicolai et al. (2012) found that the frequency in which alcohol expectancies are endorsed decreases with age. Further, endorsement of positive alcohol expectancies is associated with drinking to cope with negative emotions among older adults (Wilson et al., 2019). In a study examining prescription opioid use, endorsed positive opiate expectancies were associated with increased frequency of substance use and poorer mental health (Ilgen et al., 2011). The effect of self-efficacy for substance use has not been examined in older adults. However, when examined in studies of other health behaviors (e.g. medication adherence; physical exercise), self-efficacy was associated with the desired behavioral change (Ahmad Sharoni et al., 2018; Cloutier et al., 2018; Lachman et al., 2018).

In conclusion, there is little research regarding substance use and risk factors in the geriatric population, as it can be complicated to delineate the symptoms of substance use disorders from normal effects of aging. Consequently, screening efforts to identify alcohol misuse among older adults, as well as interventions to address problematic drinking in older adults remain an important challenge (Christensen et al., 2006). Intervention efforts targeting elderly adults may be thwarted by many obstacles such as lack of insight related to substance use impairment or issues with cognitive impairment which may impeded an individual's ability to monitor their own use (Kuerbis et al., 2014). The growing percentage of persons aging with disabilities underscores the need for disability prevention programs and interventions that promote and foster healthy aging. Isolating the variables that may contribute to successful aging, (e.g. one's genetic constitution, nutrition, physical activity behavior, exercise habits, positive outlook) and examining their role in improving quality of life is an important challenge that may decrease financial burden associated with medical care in late adulthood.

#### **Conclusions and future directions**

In this review, we have suggested substance use may threaten trajectories of healthy aging across the lifespan, a serious public health concern in the United States and worldwide. A strength of this selective review of substance use risk factors organized by chronological age is that it has revealed similarities and differences that inform age-sensitive risk factors. Awareness of underlying developmental processes unique to each of the age and life stage groups reviewed here represents a potentially important new direction for prevention initiatives and treatment programs appropriate for individuals at different stages of life. At each stage the risk of use to abuse increases, therefore, identifying age-specific risk factors is integral (Costello et al., 2007; Fergusson et al., 2008; Palmer et al., 2009). The present review also highlights a potentially promising new direction for interdisciplinary collaborative work among developmental psychologists, health care providers, and social epidemiologists. This review, although limited by a nearly exclusive focus on studies carried out in the United States, also points to the value of interdisciplinary collaboration. Developmentally specific risk factors for the initiation of alcohol and substance use could be studied cross culturally, which would represent an important new direction for future work given the global burden of alcohol use is high in low and middle-income countries (Swahn & Tumwesigye, 2013).

Regarding theoretical implications, Social Cognitive Theory was highlighted here along with its role in the initiation and maintenance of substance use, bringing into focus learned behaviors such as substance use that are reinforced by others and the environment. The role of expectancies and self-efficacy in each life stage lends further credence to the importance of Social Cognitive Theory for understanding substance use and provides a framework from which to address how age and substance use may interact. In adolescence, expectancies play a role in the initiation of substance use, and use can quickly devolve into polysubstance use and maintenance. In young adulthood, expectancies play a part in increasing the likelihood of substance use. While older adults are more resilient to the effects of expectancies, individuals who do endorse expectancies are more likely to engage in substance use to cope with negative emotions. Research has found that other factors, such as voluntary and involuntary retirement, may play a role in alcohol behaviors in older adults (Kuerbis & Sacco, 2012). Drinking to cope is associated with the experience of more alcohol-related problems in older adults (Gilson et al., 2013).

Self-efficacy, a separate but related construct in Social Cognitive Theory, functions as a buffer against substance use among adolescents. In young adults, this effect is mirrored in that higher rates of self-efficacy reflected higher rates of abstinence. In older adults, self-efficacy has not been examined in the context of substance use prevention. However, self-efficacy interventions have shown to be effective in increasing desired behaviors such as medication adherence and physical exercise. Further, research exploring the role of self-efficacy in substance use is needed.

In conclusion, awareness of substance use risk factors at various ages is important, as it can help inform targeted interventions. Adolescents are most sensitive to the effects of parenting style and early childhood trauma, so interventions for substance use that encompass modules to help adolescents process trauma and develop healthy coping skills would be most beneficial. In contrast, older adults may be sensitive due to their lack of social support as well as the effect of medical comorbidities. Therefore, substance use interventions that encourage building a social network for older adults while also providing psychoeducation regarding prescription use would be beneficial. Given the rising financial cost associated with and the increased number of individuals who are predicted to have an alcohol and substance use disorder, understanding the specific developmental risk factors for the initiation of substance use is paramount in solving the mounting addiction crisis.

The scope of this paper did not capture the importance of protective factors, such as parental monitoring, which can protect against risk of engaging in alcohol and substance use. Interested readers are referred to Eiden et al. (2020), Lipari et al. (2017) and Salmon et al. (2022) for more in depth analyses. Future research should further explore age-related protective and vulnerability factors, especially among those in the geriatric age range as the prevalence of substance use disorders continues to rise. In addition, examining the effectiveness of current interventions for SUD treatment specific to age may prove to be of benefit in an effort to promote healthy aging.
