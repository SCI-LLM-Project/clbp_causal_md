# Association between alcohol consumption and sleep traits: observational and mendelian randomization studies in the UK biobank

Previous studies have shown that excessive alcohol consumption is associated with poor sleep. However, the health risks of lightto-moderate alcohol consumption in relation to sleep traits (e.g., insomnia, snoring, sleep duration and chronotype) remain undefined, and their causality is still unclear in the general population. To identify the association between alcohol consumption and multiple sleep traits using an observational and Mendelian randomization (MR) design. Observational analyses and one-sample MR (linear and nonlinear) were performed using clinical and individual-level genetic data from the UK Biobank (UKB). Two-sample MR was assessed using summary data from genome-wide association studies from the UKB and other external consortia. Phenotype analyses were externally validated using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2017–2018). Data analysis was conducted from January 2022 to October 2022. The association between alcohol consumption and six selfreported sleep traits (short sleep duration, long sleep duration, chronotype, snoring, waking up in the morning, and insomnia) were analysed. This study included 383,357 UKB participants (mean [SD] age, 57.0 [8.0] years; 46% male) who consumed a mean (SD) of 9.0 (10.0) standard drinks (one standard drink equivalent to 14 g of alcohol) per week. In the observational analyses, alcohol consumption was significantly associated with all sleep traits. Light-moderate-heavy alcohol consumption was linearly linked to snoring and the evening chronotype but nonlinearly associated with insomnia, sleep duration, and napping. In linear MR analyses, a 1-SD (14 g) increase in genetically predicted alcohol consumption was associated with a 1.14-fold (95% CI, 1.07-1.22) higher risk of snoring (P < 0.001), a 1.28-fold (95% CI, 1.20–1.37) higher risk of evening chronotype (P < 0.001) and a 1.24-fold (95% CI, 1.13–1.36) higher risk of difficulty waking up in the morning (P < 0.001). Nonlinear MR analyses did not reveal significant results after Bonferroni adjustment. The results of the two-sample MR analyses were consistent with those of the one-sample MR analyses, but with a slightly attenuated overall estimate. Our findings suggest that even low levels of alcohol consumption may affect sleep health, particularly by increasing the risk of snoring and evening chronotypes. The negative effects of alcohol consumption on sleep should be made clear to the public in order to promote public health.

#### INTRODUCTION

Sleep plays a crucial role in promoting health, and sleep disturbances can lead to several medical illnesses, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, and mood disorders [1]. Sleep health refers to a multidimensional pattern of sleep-wakefulness [2] that includes sleep duration, timing, and quality. The effects of alcohol consumption on sleep traits, such as sleep duration and insomnia, have been extensively studied [3–7]. Consistent evidence has demonstrated that excessive chronic alcohol

consumption leads to several sleep problems including insomnia [8], obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [9], circadian abnormalities [10–12], short sleep duration [13] and poorer sleep quality [14]. However, the evidence concerning the link between light-to-moderate alcohol consumption and sleep health remains controversial. While some studies have shown that light-to-moderate alcohol consumption is associated with increased subjective sleep efficiency [15, 16], others have found that it is linked to a higher risk of poor sleep quality [17] and the incidence of sleep disorders

[5]. To date, there is a lack of large, comprehensive studies examining the association between light-to-moderate alcohol consumption and sleep traits in the general drinking population.

The causal relationship between alcohol consumption and sleep health remains unclear. However, there is a widespread misconception that light alcohol ameliorates sleep problems. Studies show that 10–42% of veteran women [18] and students [19–21] with poor sleep quality have reported using alcohol as a sleep aid. Causal links between alcohol consumption and sleep health have faced logistical and ethical challenges in randomised clinical trials. Fortunately, Mendelian randomisation (MR) provides a good option to simultaneously assess potential causality between alcohol consumption and sleep health. MR uses genetic variants as instrumental variables and is less likely to be affected by residual confounding effects and reverse causality [22]. Only two studies to date have used the linear MR method to investigate the causal associations between alcohol consumption and insomnia [23] and sleep duration [24]. The causality of a broader range of sleep traits such as chronotype remains unclear. The nonlinear MR (NLMR) [25] method can assess potential causality and the shape of the association. Research have demonstrated that there are nonlinear associations between alcohol consumption and both hypertension and coronary artery disease [26, 27]. Previous observational studies have found that causal associations between alcohol consumption and some sleep traits are likely to be nonlinear [13]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has attempted to clarify the potential nonlinear causal associations between alcohol consumption and multiple sleep traits using MR analysis.

We aimed to evaluate the association between alcohol consumption (especially light to moderate intake) and multiple sleep traits using observational and MR analyses. We first performed a compressed observational analysis of alcohol consumption and six sleep traits: chronotype, short sleep duration, long sleep duration, insomnia, snoring and difficulty waking up in the morning. Furthermore, linear and nonlinear MR methods were used to examine potential causal relationships and shapes, which would be particularly informative for the general public.

#### **METHODS**

#### Study design and participants

The UK Biobank (UKB) cohort recruited >500,000 participants aged 40-69 years from 22 assessment centres across the UK between 2006 and 2010. All participants gave informed consent to participate in the study and were required to complete a series of baseline measurements. All information, such as sleep duration, medical conditions, confounders, and genetic variants, is available from the UKB. To minimise the potential confounding caused by ancestry, our main analysis included only unrelated participants of European ancestry. Participants who refused to report alcohol or sleep traits were excluded. Participants who self-reported current or past addictions at baseline, including addictions to alcohol, miscellaneous, illicit or recreational drugs and any other substance or behaviour, were excluded. After a detailed quality control of the UKB participants and genetic variants, 383,357 participants were included in the analyses. Detailed information on participant selection and quality control is provided (eMethods and eFig. 1 in the Supplement). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomisation (STROBE-MR) reporting guidelines [28, 29] and the related checklist was provided (eTable 1 in the Supplement). UK Biobank received ethical approval from the Northwest Multicenter Research Ethics Committee. The present analyses were conducted under UK Biobank application number 48344.

#### Ascertainment of exposure and outcomes

The main exposure was self-reported weekly alcohol consumption, which was created by calculating the total alcohol content (red wine, white wine/ champagne, beer/cider, spirits, fortified wine, and others) for each self-reported drink and converting to American standard measures (14 g equals 1 measure). Drinking groups were defined as abstainers (0 drinks/week),

light (0-8.4 drinks/week), moderate (8.4–15.4 drinks/week), heavy (15.4-24.5 drinks/week) and abusive (>24.5 drinks/week) drinkers [26]. Other alcohol-related characteristics included alcohol drinking status (never, former, current) and weekly limits (with cut-offs of 14 and 7 drinks/week for men and women, respectively). Outcomes were sleep and circadian traits, including sleep duration, chronotype, snoring, difficulty waking up in the morning, and insomnia. Continuous sleep duration was categorised as short (≤6 h), normal (7–8 h), and long (≥9 h) [30]. All sleep traits are defined as categorical variables. Several factors were selected as potential covariates, including age, sex, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, overall health levels, principal genetic components, and genotyping arrays. A more detailed description of the data collection is provided in the eMethods in the Supplement Appendix. Detailed questions on the UKB questionnaire can be found at the UK Biobank Data Showcase (https://www.ukbjobank.ac.uk/data-showcase/).

#### Genetic instruments for alcohol consumption

Genetically predicted alcohol consumption was instrumented using 90 independent SNPs from the largest published genome-wide association study (GWAS) based on previous studies [31, 32] (eTable 2 in the Supplement). Genetic associations from samples, excluding UKB, were used to avoid bias toward the observational estimates for overlapping samples. The alcohol consumption weighted genetic risk score (GRS) for each participant was calculated with a previously described weighted method: weighted GRS =  $(\beta_1 \times SNP_1 + \beta_2 \times SNP_2 + ... + \beta_n \times SNP_n)$ , where  $\beta_i$ is the beta of the association test for SNP<sub>i</sub> from previous GWAS results. We then assessed the genetic instruments for the three assumptions of MR in the full sample and only in the lifelong abstainers sample: (1) association with exposure (alcohol phenotypes), with an F statistic greater than 10 indicating a strong genetic instrument [33]; (2) no association with confounders; and (3) no direct association with the outcome. We standardised each instrument to a 1-drink (equivalent to 14 g alcohol) per day increase in consumption using empirical UKB estimates to obtain population-specific genetic proxies for habitual alcohol consumption.

#### Statistical analysis

The overall flowchart of data collection and analysis is shown in Fig. 1. For the observational analysis, differences in sample characteristics according to sleep trait status and alcohol consumption were assessed. The independent associations between alcohol consumption and sleep trait status in the general drinking population were analysed using generalised linear models. We created unadjusted and adjusted models with different covariates, including graphic characteristics, BMI, self-reported health and lifestyle characteristics (smoking, vegetable intake, physical activity). Next, we tested the interaction effects of alcohol consumption with sex, age, and other covariates using logistic regression models. After observing a significant interaction (P < 0.05), we examined the association of sleep traits with alcohol consumption separately in four subgroups (menf, women, elderly, and younger), with sixty-five years old used as the cut-off, according to previous research [34]. Odds ratios (ORs) were used to quantify categorical variables among different drinking groups.

For the genetic analysis, we first estimated the genetic correlations between insomnia and alcohol consumption using LDscore regression [35, 36] to calculate the r<sub>g</sub> between alcohol and sleep traits. We then investigated the genetic association between the weighted GRS of self-reported alcohol consumption and the six sleep traits. Logistic regression analyses were performed for dichotomous variables. All regression models for gene scores were adjusted for age at assessment, sex, genotyping arrays, and the top 10 principal components. Genotyping array was represented as a dummy variable to account for the specific genotyping platform used for each participant (Affymetrix UK BiLEVE Axiom array or Affymetrix UK Biobank Axiom array). Traditional one-sample linear MR, two-sample MR, and NLMR analyses were performed to test the potential causal relationship between alcohol consumption and sleep trait status.

#### Linear Mendelian randomization analyses

In the one-sample MR, we employed a two-stage least squares method to evaluate the associations between genetically predicted self-reported alcohol consumption and the six sleep trait outcomes produced by linear MR analyses. First, we regressed the exposures on the polygenic risk scores (PRS) and subsequently, we regressed the outcomes on the fitted values of the exposures obtained from the first-stage regression. To facilitate replication in other datasets, we conducted a two-sample MR with R version 4.0.5 and the TwoSampleMR v0.4.229 package [29, 38]. The GWAS cohorts included in the two-sample MR analysis were summarised (eTable 3 in the Supplement) [31, 39]. For sleep traits, we selected GWAS summary data from the largest current meta-analyses or a single sample repository of association studies among individuals of European descent [39–45]. We obtained GWAS summary data from the largest published meta-analysis of individuals of European descent, which excluded the UK Biobank and 23andMe (https://conservancy.ump.edu/ handle/11299/201564) [31]. In the primary analysis, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used to examine the association between each SNP and outcomes, relative to its association with alcohol consumption. In secondary sensitivity analyses, MR-Egger and weighted medians were employed to evaluate for an average pleiotropic effect and obtain Q statistics for heterogeneity with regards to IVW and MR-Egger. The MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) method was utilised to identify potentially invalid instruments, outlying SNPs, and directional pleiotropy (eMethod 3, eResults). Several MR results plots were created, such as a scatter plot that showed the relationship between SNP effects on exposure and outcomes, a forest plot that calculated estimates using individual SNPs, and a funnel plot that checked for heterogeneity. There was also a plot displaying the results of leave-one-out analyses. Moreover, we conducted bidirectional two-MR analyses with alcohol consumption as the outcome in order to test for reverse causation.

#### **Nonlinear Mendelian randomization analyses**

We used fractional polynomial and piecewise linear methods to test the nonlinear association between alcohol and sleep traits with R Version 4.0.5 and the NLMR package [25], using linear or logistic regression adjusting for genotyping array, sex, age at assessment, and the top 10 principal components. In the piecewise MR analyses, we divided our sample into five strata according to the residual variation in continuous alcohol consumption after regressing on the PRS. We then calculated piecewise linear MR estimates in each stratum, which are referred to as localized average causal effects (LACE) in these strata. In the sensitivity analysis, residual alcohol intake was split into deciles to maximise power for stratified analyses, and any outlying values > Q3 + 1.5\*IQR were removed, in which Q3 was threequarters and the IQR was the interquartile range. The reference value was set at a total of 0 standard drinks per week. Three nonlinear tests have been reported: fractional polynomial nonlinearity, quadratic, and Cochran's Q tests. The code was obtained from https://github.com/jrs95/nlmr/tree/ master/R.

#### Complementary analyses

To validate our findings, we performed an observational analysis using another independent dataset, the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (2017–2018) (eMethods in the Supplement). Given the possible sex differences in drinking patterns, we also conducted stratified analyses in male and female subgroups using both linear and nonlinear MR analyses. To account for multiple testing, any association surpassing a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.008 [.05/6 traits] was considered significant, and P < 0.05 was considered suggestive evidence of associations in the main analyses. #### **RESULTS**

#### Baseline characteristics of paticipants

The baseline characteristics of the 383,357 participants from the UK Biobank are shown in Table 1. The mean (SD) age was 57.0 (8.0) years, 179,734 (46%) were men, and the mean (SD) alcohol consumption was 9.0 (10.0) standard drinks per week; 107,585 participants (28%) had self-reported insomnia, and 135,385 participants (35%) had snoring. For the drinking group, the light, moderate, heavy, and abusive drinking groups comprised 32%, 20%, 12%, and 8%, respectively; 28% of the participants were abstainers. In addition, 139,229 participants (36%) had over weekly limits alcohol intake. Phenotype external validation analysis in NHANES included 3,841 participants and the mean (SD) age was 47.0 (18.0) years. The percentages of participants reporting snoring, insomnia and excessive daytime sleepiness were 24%, 26% and 25%, respectively (eResults, eTable14 in the Supplement).

# Observational associations between six sleep traits and alcohol consumption

To investigate the association between sleep traits (sleep duration, chronotype, snoring, insomnia and getting up in the morning) and alcohol consumption, we performed linear regression with adjusted and unadjusted models (Fig. 2 and eTable 4 in

the Supplement). In the unadjusted model, all alcohol groups presented a higher risk of snoring and evening chronotype than the abstainers, which dose-dependently monotonically increased from the light (OR 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02–1.06; P < 0.001) to abusive groups (OR 1.97; 95% CI, 1.92–2.03; P < 0.001) for snoring, and from the light (OR 1.02; 95% CI, 1.00-1.04; *P* < 0.001) to abusive groups (OR 1.52; 95% CI, 1.48–1.57; *P* < 0.001) for evening chronotype. These differences remained when using the baseline model (adjusted for age and sex) and the fully adjusted model (additionally adjusted for BMI, smoking, physical activity, vegetable intake, and overall health). In the fully adjusted model, participants in the light (OR = 0.93) and abusive (OR = 1.24) drinking groups had an opposite association with insomnia compared with abstainers, indicating a nonlinear association. Alcohol drinking status (previous, current, and abstainer) and high risk over weekly limits (low risk and high risk) were further explored with sleep traits. Similarly, compared with abstainers, low- and high-risk drinkers over weekly limits status had a significantly higher risk of snoring and evening chronotype (OR 1.18-1.32 and 1.09-1.27, respectively) (eTable 4 in the Supplement). Complementary analyses of the NHANES revealed results consistent with those of the UK Biobank (eTable 15 in the Supplement). The results showed a marginally higher risk of snoring for the light (OR 1.38; 95% CI, 1.04–1.81; P = 0.06) to heavy drinking groups (OR 1.7; 95% CI, 1.19-2.44; P = 0.02) compared to abstainers.

To further explore these nonlinear relationships, well-established Jor U-shaped curves were recapitulated for the association between alcohol consumption and sleep traits using a multivariable-adjusted restricted cubic spline model (eFig. 2 in the Supplement). We found evidence of nonlinear associations of alcohol consumption and all sleep traits (*P* for nonlinear trend <0.0001). # Subgroup analyses stratifying sexes and age for observational findings

We further tested the association between sleep traits and alcohol consumption in sex (men, women) and age (older and younger) subgroups (eTable 5 in the Supplement). Sex significantly interacted with alcohol consumption for insomnia, snoring, chronotype, and short or long sleep duration. In both men and women, alcohol consumption was associated with an increased risk of insomnia, snoring, evening chronotype, and long sleep duration after full adjustment. However, compared with men, women had a higher prevalence of insomnia (32% vs. 24% in men) and snoring (26% vs. 46% in men). Age was significantly associated with alcohol consumption for snoring, and short or long sleep duration. However, the elderly group had a higher prevalence of insomnia (31% vs. 27%) and longer sleep duration (11% vs. 7%) than the younger group. Only in the elderly group (>65 years of age), alcohol consumption was found to be associated with an increased risk of long sleep duration after full adjustment.

# Linear Mendelian randomization analyses of alcohol consumption with six sleep traits

The LD score regression analysis showed that alcohol consumption and six sleep traits had a slight genetic correlation ( $r_0 = -0.085$  to 0.070, eTable 13 in the Supplement). The primary

genetic instrument was associated with alcohol intake in the UK Biobank ( $\beta=11.191$  standard drinks per week; P<0.001;  $R^2=0.5496\%$ ), with a corresponding F statistic of 2118. The validation of three instrumental variable assumptions was provided (eTable 8 in the Supplement).

In the one-sample MR analyses, the linear one-sample MR analyses showed significant positive associations between genetically predicted alcohol consumption and the three sleep traits, including evening chronotype, snoring, and difficulty waking up in the morning (Fig. 3, eTable 6 in the Supplement). The ORs per genetically predicted 1-drink alcohol consumption per day were 1.14 (95% CI 1.07–1.22; P=4.08E-05) for snoring, 1.28 (95% CI, 1.20–1.37; P=2.69E-14) for evening chronotype, and 1.24 (95% CI 1.13–1.36; P=6.33E-6) for difficulty waking up in the morning. The power of the one-sample MR analysis was provided (eTable 7 in the Supplement). Sex-stratification MR analysis showed consistent results that alcohol consumption genetically predicted snoring, evening chronotype and difficulty waking up in the morning in both male and female samples (eTable 10 and eFig. 3 in the Supplement).

In the sensitivity analyses, we adjusted for potential confounders (sex, smoking status, and vegetable consumption) that were significantly associated with the PRS or removed 13 SNPs associated with potential confounders using the PhenoScanner tool, and the results were consistent with those of the main analysis (eTable 9 in the Supplement). #### DISCUSSION

This study assessed the association between alcohol consumption and six sleep traits. Observational analyses revealed that alcohol consumption was significantly associated with almost all sleep traits. Light-moderate-heavy alcohol consumption was linearly and dose-dependently linked to snoring and the evening chronotype but nonlinearly associated with insomnia and sleep duration. Linear MR analyses showed that genetically predicted alcohol consumption was positively associated with snoring,

evening chronotype, and difficulty waking up in the morning. Nonlinear MR analyses showed no significant linear causal association after Bonferroni adjustment. Overall, these analyses suggest that even a small amount of alcohol consumption was associated with poor sleep, particularly for increased risks of snoring and evening chronotypes.

The study of the effects of alcohol on sleep was started in the late 1930s by Kleitman [46]. Many observational and experimental studies have confirmed that heavy alcohol consumption is longitudinally associated with sleep abnormalities mediated by brain alterations [12, 47]. Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent in alcohol use disorder [11]. For sleep duration, a cross-sectional investigation showed no strong association between drinking and sleep duration [48, 49], while others found that high alcohol intake was positively [50] or negatively [49] associated with short sleep duration, influenced by race and sex. Britton et al. found that women drinking more than 21 units were less likely to have short sleep durations (<7 h) than non-drinkers (OR 0.39) [49]. Regarding sleep quality, recent observational meta-studies found that alcohol drinkers had a higher risk of OSA [51], apnea-hypopnea index [51], insomnia symptoms [52], and overall sleep disorders [5]. An association between evening chronotype circadian rhythms and alcohol consumption has also been found in adolescents [53], college students [54] and adults [55]. Animal studies indicate that alcohol disrupts circadian timing via its effects on photic and nonphotic phase-resetting [3]. Previously, only two studies have analyzed the causal relationship between alcohol consumption and insomnia using a two-sample MR method [23, 56]. Similar to our study result, Chakravorty et al. [56] found a nominally positive causal effect of AUD on insomnia with 2-sample MR. However, one-sample MR result did not show a causal relation between alcohol consumption and insomnia, so the causal direction between alcohol consumption and insomnia cannot be concluded. In the current study, we employed genetic variants from Liu et al. [31] and one-sample linear MR analyses showed that genetically predicted alcohol consumption was positively associated with snoring (OR = 1.14),

Although heavy alcohol consumption has consistently been found to have strong associations with sleep disorders, it has long been debated whether light alcohol consumption is a risk to one's sleep health or is an "over the counter" sleep aid. The health risks associated with daily low or moderate alcohol consumption continue to be debated [\[34](#page-7-0), [57](#page-8-0)]. Small amounts of alcohol might lower the risk of some health outcomes but increase the risk of others, suggesting that the overall risk depends on residual confounding factors, such as region, age, sex, and lifestyle [[34](#page-7-0)]. Among individuals aged 40 years and older, the burden-weighted relative risk curve was J-shaped for all regions. Recent studies have shown non-linear effects of alcohol consumption on cognition [[58\]](#page-8-0), brain structure [[59](#page-8-0)], cardiovascular diseases [[26](#page-7-0), [60](#page-8-0)] and allcause mortality risk [[57\]](#page-8-0). It has been estimated that approximately 7.57–42% of college students [\[19](#page-7-0)–[21\]](#page-7-0) and 14.3% of veteran women [[18](#page-7-0)] use alcohol to promote sleep, whereas 50% use alcohol to self-medicate sleep disorders [[61](#page-8-0)]. In a survey of people over 60 years of age, 6% of men and 5% of women claimed that drinking more alcohol made it easier for them to fall asleep [[62](#page-8-0)]. One of the key reasons for this is the lack of clear experimental evidence to support light chronic alcohol consumption as having a harmful effect on sleep health, especially in general drinking participants. Our current study showed that compared with nondrinkers, even light drinkers had a significant association with all six sleep traits, with relative risks of snoring of 1.16 and evening chronotype of 1.09. To explain the underlying mechanism, previous experimental studies in animals and humans have confirmed that acute-dose alcohol administration affects adenosine A1 receptors [[63\]](#page-8-0) and the orexinergic system [\[61\]](#page-8-0) and causes changes in objective sleep architecture, such as reductions in sleep stages N1 and N2, disruption of REM sleep [[64\]](#page-8-0), more WASO and a higher mean apnea index [[65](#page-8-0)]. As a common psychoactive substance, even light, chronic alcohol consumption may gradually disrupt sleep homoeostasis. Meanwhile, we applied NLMR and found that light alcohol consumption was associated with minimal insomnia risk, similar to our epidemiological findings, and that the risk of insomnia increased exponentially at higher levels of intake. Another MR study found no evidence that alcohol use per week or alcohol dependence causally affects insomnia [\[23](#page-7-0)]. However, the nonlinear association between alcohol consumption and insomnia may be due to other confounders such as social support and positive mood benefits. Therefore, light drinking is not advocated from the perspective of multiple risk outcomes such as sleep health. The substantial differences in sleep health across light, moderate, and heavy alcohol consumption may have important implications for clinical and public health recommendations regarding habitual alcohol use.

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive large-scale investigation exploring the association and causality between alcohol consumption and multiple sleep traits in the general drinking population. Sleep health is multidimensional [[2\]](#page-7-0) and includes night-time sleep behaviours and chronotypes. Compared with previous studies, we used both observational and MR study designs to assess the potential causal associations between light to moderate alcohol intake and multiple sleep traits in the same population. The MR design could minimise potential biases due to confounding factors and reverse causality in observational studies. In addition, we used the NLMR method to characterise the shape of the causal associations between alcohol consumption and sleep traits. Consistent evidence suggests causal adverse effects of alcohol consumption on snoring, evening chronotypes, and other sleep traits. Therefore, our study extends previous findings on the causal effect of alcohol potential on individual sleep behaviour.

The present study had several limitations. First, self-reported alcohol consumption may have a misclassification bias despite <span id="page-7-0"></span>previous studies having shown high reproducibility [[66\]](#page-8-0). Besides, self-reported alcohol use has been shown to be strongly correlated with levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [\[67\]](#page-8-0). Sleep traits were self-reported rather than objectively measured, such as polysomnography or ICD-9/10 standard diagnoses. However, previous studies have found a moderate correlation between self-reported and objective sleep traits such as sleep duration [[68\]](#page-8-0). In addition, subjective sleep self-reports, such as sleep quality, are important and largely represent subjective perception [\[69\]](#page-8-0). Second, genetic variants were associated with some confounders, such as sex, and might be affected by pleiotropy. However, the results were broadly consistent with those of the main analyses, after adjusting for potential confounders. Despite efforts to minimise the effects of pleiotropy, the associations between alcohol intake and sleep traits may represent a shared genetic basis rather than a direct causal relationship. Third, the UK Biobank sampling participants were aged 40–69 years at baseline; therefore, there are limitations to generalisation in other age groups. We further validated the results of the cross-sectional analysis using the NHNAS database. More research is needed to explore younger individuals. Fourth, alcohol consumption habits, such as alcohol consumption during meals, may interact with consumption levels and have different relationships with sleep health. However, in this study, we focused on the relationship between alcohol consumption and sleep traits.

This study explored the associations between alcohol consumption and multiple sleep traits from different perspectives. Observational analyses revealed that alcohol consumption was significantly associated with almost all comprehensive sleep traits. MR analyses suggest a possible causal relationship between alcohol consumption and snoring, long sleep duration, evening chronotypes, and difficulty waking up in the morning. These findings should be accounted for in clinical and public health recommendations regarding habitual alcohol consumption. Even a small amount of alcohol consumption was associated with poor sleep, and common misconceptions and behaviours regarding the use of alcohol to aid sleep should be addressed to aid public health.
