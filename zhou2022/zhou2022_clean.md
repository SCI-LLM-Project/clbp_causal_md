# Journal of Psychiatric Research

# Insomnia, sleep duration, and risk of anxiety: A two-sample Mendelian randomization study

Feixiang Zhou a,1 , Shaojie Li b,\*\*, Huilan Xu a,\*

#### *Keywords:* Mendelian randomization Single nucleotide polymorphism Causal association Insomnia Sleep duration Anxiety

## Abstract

*Background:* The effect of insomnia and sleep duration on risk of anxiety has been assessed based on traditional epidemiological studies. However, the inconsistent conclusions do not establish causal associations. This study aimed to explore the causal associations of insomnia, short sleep, and long sleep with anxiety.

*Methods:* We used summary statistics from three large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of European ancestry to perform a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study. MR analyses were mainly conducted with the inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) method. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed the weighted-median approach, the MR-Egger method, and the MR-robust adjusted profile score (MR-RAPS) method for sensitivity analyses.

*Results:* There was a statistically significant association of genetically predicted insomnia with anxiety using the IVW method (OR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.23–1.51, *P <* 0.001). Genetically predicted short sleep was potentially associated with anxiety using IVW method (OR = 1.67, 95% CI = 1.08–2.60, *P* = 0.022). However, sensitivity analyses did not find the causal association of short sleep with anxiety (all *P >* 0.053). We did not observe a statistically significant causal association of genetically predicted long sleep with anxiety (OR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.48–1.74, *P* = 0.775).

*Conclusions:* We found strong evidence that insomnia but not short sleep and long sleep has a causal effect on anxiety. The characteristics of insomnia should be incorporated into anxiety prevention and intervention strategies, which have important public health significance.

## **1. Introduction**

Insomnia is one of the most common sleep disorders in the population and clinical practice, which is characterized by difficulty in starting or maintaining sleep and is accompanied by symptoms such as irritability or fatigue during awake hours ([Buysse, 2013\)](#page-5-0). A recent meta-analysis of 13 observational studies showed that the prevalence of insomnia in the general population was 22.0% [95% confidence interval (CI): 17.0–28.0%] [\(Zeng et al., 2020](#page-6-0)). This disease has adverse effects on the individual's physical, psychological, and social functions, resulting in lower quality of life. More and more evidence showed that insomnia was associated with poor health outcomes such as depression,

cardiovascular disease, and mortality ([Lovato and Lack, 2019](#page-5-0)).

Recent studies have focused on the associations between insomnia and mental disorders. Anxiety is a common and serious emotional problem with symptoms of tension, worry, and pessimism. Persistent anxiety may develop into anxiety-related disorder, which seriously damages the quality of life and well-being of individuals and leads to social burdens and economic losses [\(Chisholm et al., 2016\)](#page-5-0). In the clinical setting, insomnia and anxiety tend to be highly concurrent. A multicenter cross-sectional study including 57,298 participants in France and 12 countries showed that the prevalence of insomnia was 25.3% and 7.3% in populations with and without anxiety, respectively ([Aernout et al., 2021](#page-5-0)). These studies are difficult in establishing a causal association because they are limited by potential bias (Reeve et al., 2015).

In other aspects of sleep health, it is obvious that individuals with insomnia often have a short sleep duration (Buysse, 2013). In many previous studies, sleep duration was also related to anxiety in adolescents and adults (Kalmbach et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2020; Roberts and Duong, 2017; Zhou et al., 2020). Adequate sleep is essential for maintaining and promoting individuals' physical and mental health. The National Sleep Foundation in the United States recommended 7-9 h of appropriate sleep for young people and adults (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015). A previous cohort study found that short sleep and long sleep but not insomnia at baseline were associated with an increased risk of anxiety at follow-up (van Mill et al., 2014). However, the evidence for this association was not consistent with the experimental results. A meta-analysis of four experimental studies suggested that 3-6.5 h of sleep restriction for 4-5 consecutive days did not lead to a significant increase in anxiety levels (Pires et al., 2016). These contradictory results might be due to the inconsistent division of sleep duration in observational and experimental studies. As the relevant experimental research is still limited, it is not clear whether there is a causal relationship between sleep duration

In recent years, Mendelian randomization (MR) has been widely used in epidemiological research as a new causal inference method. MR used the genetic variation of exposure factors (such as insomnia and sleep duration) as instrumental variables to predict the causal effect on the outcome. Alleles are randomly assigned at conception and are stable, which precedes the outcome and some confounding factors. Therefore, MR has the power to detect causal associations (Davey Smith and Ebrahim, 2005). Previous studies have used MR to analyze the causal effect of insomnia and sleep duration on many diseases, such as depression and dementia (Cai et al., 2021; Henry et al., 2019). In this study, we used MR analysis to assess the causal associations of insomnia, short sleep, and long sleep with anxiety.

#### 2. Material and methods

### 2.1. Study design

This was a two-sample MR study of European ancestry and we followed the checklist about strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology using MR (STROBE-MR) (Supplementary Table 1) (Skrivankova et al., 2021).

#### 2.2. Data sources

We extracted the information of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from publicly available summary statistics of three genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of European ancestry. Summary statistics for insomnia were obtained from a large-scale GWAS including a sample size of 1,331,010 individuals (Jansen et al., 2019). Self-reported information was used to identify insomnia cases, and the self-reported information was obtained via a touchscreen device in the UK Biobank and an online questionnaire in 23andMe. "Do you have trouble falling asleep at night or do you wake up in the middle of the night?" or "Have you ever been diagnosed with, or treated for, insomnia?" were some of the questions asked. The accuracy for insomnia disorder was high (sensitivity/specificity: UK Biobank = 98%/96%; 23andMe = 84%/80%). In the UK Biobank and 23andMe, the prevalence of insomnia was 28.3% and 30.5%, respectively.

A large-scale GWAS with a sample size of 446,118 individuals of European ancestry from the UK Biobank provided the information of SNPs for sleep duration, including short sleep and long sleep. (Dashti

et al., 2019). Sleep duration was assessed by asking: "how many hours sleep do you get in every 24 h?" The mean value of sleep duration was 7.2 h (1.1 standard deviation) per day. There were 106,192 cases of short sleep (<7 h/days) and 34,184 cases of long sleep ( $\ge9$  h/day). The control group (n = 305,742) consisted of people who slept for 7–8 h every 24 h.

The GWAS from the Danish Lundbeck Foundation Initiative for Integrative Psychiatric Research (iPSYCH) cohort provided summary statistics for anxiety (Meier et al., 2019). The participants of European ancestry were born between 1981 and 2005, and 4,584 cases with anxiety and 19,225 controls with no anxiety, stress-related or mood diagnoses were included in the GWAS. The participants with anxiety were diagnosed by a psychiatrist during routine clinical care according to the *International* Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (10th Revision). There was no sample overlap between the iPSYCH cohort and UK Biobank, which ensured the independence of samples in this two-sample MR study.

#### 2.3. Selection of instrumental variables

The flowchart of the selection of instrumental variables is shown in Fig. 1. The SNPs associated with insomnia, short sleep, and long sleep at the genome-wide significance levels ( $P < 5 \times 10^{-8}$ ) were used as instrumental variables. To find independent SNPs, we used rigorous clumping criteria (LD cut-off  $r^2 < 0.001, 10,000$ -kb). From the combined results of the UK Biobank and 23andMe, 158 independent SNPs were identified as instrumental variables for insomnia (Supplementary Table 2). Among them, rs77641763, rs6973090, and rs429358 were not available in the summary statistics for anxiety. We could find proxy SNPs (rs1047998,  $r^2 = 0.94$ ) of rs6973090. 25 and 7 SNPs were selected as instrumental variables for short sleep and long sleep, respectively. SNPs with A/T or G/C alleles were known as palindromic SNPs (Hemani et al., 2018). Effect allele frequency of palindromic SNPs was not close to 0.5 in the iPSYCH cohort and UK Biobank, which helped us to harmonize the effect direction of palindromic SNPs. The strength of instrumental variables was reflected by the F-statistic, which was calculated from the proportion of variation (R<sup>2</sup>) explained by the instrumental variables (Brion et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2012).

### 2.4. Statistical analysis

The approach of MR analysis was a two-sample MR and the main results were estimated by the inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) method. The standard error of the ratio estimates may be calculated using the delta approach, and the IVW estimate of the causal effect combines the ratio estimates of each variant in a meta-analysis model (Burgess et al., 2013). If all instrumental variables are valid and any horizontal pleiotropy is balanced, the results of the IVW method are robust (Hartwig et al., 2017). The Cochran Q statistic and I-squared ( $I^2$ ) were used to assess the heterogeneity of the IVW estimations. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using the weighted-median approach, the MR-Egger method, the MR-robust adjusted profile score (MR-RAPS) method, and the leave-one-out analysis, and produced funnel plots.

If valid instrumental variables account for at least 50% of the weight variance, the weighted-median method provides a consistent estimate (Bowden et al., 2016). A test for directional pleiotropy is provided by the MR-Egger regression method. The directional pleiotropic effect differs from zero if the intercept from the MR-Egger analysis is not equal to zero (*P* for intercept <0.05). In contrast, if the intercept is zero, the IVW method can provide a consistent estimate of the causal effect(Bowden et al., 2015). The MR-RAPS used robust adjusted profile scores to correct for horizontal pleiotropy in the IVW analyses (Zhao et al., 2019). The plots of leave-one-out analysis were produced to determine if any outlier SNPs had an impact on the model, and the overall MR estimates were compared to the estimates of the most precise individual SNP estimators

R version 4.0.1 was used for all analyses. The "MendelianRandomization (v0.5.1)", "TwoSampleMR (v0.5.6)", "mr.raps (v0.3.1)", and "phenoscanner (v1.0)" R packages were used to conduct MR analysis. We used a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of 0.0167 (0.05/ 3), which was calculated to address the issue of multiple testing. *P <* 0.05 was considered indicative of evidence for a potential association even if it was over the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold.

## **3. Results**

#### *3.1. The causal association of insomnia with anxiety*

Using the IVW method, there was a statistically significant association of genetically predicted insomnia with anxiety (OR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.23–1.51, *P <* 0.001) (Table 1). The weighted-median method (OR = 1.29, 95% CI = 1.11–1.50, *P* = 0.001) and MR-RAPS method (OR = 1.33, 95% CI = 1.20–1.48, *P <* 0.001) also produced consistent effect estimates. The MR-Egger method indicated a positive association (OR = 1.84, 95% CI = 1.25–2.72, *P* = 0.002). ## **4. Discussion**

The neurobiological mechanism of the relationship between sleep and anxiety is relatively complex. Sleep disorders and sleep habits such as insomnia and sleep duration may affect anxiety through different biological pathways, such as circadian rhythm, dopamine, and serotonergic functions [\(Richards et al., 2020\)](#page-6-0). Clarifying the causal relationship between insomnia and sleep duration and anxiety is of great significance for understanding the mechanism of anxiety and developing related interventions. This study is the first to use the MR method to explore the causal associations of insomnia and sleep duration with anxiety. We found that insomnia was associated with an increased risk of anxiety, which confirmed the adverse effect of insomnia on anxiety. There was no evidence of a causal relationship between sleep duration, including short sleep and long sleep, and the risk of anxiety. This study improves the understanding of the causal associations of insomnia and sleep duration with anxiety and provides new evidence for the intervention and treatment of anxiety from the perspective of sleep.

The association between insomnia and anxiety has been examined in many population-based cohort studies, and a meta-analysis of 116,450 participants including 6 cohort studies found that the risk of future anxiety for participants with insomnia was 3.23 times that of participants without insomnia [\(Hertenstein et al., 2019](#page-5-0)). In our study, the results of four MR analysis methods including IVW, weighted-median, MR-Egger, and MR-RAPS showed that insomnia was statistically significantly associated with anxiety. This finding was consistent with previous cohort studies and meta-analyses. In addition, a hypothesis-free MR phenotype association study using different data sets than ours also found a potential causal effect of insomnia on anxiety and estimated an OR of 1.55 (95% CI = 1.30–1.86) using a two-sample MR ([Gibson et al., 2022](#page-5-0)). This suggested that the evidence for a causal association of insomnia with anxiety was robust. One possible explanation

Insomnia can cause a decrease or imbalance in circadian rhythms, which reduces the connectivity between the prefrontal cortex and the amygdala, and leads to emotion regulation disorders ([Gruber and Cassoff, 2014](#page-5-0)). An imaging study has provided that decreased top-down control of the amygdala is neurobiological evidence for the role of sleep abnormalities in anxiety pathogenesis [\(Pace-Schott et al., 2017](#page-5-0)). In addition, a previous meta-analysis provided some evidence that non-pharmacological interventions for insomnia can reduce anxiety symptoms [\(Staines et al.,](#page-6-0)  [2021\)](#page-6-0). After a brief review of MR studies, we found that smoking, neuroticism, and education level were associated with insomnia and anxiety and could be considered as potential confounders in the causal association between insomnia and anxiety [\(Yuan et al., 2020,](#page-6-0) [2021](#page-6-0); [Zhang et al., 2021](#page-6-0)).

Observational studies in adolescents and medical students provide some evidence for a significant association of short sleep with anxiety. A one-year longitudinal study of 3,134 adolescents showed that short sleep at baseline could increase the risk of anxiety twofold at follow-up ([Roberts and Duong, 2017\)](#page-6-0). Moreover, in the longitudinal survey of single-egg twin adolescents in Japan, short sleep could predict the subsequent occurrence of depression and anxiety after controlling for the genetic and common environmental factors of twins [\(Matamura](#page-5-0)  [et al., 2014\)](#page-5-0). These findings are not consistent with our results. It should be mentioned that only the IVW method suggested a potential association between short sleep and anxiety in this study. This may be because the principles of different MR analysis methods are different. Each study method has its advantages for estimating the true value, and various sensitivity analyses are reliable methods for testing the robustness of results [\(Hartwig et al., 2017](#page-5-0)). In addition, MR analysis was susceptible to pleiotropic effects. Four SNPs (rs2863957, rs2014830, rs4585442, and rs2820313) were found to be associated with sleeplessness or insomnia when we excluded pleiotropic SNPs of short sleep using the PhenoScanner. Therefore, we have reason to believe that there is no potential causal relationship between short sleep and anxiety.

Our study also found long sleep was not associated with anxiety. Previous observational studies had inconsistent results on the association between long sleep and anxiety. A study of Dutch adults found that long sleep could predict anxiety symptoms two years later [\(van Mill](#page-6-0)  [et al., 2014](#page-6-0)). However, a 15-month longitudinal study of college students in Bangladesh found that long sleep at baseline was not associated with anxiety at follow-up [\(Hossain et al., 2019\)](#page-5-0). In our study, MR analysis used instrumental variables to estimate the effect of long sleep on anxiety, and the results provided strong evidence denying the causal relationship between long sleep and anxiety. Our findings mean that the association between long sleep and anxiety in the previous cohort study of adults is not causal, which may be caused by interaction effect, reverse causality, or confounding factors.

This is a two-sample MR study with no sample overlap. The sources of summary statistics are from the cohorts of the UK Biobank, 23andMe, and iPSYCH, which avoids the ambiguous definition caused by summarizing multiple research data. We did an effort to exclude the effect of weak instrumental variables on the study results because many SNPs were potentially pleiotropic. Multiple sensitivity analyses validated the robustness of the results. This study still has some limitations. First, further studies are required to estimate a direct causal effect of insomnia on anxiety because this two-sample MR analysis only provides an estimate of the putative causal effect. Second, the quality of MR studies depended on the quality of the selected GWAS. It was unable to address the biases existing in the summary statistics from GWAS, such as misclassification of cases, insufficient adjustment factors, and

<span id="page-5-0"></span>methodological mistakes. Third, we did not explore reverse causality about the causal effect of anxiety on insomnia, short sleep, and long sleep. This was because the number of SNPs currently found to be genome-wide associated with anxiety was insufficient (*P <* 5 × 10<sup>−</sup> <sup>8</sup> ). Forth, this is not a multi-racial MR study. Our conclusions were based on European ancestry and could not be generalizable to other populations. Fifth, sleep duration was divided into short sleep and long sleep. We did not assess the causal effect of linear changes in sleep duration on anxiety. Sixth, nap, sleep apnea, and changes in sleep rhythm would be a meaningful direction for the management of anxiety.

In conclusion, we found strong evidence that insomnia but not short sleep and long sleep has a causal effect on anxiety. In the future, the characteristics of insomnia should be incorporated into anxiety prevention and intervention strategies, which have important public health significance.
